>[!note] Preference Relations
>A **preference relation** $\succsim$ describes how a consumer ranks the bundles in a **commodity set** $X$. The commodity set contains every bundle the consumer could consider. A bundle lists a quantity of each good, such as $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2)$. With two goods the commodity set is usually $X = \mathbb{R}^2_+$, but it can also be a finite list, such as four fruit baskets. The statement $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ reads "$\mathbf{x}$ is at least as good as $\mathbf{y}$". Two more relations are built from $\succsim$:
>$$\mathbf{x} \sim \mathbf{y} \iff \mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y} \text{ and } \mathbf{y} \succsim \mathbf{x}, \qquad \mathbf{x} \succ \mathbf{y} \iff \mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y} \text{ and not } \mathbf{y} \succsim \mathbf{x}.$$
>The first is **indifference** and the second is **strict preference**.
>
>The relation $\succsim$ is **rational** if it is complete and transitive. It is **complete** if for all $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in X$, $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ or $\mathbf{y} \succsim \mathbf{x}$ or both. It is **transitive** if for all $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}, \mathbf{z} \in X$, $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{y} \succsim \mathbf{z}$ imply $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{z}$. A rational relation is **reflexive**, meaning $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{x}$ for every $\mathbf{x}$. For a rational relation, both $\sim$ and $\succ$ are transitive.
>
>The relation is **continuous** if for every $\mathbf{y} \in X$ the two sets
>$$\{\mathbf{x} \in X : \mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}\} \quad \text{and} \quad \{\mathbf{x} \in X : \mathbf{y} \succsim \mathbf{x}\}$$
>are closed. The first set is the **upper contour set** of $\mathbf{y}$, the bundles at least as good as $\mathbf{y}$. The second is the **lower contour set**, the bundles no better than $\mathbf{y}$. A set is closed if it contains the limit of every convergent sequence of its points. In the plane, a closed set contains its boundary.
>
>A rational and continuous preference relation can be represented by a continuous [[6.1.3 Utility Functions|utility function]]. Two standard examples fail the axioms. Preferences with barely noticeable differences can fail transitivity. The **lexicographic order** ranks bundles by good 1 and uses good 2 only to break ties. It is rational but not continuous. [[6.1.2 Monotone and Convex Preferences]] adds assumptions about the shape of preferences.

# Intuition

Economists build consumer theory on rankings because a consumer can report them. A shopper can say whether she likes 2 apples and 1 orange at least as much as 1 apple and 3 oranges. She cannot report a meaningful count of "happiness units" for each basket. The preference relation is the list of her answers to such pairwise questions, and nothing more.

Economists impose axioms on these answers so that they can be used to predict choices. Completeness means the consumer can compare any two bundles, so no choice is left undecided. Transitivity means her answers never run in a circle, with $A$ above $B$, $B$ above $C$, and $C$ above $A$. Under continuity there are no sudden reversals. A sequence of bundles, each at least as good as $\mathbf{y}$, cannot converge to a bundle that is worse than $\mathbf{y}$.

The diagram shows the two contour sets of a bundle $\mathbf{y}$ for a consumer who likes both goods. The grey curve holds the bundles indifferent to $\mathbf{y}$. The upper contour set is the hatched region together with the curve. The lower contour set is the region below the curve, again with the curve. Each set contains the curve, which is the boundary between the two sets. Neither set leaves out a boundary point, so both are closed.

# Properties

#### *(Testing the Axioms)* One counterexample disproves an axiom, while a proof that an axiom holds must cover every bundle.

To show that completeness fails, exhibit two bundles that the relation leaves unranked. To show that transitivity fails, exhibit three bundles with $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{y} \succsim \mathbf{z}$ but not $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{z}$. To prove that an axiom holds, reduce it to the order $\ge$ on the real numbers. For example, the relation "$\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ if and only if $x_1 + x_2 \ge y_1 + y_2$" is complete because any two real numbers are comparable. It is transitive because $a \ge b$ and $b \ge c$ imply $a \ge c$. On a finite commodity set, check every pair of options for completeness and every chain of two comparisons for transitivity.

#### *(One Axiom Each)* Reflexivity follows from completeness alone, and the transitivity of $\sim$ and $\succ$ follows from the transitivity of $\succsim$ alone.

So a relation that fails one axiom can still keep some of these facts. A transitive but incomplete relation still has transitive $\sim$ and $\succ$. A complete but intransitive relation is still reflexive. The proofs below apply the definitions of $\sim$ and $\succ$ and then the relevant axiom.

>[!example]- Proof that $\succsim$ is reflexive and that $\sim$ and $\succ$ are transitive
>Reflexivity. Apply completeness with $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}$. It says $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{x}$ or $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{x}$, so $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{x}$.
>
>Transitivity of $\sim$. Suppose $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{y} \sim \mathbf{z}$. From $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{y} \succsim \mathbf{z}$, transitivity gives $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{z}$. From $\mathbf{z} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{y} \succsim \mathbf{x}$, it gives $\mathbf{z} \succsim \mathbf{x}$. Hence $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathbf{z}$.
>
>Transitivity of $\succ$. Suppose $\mathbf{x} \succ \mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{y} \succ \mathbf{z}$. Transitivity of $\succsim$ gives $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{z}$. Suppose, for contradiction, that $\mathbf{z} \succsim \mathbf{x}$. Together with $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$, transitivity gives $\mathbf{z} \succsim \mathbf{y}$. This contradicts $\mathbf{y} \succ \mathbf{z}$, which says that $\mathbf{z} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ fails. Hence $\mathbf{z} \succsim \mathbf{x}$ fails too, and $\mathbf{x} \succ \mathbf{z}$.

#### *(Barely Noticeable Differences)* Differences too small to notice can add up to a difference the consumer does notice, and then transitivity fails.

A coffee drinker cannot taste one extra grain of sugar. Let $c_n$ be a cup with $n$ grains. She is indifferent between $c_n$ and $c_{n+1}$ for every $n$. She clearly prefers the sweet cup $c_{5000}$ to the unsweetened cup $c_0$. If her preferences were transitive, the chain $c_0 \succsim c_1 \succsim \dots \succsim c_{5000}$ would give $c_0 \succsim c_{5000}$. That contradicts $c_{5000} \succ c_0$. Each single comparison is reasonable, and only the long chain produces the contradiction. A formula for such preferences appears in the Hard example below. No utility function can represent them.

#### *(Lexicographic Order)* Ranking the goods in strict priority gives preferences that are rational but not continuous.

A student compares meal plans by the number of meals $x_1$ first. She looks at the coffee credits $x_2$ only when two plans have the same number of meals. Formally, $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ exactly when $x_1 > y_1$, or when $x_1 = y_1$ and $x_2 \ge y_2$. The name comes from the dictionary, which sorts words by the first letter and uses later letters only to break ties. The relation is complete and transitive. Two bundles are indifferent only if they are equal, so every indifference set is a single point.

Continuity fails. Fix $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, y_2)$ and a bundle $\mathbf{z} = (y_1, z_2)$ with $z_2 < y_2$. The bundles $\mathbf{x}^n = (y_1 + 2/n, z_2)$ all have more of good 1 than $\mathbf{y}$, so $\mathbf{x}^n \succ \mathbf{y}$ for every $n$. The sequence converges to $\mathbf{z}$, and $\mathbf{y} \succ \mathbf{z}$. So the upper contour set of $\mathbf{y}$ does not contain the limit of its own sequence, and it is not closed. In the diagram, the dashed segment is the part of the boundary that the upper contour set leaves out.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Pizza and Soda
>A student ranks bundles $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2)$ of pizza slices $x_1$ and cans of soda $x_2$, with $X = \mathbb{R}^2_+$. Consider three candidate relations.
>- Relation (a): $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ if and only if $x_1 \ge y_1$.
>- Relation (b): $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ if and only if $x_1 \ge y_1$ and $x_2 \ge y_2$.
>- Relation (c): $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ if and only if $4x_1 \ge 4y_1$ and $3x_2 \ge 3y_2$.
>
>>[!example]- Is each relation complete? Is each one transitive?
>>Relation (a) is complete and transitive. Any two real numbers satisfy $x_1 \ge y_1$ or $y_1 \ge x_1$, so every pair of bundles is ranked. If $x_1 \ge y_1$ and $y_1 \ge z_1$, then $x_1 \ge z_1$. The student ignores soda, so $(2, 0) \sim (2, 5)$. This behavior is odd but still rational.
>>
>>Relation (b) is transitive but not complete. Transitivity holds good by good, since $x_1 \ge y_1 \ge z_1$ and $x_2 \ge y_2 \ge z_2$. Completeness fails for the bundles $(3, 1)$ and $(1, 3)$. Neither bundle has at least as much of both goods, so the relation ranks neither one above the other.
>>
>>Relation (c) is the same relation as (b). Dividing $4x_1 \ge 4y_1$ by 4 and $3x_2 \ge 3y_2$ by 3 gives back the two conditions of (b). Multiplying both sides of an inequality by a positive number never changes which bundles satisfy it. So (c) is also transitive but not complete.

>[!example] (Medium) Three Apartments
>A student compares three apartments, so $X = \{A, B, C\}$. Her stated relation contains exactly these comparisons: each apartment is at least as good as itself, $A \succsim B$, and $B \succsim C$.
>
>>[!example]- Is the relation complete? Is it transitive? Which single comparison would make it rational?
>>The relation is not complete. The apartments $A$ and $C$ are never compared, since neither $A \succsim C$ nor $C \succsim A$ is listed.
>>
>>The relation is not transitive either. Transitivity applied to $A \succsim B$ and $B \succsim C$ requires $A \succsim C$, which is missing. A common mistake is to fill in $A \succsim C$ automatically. A relation contains only the comparisons it lists, and transitivity is a condition that those comparisons must satisfy.
>>
>>Adding $A \succsim C$ fixes both problems. Every pair is then compared. The only chain of two different comparisons is $A \succsim B$ followed by $B \succsim C$, and its conclusion $A \succsim C$ is now listed. Adding $C \succsim A$ instead would make the relation complete, but the chain $A \succsim B \succsim C$ would still require the missing $A \succsim C$. Completeness forces a comparison between $A$ and $C$, so $A \succsim C$ is the only single addition that makes the relation rational.

>[!example] (Hard) A Sugar Threshold
>A café customer ranks cups of coffee by their sugar content $s$ in grams, with $s \in [0, 10]$. She likes sweeter coffee, but she cannot taste a difference of 1 gram or less. Her relation is
>$$s \succsim t \iff s \ge t - 1.$$
>So she is indifferent between any two cups whose sugar differs by at most 1 gram.
>
>>[!example]- Is the relation complete, transitive, and continuous? Which of $\sim$ and $\succ$ is transitive?
>>The relation is complete. If $s \succsim t$ fails, then $s < t - 1$. Then $t > s + 1 > s - 1$, so $t \succsim s$ holds.
>>
>>The relation is not transitive. Take the cups $0$, $0.8$, and $1.6$. Then $0 \succsim 0.8$ because $0 \ge -0.2$, and $0.8 \succsim 1.6$ because $0.8 \ge 0.6$. But $0 \succsim 1.6$ would need $0 \ge 0.6$, which is false.
>>
>>The relation is continuous. The upper contour set of $t$ is the set of $s \in [0, 10]$ with $s \ge t - 1$. The lower contour set is the set of $s \in [0, 10]$ with $s \le t + 1$. Both are closed intervals.
>>
>>From the definitions, $s \sim t$ exactly when $|s - t| \le 1$, and $s \succ t$ exactly when $s > t + 1$. Indifference is not transitive, since $0 \sim 0.8$ and $0.8 \sim 1.6$ but $1.6 \succ 0$. Strict preference is transitive, since $s > t + 1$ and $t > w + 1$ give $s > w + 2$, so $s \succ w$. This does not contradict the result that $\sim$ and $\succ$ are transitive for rational preferences. That result assumes a transitive $\succsim$, and this relation is not transitive.
