>[!note] Lookback Options
>A **lookback option** replaces the final price $S_T$ or the strike with the highest or lowest observed price. The contract fixes the dates on which the price is observed, up to the expiry $T$. Let $M_T$ and $m_T$ be the highest and lowest observed prices. The four lookback payoffs are
>$$S_T - m_T, \qquad M_T - S_T, \qquad \max(M_T - K, 0), \qquad \max(K - m_T, 0).$$
>Here $K$ is a fixed strike. Lookbacks come in two kinds, each with a call and a put.
>- The **floating strike** lookback call and put pay the first two. The path sets their strike at expiry.
>- The **fixed strike** lookback call and put pay the last two. They replace $S_T$ in a vanilla payoff with $M_T$ or $m_T$.
>
>The observed dates include $T$, so $m_T \le S_T \le M_T$, and the floating payoffs are never negative.
>
>Like [[2.3.6.2a Barrier Options|barrier]] and [[2.3.6.2b Asian Options|Asian options]], a lookback option is path-dependent, since its payoff uses prices observed before $T$. It builds on the vanilla options of the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]].

# Intuition

Many exposures depend on a path. An investor who fears selling at the wrong moment cares about the highest price of the quarter. A lookback pays the best price with hindsight, so it costs more than the vanilla.

# Properties

#### *(Cost)* A lookback pays at least as much as a matching vanilla option on every path, so it costs more.

The figure marks the running maximum and minimum of one path. The path ends at $S_T = 103.5$ after a high of $122.3$ and a low of $89.2$. The floating strike call pays $14.3$, and the floating strike put pays $18.8$. A vanilla call struck at $S_0 = 100$ pays $3.5$, and the vanilla put pays nothing.

The bounds hold on every path. Since $M_T \ge S_T$, the fixed strike call pays $\max(M_T - K, 0) \ge \max(S_T - K, 0)$. The observed dates include the start, so $m_T \le S_0$, and the floating strike call pays $S_T - m_T \ge \max(S_T - S_0, 0)$. The extra payoff grows with how far the path wanders, which depends on future realized volatility. A dealer who sells lookbacks must estimate that volatility well. Observing the price more often can only raise $M_T$ and lower $m_T$, so the observation schedule also sets the price.

#### *(Uses)* Lookbacks remove the timing risk of a single purchase or sale.

A floating strike call buys at the lowest observed price, and a floating strike put sells at the highest. Lookbacks serve as hedges in FX and commodity markets. They also appear as features in equity notes tied to an index.

#### *(The Range Has Vega)* A floating strike call plus put pays the range $M_T - m_T$, which still depends on volatility.

Adding the two floating strike payoffs cancels the final price:
$$(S_T - m_T) + (M_T - S_T) = M_T - m_T.$$
The average price call minus put of [[2.3.6.2b Asian Options|Asian parity]] pays $A_T - K$, where $A_T$ is the average price. Shares and cash replicate that payoff. The range has no such replication. It depends on the extremes of the path, and their spread grows with $\sigma$. So the pair has positive vega. On the three-step tree of the hard example below, the range is worth $\$8.62$ at $\sigma = 30\%$ and $\$11.48$ at $\sigma = 40\%$. An at-the-money straddle on the same tree gains less, from $\$6.46$ to $\$8.61$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Lookback Payoffs on One Path
>A six-week lookback contract on a stock observes the close today and at the end of each week. The closes are $\$80$ today, then $\$76$, $\$83$, $\$79$, $\$88$, $\$91$ and $\$85$ at expiry. The fixed strike call and put use the strike $K = \$80$. Only payoffs are needed, so no discounting is used.
>
>>[!example]- Find the payoffs of the four lookbacks and of the vanilla call and put with strike $\$80$. Then repeat with a schedule that observes only every other week.
>>With weekly observation, $M_T = 91$, $m_T = 76$ and $S_T = 85$.
>>- The floating strike call pays $S_T - m_T = 85 - 76 = \$9$.
>>- The floating strike put pays $M_T - S_T = 91 - 85 = \$6$.
>>- The fixed strike call pays $\max(91 - 80, 0) = \$11$.
>>- The fixed strike put pays $\max(80 - 76, 0) = \$4$.
>>
>>The vanilla call pays $\max(85 - 80, 0) = \$5$, and the vanilla put pays nothing. Each lookback pays at least as much as the matching vanilla. The floating pair together pays the range $91 - 76 = \$15$.
>>
>>Observation every other week sees only $\$80$, $\$83$, $\$88$ and $\$85$. Now $M_T = 88$ and $m_T = 80$. The floating call pays $\$5$, the floating put $\$3$, and the fixed call $\$8$. The fixed put pays nothing. The low of $\$76$ and the high of $\$91$ fell in weeks the schedule skips. So each lookback pays less on this path than with weekly observation.

>The lecture does not link fixed and floating strike lookbacks. This example derives the link from the payoffs.

>[!example] (Medium) A Fixed Strike Call From a Floating Strike Put
>A stock trades at $\$120$ and pays no dividends. A six-month floating strike lookback put observes the close on every trading day, including today and expiry. It trades at $\$18.85$, a price that matches a volatility of $30\%$. The risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A client asks for fixed strike lookback calls on the same schedule, with strikes $\$110$, $\$120$ and $\$130$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the prices of the fixed strike calls with strikes $\$110$ and $\$120$ from the put. Explain why the same method gives only a bound for the strike of $\$130$.
>>The schedule includes today, so $M_T \ge S_0 = 120$. For a strike $K \le 120$, the fixed strike call always pays $M_T - K$. Split this payoff at the final price:
>>$$M_T - K = (M_T - S_T) + (S_T - K).$$
>>The first part is the floating strike put. The second part pays $S_T - K$, which one share bought today and a loan of $Ke^{-rT}$ replicate. So the fixed strike call $C_{\text{fix}}$ and the floating strike put $P_{\text{float}}$ satisfy
>>$$C_{\text{fix}}(K) = P_{\text{float}} + S_0 - Ke^{-rT}, \qquad K \le S_0.$$
>>Here $e^{-0.04 \times 0.5} = 0.98020$. The two calls are worth
>>$$C_{\text{fix}}(110) = 18.85 + 120 - 107.82 = \$31.03, \qquad C_{\text{fix}}(120) = 18.85 + 120 - 117.62 = \$21.23.$$
>>Vanilla calls at $30\%$ volatility cost $\$16.90$ and $\$11.27$. The lookback calls cost about $1.8$ and $1.9$ times as much.
>>
>>For $K = 130$, $M_T$ can stay below $130$. The call then pays $0$, while $M_T - 130$ is negative. So $\max(M_T - 130, 0) \ge M_T - 130$, and the same formula gives only a lower bound:
>>$$C_{\text{fix}}(130) \ge 18.85 + 120 - 127.43 = \$11.42.$$
>>A simulation with daily observation at $30\%$ volatility values this call at $\$13.34$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture defines lookback options but does not price them. This example prices them on a binomial tree by listing every path.

>[!example] (Hard) Lookbacks on a Three-Step Tree
>A stock trades at $\$50$ and pays no dividends. Its volatility is $30\%$ per year, and the risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A three-month floating strike lookback call and put observe the price today and at the end of each month. Use a three-step [[2.3.3c Binomial Tree|binomial tree]] with $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ and $d = 1/u$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the values of the lookback call, the lookback put and the range $M_T - m_T$. Compare them with at-the-money vanilla options on the same tree, and repeat the range and the straddle with $\sigma = 40\%$.
>>Here $\Delta t = 1/12$, so $u = e^{0.3\sqrt{1/12}} = 1.0905$ and $d = 0.9170$. The risk-neutral probability of an up move is
>>$$p = \frac{e^{0.04/12} - 0.9170}{1.0905 - 0.9170} = 0.4976.$$
>>The payoffs depend on the order of the moves, so we list all eight paths. A path with $k$ up moves has probability $p^k(1 - p)^{3 - k}$.
>>
>>| Path | Probability | $M_T$ | $m_T$ | $S_T$ | Call $S_T - m_T$ | Put $M_T - S_T$ |
>>|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
>>| uuu | $0.1232$ | $64.83$ | $50.00$ | $64.83$ | $14.83$ | $0.00$ |
>>| uud | $0.1244$ | $59.46$ | $50.00$ | $54.52$ | $4.52$ | $4.93$ |
>>| udu | $0.1244$ | $54.52$ | $50.00$ | $54.52$ | $4.52$ | $0.00$ |
>>| udd | $0.1256$ | $54.52$ | $45.85$ | $45.85$ | $0.00$ | $8.67$ |
>>| duu | $0.1244$ | $54.52$ | $45.85$ | $54.52$ | $8.67$ | $0.00$ |
>>| dud | $0.1256$ | $50.00$ | $45.85$ | $45.85$ | $0.00$ | $4.15$ |
>>| ddu | $0.1256$ | $50.00$ | $42.05$ | $45.85$ | $3.80$ | $4.15$ |
>>| ddd | $0.1268$ | $50.00$ | $38.56$ | $38.56$ | $0.00$ | $11.44$ |
>>
>>The expected payoffs are $4.5097$ for the call and $4.1951$ for the put. Discounting by $e^{-0.04 \times 0.25} = 0.99005$ gives a lookback call worth $\$4.46$ and a lookback put worth $\$4.15$. The vanilla call and put struck at $\$50$ are worth $\$3.48$ and $\$2.98$ on the same tree. The lookbacks cost $28\%$ and $39\%$ more.
>>
>>The range is worth $4.4648 + 4.1533 = \$8.62$, against $\$6.46$ for the straddle. With $\sigma = 40\%$, the tree has $u = 1.1224$, $d = 0.8909$ and $p = 0.4856$. The range is then worth $\$11.48$, a rise of $\$2.86$. The straddle is worth $\$8.61$, a rise of $\$2.14$. The range gains more than the straddle from the same rise in volatility. The floating strike pair has a large positive vega.
