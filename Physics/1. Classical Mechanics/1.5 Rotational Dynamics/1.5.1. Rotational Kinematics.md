>[!note] Rotational Motion
>To describe an object's rotational motion, we track its angular displacement $\Delta\theta$, where
>$$\Delta \theta = \theta - \theta_{0},$$
>such that if $\Delta \theta > 0$, the rotation is counter-clockwise, and vice-versa.
>
>Then, **angular velocity** can be defined as the overall rate of change of the object's rotation, where
>$$\omega_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta \theta}{\Delta t}, \enspace \text{and} \enspace \omega = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{\Delta \theta}{\Delta t}=\frac{d \theta}{dt}.$$
>
>Just like in translational kinematics, **angular acceleration** denotes the rate of change in angular velocity over time, such that 
>$$\alpha_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\omega - \omega_{0}}{t - t_{0}} = \frac{\Delta \omega}{\Delta t}, \enspace \text{and} \enspace \alpha = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta \omega}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\omega}{dt}.$$
>
>Similarly, the equations for motion at constant acceleration still hold, such that
>$$
>\begin{align}
>\omega &= \omega_0 + \alpha t && \enspace \text{(Angular Velocity)}, \\
>\theta &= \theta_0+\omega_0t+\frac{1}{2}\alpha t^2 && \enspace \text{(Angular Displacement)}, \\
\omega^2 &= \omega_0^2+2\alpha(\theta-\theta_0) && \enspace \text{(Angular Velocity-Displacement)}.
>\end{align}
>$$
>
>In addition, angular motion can be used to describe an object's translational motion. Let $r$ be the distance of a point from the rotational axis. Then, one can express the distance traveled as $d=r\Delta \theta$. As such, $v=rd\theta=\omega r$, and $a=\alpha r$.
