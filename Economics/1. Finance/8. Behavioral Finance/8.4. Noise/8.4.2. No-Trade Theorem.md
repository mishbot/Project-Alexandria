>[!note] Milgrom-Stokey No-Trade Theorem
>The **no-trade theorem** says that in a fully rational market, **private information by itself does not generate trade**: if everyone knows everyone is rational and trading voluntarily, then an order to buy/sell is itself informative, so the other side updates and becomes unwilling to trade at the quoted price.
>
>The core logic is that **trades reveal information**. If a trader is willing to buy at price $P$, that willingness is evidence they believe the asset is worth more than $P$. Once the counterparty accounts for this inference, the "deal" should disappear unless there is some other motive to trade.
>
>For the theorem to bite, the environment has to be very clean:
> - **Common priors + rational Bayesian updating:** disagreements come from information, not different baseline beliefs.
> - **Common knowledge of rationality and voluntary trade:** everyone expects others to interpret orders strategically.
> - **No non-informational motives:** no liquidity needs, hedging demands, taxes, constraints, rebalancing, etc.
> - **No noise or bounded rationality:** prices and order flow are not contaminated by “irrelevant” shocks.
>
>The practical implication is not that "markets have zero volume," but rather: **persistent volume requires something beyond pure information**. Real markets trade because there are **liquidity shocks, risk-sharing motives, institutional constraints, heterogeneous beliefs, bounded rationality, or noise trading** (i.e., reasons someone might trade even if the other side infers something from the trade).
>
> In behavioral finance, the theorem is used as a discipline device: if a model predicts lots of trading, it must clearly specify **what breaks the no-trade logic** (noise, overconfidence, disagreement, constraints, etc.).

In Baker’s story, traders face uncertainty about others’ information and reliability, so they form **trusted networks** to reduce search and verification costs. Those networks improve information quality _within_ the clique, but they also **segment the market**, so information travels imperfectly across groups and traders can become stuck in locally shared views, sustaining belief dispersion and noise.
