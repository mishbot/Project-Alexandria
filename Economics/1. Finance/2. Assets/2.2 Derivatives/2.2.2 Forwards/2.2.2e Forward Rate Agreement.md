>[!note] Forward Rate Agreement
>A **forward rate agreement (FRA)** is an over-the-counter contract that fixes today the interest rate for a future period $[T_1, T_2]$. It applies a fixed rate $R_K$ to a notional $L$ (the principal used only to compute payments). No principal changes hands. Let $\tau = T_2 - T_1$ be the length of the period, usually three or six months. At $T_1$ the market observes a reference floating rate $R_M$ for the period, such as term SOFR. Rates in an FRA are simple rates over the period.
>
>The fair rate comes from zero-coupon bond prices. Let $P(t, T)$ be the price at time $t$ of a zero-coupon bond that pays $\$1$ at $T$. Investing until $T_2$ must give the same as investing until $T_1$ and rolling over at a rate fixed today,
>$$P(0, T_1)\,P(T_1, T_2) = P(0, T_2).$$
>Here $P(T_1, T_2) = P(0, T_2)/P(0, T_1)$ is the **forward discount factor**, the value at $T_1$ of $\$1$ paid at $T_2$, as fixed today. Let $r_1$ and $r_2$ be the continuously compounded zero rates to $T_1$ and $T_2$. The continuous forward rate $r_{12}$ and the simple **forward rate** $R_F$ for the period are
>$$r_{12} = \frac{r_2T_2 - r_1T_1}{T_2 - T_1}, \qquad R_F = \frac{1}{\tau}\left(\frac{P(0, T_1)}{P(0, T_2)} - 1\right).$$
>Both describe the same growth, since $1 + R_F\tau = e^{r_{12}\tau}$. Traders compare an FRA quote with $R_F$.
>
>The side that pays fixed and receives floating gets the following amount, set at $T_1$ when $R_M$ is observed,
>$$L(R_M - R_K)\tau.$$
>The other side gets the negative of this amount. The contract pays it on one of two dates.
>- **Settlement at $T_2$** pays $L(R_M - R_K)\tau$ at the end of the period, when the interest it measures falls due.
>- **Settlement at $T_1$** pays $L(R_M - R_K)\tau/(1 + R_M\tau)$, the same amount discounted over the period at $R_M$.
>
>Traders name the two sides by the fixed leg.
>- The **payer of fixed**, also called the buyer or long side, gains when $R_M > R_K$ and hedges a planned loan.
>- The **receiver of fixed**, also called the seller or short side, gains when $R_M < R_K$ and hedges a planned deposit.
>
>No arbitrage sets the fair FRA rate equal to the forward rate, $R_K = R_F$, so a new FRA is worth zero. At a later date $t < T_1$, an FRA with fixed rate $R_K$ is worth to the receiver of fixed
>$$V_{\text{FRA}} = L(R_K - R_F)\tau\,P(t, T_2),$$
>where $R_F$ now uses today's prices $P(t, T_1)$ and $P(t, T_2)$. The payer of fixed holds $-V_{\text{FRA}}$. If a dealer quotes $R_K > R_F$, a trader receives fixed and locks in $L\tau(R_K - R_F)$ at $T_2$. If $R_K < R_F$, the trader pays fixed instead. These results assume borrowing and lending at the zero-coupon rates and no counterparty risk. An FRA is a [[2.2.2a Forward Contract|forward contract]] on an interest rate, and an [[2.2.5a Interest Rate Swap|interest rate swap]] is a series of FRAs.

>[!warning] Lecture Notation
>The lecture writes $N$ for the notional, RFR for $R_M$, and $f_{T_1,T_2}$ for the FRA rate $R_K$. It writes $r_{12}$ for the forward rate, so its fair-rate condition reads $f_{T_1,T_2} = r_{12}$. In its FRA arbitrage, $r_{12}$ compounds once over the period, so there it equals the simple rate $R_F$. It calls $N(\text{RFR} - f_{T_1,T_2})(T_2 - T_1)$ the payoff at $T_1$, meaning the amount set at $T_1$, with no discount. It writes $f$ for the value of an FRA. Its value formula prints $(T_1 - T_2)$ where the period length $T_2 - T_1$ belongs. The lecture does not name the sides of an FRA, and the buyer and seller labels follow market usage.

# Intuition

A firm that will borrow in six months does not know the rate it will pay. If rates rise, its interest bill rises with them. An FRA removes this risk. The firm agrees on a rate $R_K$ today, and the FRA pays the gap between the market rate and $R_K$. The loan costs $R_M$, the FRA refunds $R_M - R_K$, and the net cost is $R_K$.

Bond prices today fix the fair $R_K$. A trader can already lock in a rate from $T_1$ to $T_2$ with zero-coupon bonds. The trader shorts one bond that matures at $T_1$ and buys $P(0, T_1)/P(0, T_2)$ bonds that mature at $T_2$. The two legs cost the same, so the trade is free today. The trader owes $\$1$ at $T_1$ and receives $1 + R_F\tau$ at $T_2$. An FRA at any rate other than $R_F$ would let the trader combine the two routes for a sure profit.

# Properties

#### *(Rate Conventions)* An FRA quote must be compared with the simple rate $R_F$, which exceeds the continuous rate $r_{12}$.

Expanding $e^{r_{12}\tau} = 1 + R_F\tau$ gives $R_F = r_{12} + r_{12}^2\tau/2 + \dots$, so $R_F > r_{12}$ for any positive rate. For a six-month period with $r_{12} = 5\%$, the simple rate is $R_F = (e^{0.025} - 1)/0.5 = 5.0630\%$. The gap of about six basis points (a basis point is $0.01\%$) can make a fair quote look mispriced. A trader who compares the quote with $r_{12}$ may trade on an arbitrage that does not exist. The forward discount factor $P(0, T_2)/P(0, T_1)$ needs no convention. Working from bond prices avoids the error.

#### *(Shape of the Zero Curve)* The forward rate lies above the longer zero rate on a rising curve and below it on a falling curve.

Rearranging the continuous formula splits the forward rate into the longer zero rate and a slope term,
$$r_{12} = r_2 + (r_2 - r_1)\frac{T_1}{T_2 - T_1}.$$
The second term has the sign of the slope $r_2 - r_1$. On a rising curve, $r_2$ averages a low rate before $T_1$ with the forward rate after it. The forward rate must therefore be higher than $r_2$. The factor $T_1/(T_2 - T_1)$ is large when the period is short and far away. Small bumps in the zero curve then create large swings in forward rates. A spline fitted through zero rates can oscillate between data points. The forward rates computed from it oscillate even more. [[2.1.1f Yield Curves]] describes the zero curve itself.

#### *(Settlement at $T_1$)* The $T_1$ payment uses the realized rate $R_M$ as its discount rate, which makes it worth exactly the $T_2$ payment.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules state the payoff as an amount set at $T_1$, with no discount. The discounted payment at $T_1$, used here and in the examples, follows market practice.

At $T_1$ the amount $L(R_M - R_K)\tau$ is known, and it is interest for a period that ends at $T_2$. A party paid at $T_1$ can deposit the cash at $R_M$ until $T_2$. The deposit grows by $1 + R_M\tau$ and reaches the $T_2$ amount exactly. A party that pays at $T_1$ can borrow the cash at $R_M$ and repay the $T_2$ amount. The discount uses $R_M$ because $R_M$ is the market rate for the period on the settlement date. So the two settlement rules have the same value on every date. A valuation can use the $T_2$ form throughout.

#### *(Hedging a Future Loan)* A borrower who pays fixed on an FRA fixes the interest on a loan that starts at $T_1$.

Take a firm that will borrow $L$ at $T_1$ at the floating rate $R_M$. At $T_2$ it owes interest $LR_M\tau$ and receives $L(R_M - R_K)\tau$ from the FRA. Its net interest is $LR_K\tau$, whatever $R_M$ turns out to be. A firm that plans to lend at $T_1$ receives fixed instead. The hedge removes the loss from rising rates and also the gain from falling rates. Traders use the same tool for any borrowing planned at a future date. In the reverse trade on a stock with cash dividends, the short seller borrows each dividend when it is paid. An FRA fixes the rate on that loan today ([[2.2.2b Forward Price]]). The hedge is exact only if the loan rate moves one for one with the FRA's reference rate. A credit spread that the lender sets only at $T_1$ stays unhedged.

#### *(Not a Forecast)* The forward rate comes from today's bond prices, and it need not equal the rate traders expect at $T_1$.

The replication uses only $P(0, T_1)$ and $P(0, T_2)$. It needs no view on future rates, as with the [[2.2.2b Forward Price|forward price]] of a stock. Theories of the term structure (how rates vary with maturity) differ on what $R_F$ says about the future. The pure expectations theory treats the forward rate as an unbiased estimate of the future rate. The liquidity preference theory says investors prefer short bonds, whose prices move less. Long bonds then pay a premium, and the forward rate equals the expected rate plus that premium. Under this theory the forward rate tends to overstate the future rate. Under both theories the fair FRA rate stays $R_F$, because traders enforce it by arbitrage.

# Derivation

We want the fair FRA rate and the value of an FRA after inception. Assume that a trader can borrow and lend at the zero-coupon rates today and at $R_M$ at $T_1$.

Start with the forward rate. One dollar invested today until $T_2$ grows to $1/P(0, T_2)$. Invested until $T_1$, it grows to $1/P(0, T_1)$. Rolling that amount over at a rate fixed today gives $(1 + R_F\tau)/P(0, T_1)$ at $T_2$. Both plans are riskless, so the two amounts agree, and $1 + R_F\tau = P(0, T_1)/P(0, T_2)$. With continuous zero rates the same equality reads $e^{r_1T_1}e^{r_{12}(T_2 - T_1)} = e^{r_2T_2}$. Taking logs gives the formula for $r_{12}$.

Next, suppose a dealer quotes $R_K > R_F$. The trader receives fixed in the FRA and builds a loan from $T_1$ to $T_2$ out of zero-coupon bonds. The table lists the cash flows on notional $L$.

| Position | $t = 0$ | $T_1$ | $T_2$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lend $LP(0, T_1)$ until $T_1$ | $-LP(0, T_1)$ | $+L$ | |
| Borrow $LP(0, T_1)$ until $T_2$ | $+LP(0, T_1)$ | | $-L(1 + R_F\tau)$ |
| Lend $L$ from $T_1$ to $T_2$ at $R_M$ | | $-L$ | $+L(1 + R_M\tau)$ |
| FRA, receive $R_K$ and pay $R_M$ | $0$ | | $+L(R_K - R_M)\tau$ |
| Total | $0$ | $0$ | $L\tau(R_K - R_F)$ |

The loan until $T_2$ costs $LP(0, T_1)/P(0, T_2) = L(1 + R_F\tau)$ to repay. The terms in $R_M$ cancel at $T_2$, so the profit is known today. If $R_K < R_F$, the trader reverses every row and earns $L\tau(R_F - R_K)$. No arbitrage therefore requires $R_K = R_F$ when the FRA starts.

Finally, value an FRA that receives $R_K$ at a date $t < T_1$. Enter a new FRA today that pays the current forward rate $R_F$ and receives $R_M$. The new FRA costs nothing. At $T_2$ the pair pays
$$L(R_K - R_M)\tau + L(R_M - R_F)\tau = L(R_K - R_F)\tau.$$
The floating rate cancels, so this amount is certain. Its value today is the amount times $P(t, T_2)$, which gives $V_{\text{FRA}}$ in the callout. The same pair of offsetting contracts gives the [[2.2.2c Value of a Forward Contract|value of a forward contract]].

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Locking In a Loan Rate
>A firm will borrow $\$8$ million in six months for six months, at the six-month rate set on that date. Zero-coupon bonds that pay $\$1$ trade today at $P(0, 0.5) = 0.98064$ and $P(0, 1) = 0.96000$. The firm enters an FRA at the fair rate and pays fixed. The FRA rate is a simple rate over the six-month period.
>
>>[!example]- Find the fair FRA rate, the FRA payment if the six-month rate is $4.80\%$, and the firm's net interest.
>>The period is $\tau = 0.5$. The fair rate is the simple forward rate,
>>$$R_K = R_F = \frac{1}{0.5}\left(\frac{0.98064}{0.96000} - 1\right) = \frac{0.0215}{0.5} = 4.30\%.$$
>>The continuous forward rate is $\ln(1.0215)/0.5 = 4.2544\%$, a little lower, as expected.
>>
>>At $T_1$ the rate is $R_M = 4.80\%$. The firm pays fixed, so the FRA pays it
>>$$L(R_M - R_K)\tau = 8{,}000{,}000 \times (0.048 - 0.043) \times 0.5 = \$20{,}000.00.$$
>>Under settlement at $T_2$ the firm receives this amount at the end of the loan. Under settlement at $T_1$ it receives $20{,}000/1.024 = \$19{,}531.25$ when the loan starts. The loan costs $8{,}000{,}000 \times 0.048 \times 0.5 = \$192{,}000.00$ in interest. Net of the FRA, the firm pays $192{,}000 - 20{,}000 = \$172{,}000.00$. That is interest at $4.30\%$ for half a year, the rate the firm locked in.

>[!example] (Medium) Valuing an FRA After Five Months
>Five months ago a firm entered an FRA on $\$30$ million. It pays a fixed $4.25\%$ and receives the six-month floating rate. Today the period starts in four months and ends in ten months, so $\tau = 0.5$. Zero-coupon bonds trade today at $P(t, T_1) = 0.98637$ and $P(t, T_2) = 0.96250$. Rates in the FRA are simple over the period.
>
>>[!example]- Value the FRA for the firm and its counterparty, and find the error from using the continuous forward rate.
>>Today's simple forward rate is
>>$$R_F = \frac{1}{0.5}\left(\frac{0.98637}{0.96250} - 1\right) = \frac{0.0248}{0.5} = 4.96\%.$$
>>The forward rate has risen since the firm entered the FRA at $4.25\%$. The firm could now enter a new FRA that receives $4.96\%$ and pays floating, at no cost. The old and new FRAs together pay the firm $30{,}000{,}000 \times (0.0496 - 0.0425) \times 0.5 = \$106{,}500.00$ at $T_2$ for sure. Its value today is
>>$$106{,}500 \times 0.96250 = \$102{,}506.25.$$
>>The FRA is worth $\$102{,}506.25$ to the firm and $-\$102{,}506.25$ to its counterparty.
>>
>>The continuous forward rate is $r_{12} = \ln(1.0248)/0.5 = 4.8995\%$. Using it in place of $R_F$ values the FRA at $30{,}000{,}000 \times (r_{12} - 0.0425) \times 0.5 \times 0.9625 = \$93{,}770.74$. That figure is $\$8{,}735.51$ too low, about $8.5\%$ of the true value. The FRA pays simple interest, so only $R_F$ gives the right value.

>[!example] (Hard) Arbitrage Against a Dealer Quote
>Zero rates with annual compounding are $3.80\%$ for one year and $4.10\%$ for eighteen months. A dealer quotes an FRA on $\$50$ million for the period from year $1$ to year $1.5$. The quoted rate is $5.00\%$, simple over the period. A trader can borrow and lend at these zero rates today. In one year the trader can do so at the six-month rate $R_M$.
>
>>[!example]- Show that the quote is mispriced, build an arbitrage, and check its profit for $R_M = 3\%$ and $R_M = 7\%$. Then say whether settlement at $T_1$ changes the profit.
>>With annual compounding, $P(0, 1) = 1/1.038 = 0.963391$ and $P(0, 1.5) = 1.041^{-1.5} = 0.941508$. The fair rate is
>>$$R_F = \frac{1}{0.5}\left(\frac{0.963391}{0.941508} - 1\right) = 4.6486\%.$$
>>The quote of $5.00\%$ is too high, so the trader receives fixed.
>>
>>The trader lends $50{,}000{,}000 \times 0.963391 = \$48{,}169{,}556.84$ for one year, which returns $\$50{,}000{,}000$ at $T_1$. The trader funds this by borrowing the same amount for eighteen months. The loan grows to $48{,}169{,}556.84 \times 1.041^{1.5} = 48{,}169{,}556.84 \times 1.0621261 = \$51{,}162{,}145.10$ at $T_2$. At $T_1$ the trader lends the $\$50$ million for six months at $R_M$. The table shows the cash flows at $T_2$.
>>
>>| Cash flow at $T_2$ | $R_M = 3\%$ | $R_M = 7\%$ |
>>|---|---|---|
>>| Six-month deposit | $+50{,}750{,}000.00$ | $+51{,}750{,}000.00$ |
>>| FRA, receive $5\%$ and pay $R_M$ | $+500{,}000.00$ | $-500{,}000.00$ |
>>| Repay the eighteen-month loan | $-51{,}162{,}145.10$ | $-51{,}162{,}145.10$ |
>>| Total | $+87{,}854.90$ | $+87{,}854.90$ |
>>
>>The deposit and the FRA together pay $L(1 + R_K\tau) = \$51{,}250{,}000$ in every case. The profit is $\$87{,}854.90$ at $T_2$, worth $87{,}854.90 \times 0.941508 = \$82{,}716.07$ today. Nothing is paid at $t = 0$ or at $T_1$.
>>
>>Settlement at $T_1$ does not change the profit. With $R_M = 3\%$, the FRA pays $500{,}000/1.015 = \$492{,}610.84$ at $T_1$. The trader deposits it at $3\%$ for six months, and it grows back to $\$500{,}000$. With $R_M = 7\%$, the trader owes $500{,}000/1.035 = \$483{,}091.79$ at $T_1$. The trader borrows it at $7\%$ and repays $\$500{,}000$ at $T_2$.
