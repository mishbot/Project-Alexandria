>[!note] SIFT
>**SIFT (Scale-Invariant Feature Transform)** is a feature detector and descriptor that produces keypoints invariant to scale, rotation, and moderate illumination change, along with a 128-dimensional descriptor vector used for matching. It is the canonical feature in classical computer vision and remains a baseline against which every modern detector (ORB, SuperPoint) is measured.
>
>The pipeline has four stages:
>
>1. **Scale-space extrema detection.** Build a Gaussian scale-space pyramid $L(x, y, \sigma) = G(x, y, \sigma) * I(x, y)$ and compute the difference of Gaussians (DoG) across adjacent scales:
>$$D(x, y, \sigma) = L(x, y, k\sigma) - L(x, y, \sigma).$$
>Keypoints are local extrema of $D$ over the $3\times 3 \times 3$ spatial-scale neighborhood.
>
>2. **Keypoint localization.** Subpixel refinement via a Taylor expansion of $D$; reject low-contrast and edge-like extrema using the Hessian determinant-to-trace ratio (same criterion as [[4.1.1. Harris Corner Detection|Harris]]).
>
>3. **Orientation assignment.** Compute a gradient orientation histogram in a neighborhood around the keypoint, weighted by magnitude and a Gaussian window. The peak orientation (and any secondary peak within 80% of it) becomes the keypoint's canonical orientation.
>
>4. **Descriptor construction.** Rotate the $16 \times 16$ patch around the keypoint to its canonical orientation, divide into a $4\times 4$ grid of subregions, compute an 8-bin gradient orientation histogram in each subregion, and concatenate into a $4 \cdot 4 \cdot 8 = 128$-dimensional descriptor. Normalize to unit length; clip values above $0.2$; renormalize.
>
>Descriptors are matched across images by nearest-neighbor search in descriptor space, filtered by Lowe's ratio test.

# Intuition
[[4.1.1. Harris Corner Detection|Harris corners]] are local features but not scale-invariant: zooming in on an image changes which pixels look corner-like. SIFT layers two ideas on top of Harris to fix this. First, **scale selection**: search for features not just spatially but across a Gaussian pyramid, picking the scale at which each feature is most distinctive (automatic scale selection via DoG extrema). Second, **a rich descriptor**: once a keypoint is localized and oriented, summarize the gradient pattern in its neighborhood as a fingerprint that can be compared against other fingerprints by simple Euclidean distance.

The geometric invariances are earned one at a time: scale invariance from the pyramid, rotation invariance from the canonical orientation assignment, illumination invariance from gradient-based descriptors and the normalize-clip-renormalize step. The descriptor's grid-of-histograms structure trades exact pixel-matching for tolerance to small spatial deformations — two patches agree on their descriptor as long as they agree on the *distribution* of gradients in corresponding subregions, which is much more forgiving than requiring pixel-perfect alignment.

# Properties

## Scale-Space Construction

#### *(Gaussian pyramid)* Successive blurs with $\sigma, k\sigma, k^2\sigma, \dots$ produce an *octave* of scales; downsampling by 2 starts the next octave.

Typical parameters: $\sigma_0 = 1.6$, $s = 3$ scales per octave, $k = 2^{1/s}$. The pyramid covers several octaves of scale (often 4–6) to catch features at all sizes.

#### *(Difference of Gaussians)* DoG approximates the scale-normalized Laplacian $\sigma^2 \nabla^2 L$, whose extrema are the characteristic scales.

Analytically, $G(x,y,k\sigma) - G(x,y,\sigma) \approx (k - 1)\sigma^2 \nabla^2 G$, so DoG is a cheap approximation to the Laplacian without convolving with a second-derivative kernel. Extrema of DoG in the joint spatial-scale domain mark "blobs" at their characteristic scales.

## Keypoint Filtering

#### *(Subpixel localization)* A second-order Taylor expansion of $D$ about the discrete extremum yields the offset to the true continuum maximum.

Setting $\partial D/\partial x = 0$ gives a linear system whose solution $\hat{x}$ is the subpixel offset. If $|\hat{x}| > 0.5$ in any dimension, the keypoint is reassigned to the nearest neighbor and the solve is repeated.

#### *(Edge rejection)* Extrema along edges have one small and one large principal curvature; ratioing the Hessian's trace and determinant flags them.

The $2 \times 2$ Hessian of $D$ in the spatial plane has eigenvalues $\alpha \geq \beta$. If $\alpha/\beta = r$, then $\operatorname{tr}^2(H)/\det(H) = (r+1)^2/r$, which is minimized at $r = 1$. SIFT rejects keypoints with $r > 10$, eliminating edge-like responses.

## Descriptor Design

#### *(Rotation normalization)* Each keypoint is rotated to its dominant gradient orientation before descriptor extraction.

The orientation histogram has 36 bins covering $[0, 2\pi)$, each weighted by gradient magnitude times a Gaussian centered on the keypoint. The argmax (fitted with a parabola for subbin accuracy) becomes the canonical orientation; the patch is then rotated so this direction is "up."

#### *(Spatial-orientation histogram)* The descriptor is a $4 \times 4$ spatial grid of 8-bin orientation histograms, giving $128$ dimensions.

Gradients in the $16 \times 16$ patch are accumulated into the closest four spatial subregions and closest two orientation bins using trilinear interpolation. This smoothing reduces aliasing when the patch is slightly misaligned.

#### *(Illumination normalization)* Unit-length normalization, clipping at $0.2$, and renormalization provide robustness to contrast changes.

Brightness offsets are removed by gradient differencing. Contrast changes are removed by the first normalization. Nonlinear illumination (e.g., a highlight dominating one bin) is mitigated by clipping, after which the second normalization restores unit length.

## Matching

#### *(Nearest neighbor in descriptor space)* Descriptors are matched by Euclidean distance; efficient search uses KD-trees or approximate NN libraries.

With 128-D descriptors and thousands of keypoints per image, exact NN is slow. Practical systems use FLANN or similar approximate nearest-neighbor structures; the approximation rarely hurts match quality.

#### *(Lowe's ratio test)* A match is kept only if the nearest neighbor is substantially closer than the second-nearest.

If $d_1$ and $d_2$ are the distances to the nearest and second-nearest descriptors, the match is accepted iff $d_1 / d_2 < 0.8$. The intuition: an unambiguous match has one clear winner; a wrong match has many nearly-equidistant competitors. This single test discards the majority of false matches at the cost of rejecting a small fraction of correct ones.

## Successors and Variants

#### *(SURF)* Uses integral images and box filters to approximate DoG extrema at a fraction of the cost.

SURF descriptors are 64-dimensional (Haar-wavelet responses in a $4 \times 4$ grid) and are much faster to compute than SIFT, with comparable matching accuracy.

#### *(ORB)* Combines FAST corners with rotation-aware BRIEF binary descriptors; ~ 100× faster than SIFT.

ORB descriptors are 256-bit binary strings matched by Hamming distance, making them ideal for real-time systems (VO, SLAM).

#### *(SuperPoint)* Jointly learns detector and descriptor in a single CNN.

Trained with self-supervised homographic adaptation. Dominates SIFT on modern benchmarks but requires GPU inference.
