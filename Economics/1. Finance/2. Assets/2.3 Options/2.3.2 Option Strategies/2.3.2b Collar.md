>[!note] Collar
>A **collar** holds one share of a stock, buys a European put with strike $K_1$, and sells a European call with strike $K_2 > K_1$. Both options have the same expiry $T$. Let $p_1$ and $c_2$ be their premiums. At $T$ the position is worth
>$$S_T + \max(K_1 - S_T, 0) - \max(S_T - K_2, 0) = \begin{cases} K_1, & S_T \le K_1, \\ S_T, & K_1 < S_T < K_2, \\ K_2, & S_T \ge K_2. \end{cases}$$
>The first term is the share, the second is the long put, and the third is the short call. The put sets a floor $K_1$ under the share's value, and the call sets a cap $K_2$. The value at $T$ is therefore locked in the interval $[K_1, K_2]$. The options cost $p_1 - c_2$ today, which is a credit when it is negative. A **zero-cost collar** chooses the strikes so that $c_2 = p_1$, and the options then cost nothing. Consider an investor who buys the share at $S_0$ and sets up the collar at the same time. Ignoring interest, the profit at $T$ is the value minus $S_0 + p_1 - c_2$. The investor breaks even at $S_T = S_0 + p_1 - c_2$ when this price lies between the strikes. The maximum gain is $K_2 - S_0 - (p_1 - c_2)$, and the maximum loss is $S_0 - K_1 + (p_1 - c_2)$. A collar is the standard way to protect a share already owned at low or zero cost. Its value at $T$ equals the payoff of a [[2.3.2a Vertical Spread|bull call spread]] with strikes $K_1$ and $K_2$, raised by $K_1$.

# Intuition

An investor who holds a stock with a large gain may want protection against a fall over the next few months. A put gives that protection, but it costs a premium. Selling a call with a higher strike brings in a premium that pays for the put. In exchange, the investor gives up every gain above $K_2$. The investor keeps the share, and its value at $T$ can move only inside the band $[K_1, K_2]$. The name refers to this band, which the two options place around the share's value.

# Properties

#### *(Bull Spread Plus Bond)* A collar is a zero-coupon bond paying $K_1$ plus a bull call spread, and put-call parity prices it that way.

The value at $T$ equals $K_1 + \max(S_T - K_1, 0) - \max(S_T - K_2, 0)$ in every state. Let $S$ be the stock price today and $c_1$ the price of a call with strike $K_1$. [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity|Put-call parity]] at $K_1$ says $S + p_1 = c_1 + K_1e^{-rT}$. Subtracting $c_2$ from both sides gives the cost of the collar,
$$S + p_1 - c_2 = K_1e^{-rT} + (c_1 - c_2)
.$$
The left side is the share plus the two options. The right side is a bond paying $K_1$ plus a bull call spread. An investor could sell the share, buy the bond and the spread, and hold the same payoff. The bound $c_1 - c_2 \le (K_2 - K_1)e^{-rT}$ for vertical spreads then places the collar's cost between $K_1e^{-rT}$ and $K_2e^{-rT}$.

#### *(Zero Cost Brackets the Forward)* A zero-cost collar must have its floor below the forward price and its cap above it.

Let $F_0 = Se^{rT}$ be the [[2.2.2b Forward Price|forward price]] of a stock with no dividends. Parity gives $c - p = S - Ke^{-rT}$ at each strike $K$. So a call and a put with the same strike cost the same exactly when $K = F_0$. Suppose $K_2 \le F_0$. Then $c_2 \ge p_2 > p_1$, because puts cost more at higher strikes. The call is worth more than the put, so the collar pays a credit. Suppose instead $K_1 \ge F_0$. Then $p_1 \ge c_1 > c_2$, and the collar costs money. Zero cost therefore requires $K_1 < F_0 < K_2$. In the limit $K_1 = K_2 = F_0$, the two options form a short forward with delivery price $F_0$. The collar then locks the value at exactly $F_0$.

#### *(Floor Against Cap)* Raising the floor of a zero-cost collar lowers its cap.

A higher floor $K_1$ needs a more expensive put. The call must then bring in more premium, so its strike $K_2$ must fall. Take a stock at $\$80$ with six-month options priced by the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]] at $25\%$ volatility and $r = 4\%$. The forward price is $\$81.62$. The table lists the zero-cost cap for four floors.

| Floor $K_1$ | Put premium $p_1$ | Zero-cost cap $K_2$ |
|---|---|---|
| $\$70$ | $\$1.39$ | $\$96.64$ |
| $\$72$ | $\$1.86$ | $\$93.78$ |
| $\$75$ | $\$2.77$ | $\$89.72$ |
| $\$78$ | $\$3.92$ | $\$85.92$ |

Every row brackets the forward price, as the previous property requires. Exchanges list strikes at fixed steps. A collar built from listed options is therefore usually close to zero cost, and rarely exactly zero.

#### *(Protective Put)* Without the short call, the position is a protective put, which keeps the upside and pays the full put premium.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules use puts to hedge a long position but do not name the protective put.

A **protective put** holds a share and a put with strike $K_1$. Its value at $T$ is $\max(S_T, K_1)$, a floor with no cap. By parity it is worth a call with strike $K_1$ plus a bond paying $K_1$. A collar is a protective put plus a short call at $K_2$. The short call gives up the gains above $K_2$ and pays back $c_2$ of the put premium. With the prices in the previous table, a $\$72$ floor costs $\$1.86$ per share as a protective put. As a collar with a cap of $\$93.78$, it costs nothing.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Zero-Cost Collar
>An investor owns $100$ shares of a stock that trades at $\$45$. She buys six-month European puts with strike $\$40$ at $\$1.30$ each. She sells six-month European calls with strike $\$52$, also at $\$1.30$ each.
>
>>[!example]- Find the cost of the options and the value of her shares in six months if the stock ends at $\$32$, $\$47$, or $\$60$.
>>The options cost $100 \times (1.30 - 1.30) = \$0$, so this is a zero-cost collar.
>>
>>At $S_T = 32$ the put pays $40 - 32 = \$8$ per share, and the call expires. Each share is worth $\$40$, so the holding is worth $\$4{,}000$. At $S_T = 47$ both options expire, and the holding is worth $\$4{,}700$. At $S_T = 60$ the call holder buys at $\$52$, so the investor gives up $\$8$ per share. The holding is worth $\$5{,}200$. Whatever happens, the holding ends between $\$4{,}000$ and $\$5{,}200$.

>[!example] (Medium) A Collar That Pays a Credit
>An investor bought a share for $\$60$ a year ago, and it now trades at $\$80$. To protect the gain for six months, she buys a six-month put with strike $\$72$ for $\$1.86$. She also sells a six-month call with strike $\$88$ for $\$3.25$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the net premium, the range of values at expiry, and her gain at final prices of $\$65$, $\$84$, and $\$95$, with and without the collar.
>>The net premium is $p_1 - c_2 = 1.86 - 3.25 = -\$1.39$. She receives $\$1.39$ today. The share and options are worth between $\$72$ and $\$88$ at expiry. With the credit added and interest ignored, her total ends between $\$73.39$ and $\$89.39$.
>>
>>At $S_T = 65$ the collar is worth $\$72$. Her gain is $72 + 1.39 - 60 = \$13.39$, against $\$5$ unhedged. At $S_T = 84$ the collar is worth $\$84$. Her gain is $\$25.39$, against $\$24$ unhedged. At $S_T = 95$ the collar is worth $\$88$. Her gain is $\$29.39$, against $\$35$ unhedged.

>[!example] (Hard) A Collar That Cannot Be Free
>A stock trades at $\$80$ and pays no dividends. The risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A client asks a dealer for a six-month zero-cost collar with a floor of $\$82$ and a cap of $\$86$.
>
>>[!example]- Show that no fair zero-cost collar has these strikes, and bound the client's cost per share using only parity. Then find the single strike $K = K_1 = K_2$ that gives a zero-cost collar, and describe that position.
>>The forward price is $F_0 = 80e^{0.04 \times 0.5} = \$81.62$. The floor $\$82$ lies above $F_0$, so the collar cannot be free.
>>
>>The client pays $p(82) - c(86)$ for the options. Calls get cheaper as the strike rises, so $c(86) \le c(82)$. Hence
>>$$p(82) - c(86) \ge p(82) - c(82) = 82e^{-0.02} - 80 = 80.38 - 80 = \$0.38.$$
>>The equality is put-call parity at $K = 82$. The client must pay at least $\$0.38$ per share. With Black-Scholes-Merton prices at $25\%$ volatility, $p(82) = \$5.84$ and $c(86) = \$3.89$, so the fair cost is $\$1.95$.
>>
>>A collar with $K_1 = K_2 = K$ costs $p(K) - c(K) = Ke^{-rT} - S$, which is zero at $K = 80e^{0.02} = \$81.62$. The long put and short call at $\$81.62$ form a short forward. The share plus the short forward is worth exactly $\$81.62$ at expiry in every state. This position locks in the forward price of the stock.
