>[!note] Utility Maximization
>In **utility maximization** a consumer with utility $u(x_1, x_2)$ picks the best bundle in the [[6.2.1 Budget Constraint|budget set]]:
>$$\max_{x_1, x_2}\ u(x_1, x_2) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 \le M, \quad x_1 \ge 0, \quad x_2 \ge 0.$$
>Two steps turn this into a problem with one equality constraint. If $u$ is strictly increasing in at least one good, the budget binds, because leftover money would buy more of that good. The constraints $x_i \ge 0$ are dropped and checked after solving. The [[7.2 Lagrange Multipliers|Lagrangian]] is
>$$\mathcal{L}(x_1, x_2, \lambda) = u(x_1, x_2) - \lambda\,(p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 - M),$$
>and the first-order conditions (FOCs) are
>$$u'_{x_1} = \lambda p_1, \qquad u'_{x_2} = \lambda p_2, \qquad p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 = M.$$
>Dividing the first two gives the **tangency condition**
>$$\text{MRS} = -\frac{u'_{x_1}}{u'_{x_2}} = -\frac{p_1}{p_2}, \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad \frac{u'_{x_1}}{p_1} = \frac{u'_{x_2}}{p_2} = \lambda .$$
>The indifference curve has the slope of the budget line, and the last dollar spent on each good adds the same utility $\lambda$. With positive marginal utilities, $\lambda = u'_{x_i}/p_i > 0$. The second-order condition (SOC) for a maximum is a positive determinant of the bordered Hessian at the candidate point:
>$$|H| = \begin{vmatrix} 0 & -p_1 & -p_2 \\ -p_1 & u''_{x_1x_1} & u''_{x_1x_2} \\ -p_2 & u''_{x_2x_1} & u''_{x_2x_2} \end{vmatrix} = -p_1^2\, u''_{x_2x_2} + 2p_1p_2\, u''_{x_1x_2} - p_2^2\, u''_{x_1x_1} > 0 .$$
>The pattern $u''_{x_1x_1} < 0$, $u''_{x_2x_2} < 0$, $u''_{x_1x_2} > 0$ is sufficient, because every term is then positive. A negative $|H|$ marks a constrained minimum. The solution as a function of prices and income is the **Marshallian demand** $x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)$. For Cobb-Douglas $u = x_1^{\alpha}x_2^{1-\alpha}$ it is $x_1^* = \alpha M/p_1$ and $x_2^* = (1-\alpha)M/p_2$. For CES $u = (\alpha x_1^{\rho} + \beta x_2^{\rho})^{1/\rho}$ with $\rho < 1$, $\rho \ne 0$, it is
>$$x_1^* = \frac{M}{p_1\left[1 + \left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{\rho-1}}\left(\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}}\right]}, \qquad x_2^* = \frac{M}{p_2\left[1 + \left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\rho-1}}\left(\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right)^{\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}}\right]}.$$
>The checks for a tangency point with a negative quantity, a failed SOC, or a tie are in [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions]]. The utility reached at the optimum is the [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function|indirect utility]] $v(\mathbf{p}, M)$.

# Intuition

The consumer wants the highest indifference curve that still touches the budget set. In the diagram, $B$ is affordable and lies on the curve $\bar u_0$. That curve crosses the budget line, so moving along the line toward $A$ reaches higher curves. At $A$ the curve $\bar u_1$ touches the line without crossing it, and every other point on the line lies on a lower curve. The curve $\bar u_2$ would be better still, but no affordable bundle reaches it.

At $B$ the indifference curve is flatter than the budget line. The consumer would give up only a little of good $2$ for one more unit of good $1$. The market pays $p_1/p_2$ units of good $2$ for that unit. Selling good $1$ for good $2$ at the market rate therefore raises utility. The trades stop at $A$, where the consumer's own rate of exchange, the [[6.1.4 Indifference Curves and MRS|MRS]], equals the market rate.

# Properties

#### *(The Dollar Argument)* If $u'_{x_1}/p_1 > u'_{x_2}/p_2$, moving one dollar from good $2$ to good $1$ raises utility.

A dollar taken from good $2$ buys $1/p_2$ fewer units and lowers utility by about $u'_{x_2}/p_2$. The same dollar buys $1/p_1$ more units of good $1$ and raises utility by about $u'_{x_1}/p_1$. The consumer keeps moving money until the two ratios are equal, or until one good runs out. The second case is a corner solution. The common value $\lambda$ is the extra utility from one more dollar of income, spent in the best way. The envelope theorem gives $\partial v/\partial M = \lambda$, as shown in [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function]].

#### *(Exam Recipe)* Divide the two FOCs to remove $\lambda$, solve for one good in terms of the other, and substitute into the budget line.

The full sequence has five steps. Check that $u$ is strictly increasing in some good, so the budget binds. Write the Lagrangian and its three FOCs. Divide the first FOC by the second, which gives $x_2$ as a function of $x_1$. Substitute into $p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 = M$ and solve for $x_1$, then $x_2$. Check that both are nonnegative and that $|H| > 0$, or that preferences are [[6.1.2 Monotone and Convex Preferences|convex]]. A strictly increasing transformation of $u$ gives the same demand, so taking logs of a Cobb-Douglas function first makes the FOCs linear in $1/x_i$. The sign convention matters for $\lambda$. With $\mathcal{L} = u + \lambda\,(p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 - M)$ the multiplier comes out negative, so this note always subtracts the constraint.

#### *(An Ordinal Test)* The sign of $|H|$ at a tangency point does not depend on which utility function represents the preferences.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The invariance of the bordered Hessian under monotone transformations is an extension. The lecture stated only the formula for $|H|$ and the sufficient sign pattern.

The signs of $u''_{x_1x_1}$ and $u''_{x_1x_2}$ change under a [[6.1.3 Utility Functions|monotone transformation]]. The function $x_1x_2$ has $u''_{x_1x_1} = 0$, while $\sqrt{x_1x_2}$ has $u''_{x_1x_1} < 0$. The bordered Hessian does not have this problem. For $w = f(u)$ with $f' > 0$, the chain rule gives
$$|H_w| = f'(u)\,|H_u| - f''(u)\,\big(p_1 u'_{x_2} - p_2 u'_{x_1}\big)^2 .$$
At a tangency point $p_1 u'_{x_2} = p_2 u'_{x_1}$, so the last term vanishes and $|H_w|$ has the sign of $|H_u|$. So the SOC gives the same answer for every representation of the preferences. For $u = x_1x_2$ the sufficient pattern fails, since $u''_{x_1x_1} = u''_{x_2x_2} = 0$, yet $|H| = 2p_1p_2 > 0$.

#### *(Local vs Global)* A point with $|H| > 0$ is best among nearby bundles on the line, and convex preferences make it best overall.

The FOCs and the SOC use only derivatives at the candidate point. They carry no information about bundles far away on the budget line. With convex preferences the bundles at least as good as the tangency point form a convex set. That set lies on one side of the budget line. So no affordable bundle beats the tangency point. With non-convex preferences the method can return a local maximum, or a minimum, while the best bundle is at a corner. The method also needs an MRS at the optimum. For [[6.4c Perfect Complements|perfect complements]] the optimum is at a kink, where the MRS is undefined. That problem is solved from $\alpha x_1 = \beta x_2$ and the budget line.

# Derivation

We want the Marshallian demand for the CES utility $u = (\alpha x_1^{\rho} + \beta x_2^{\rho})^{1/\rho}$ with $\alpha, \beta > 0$, $\rho < 1$, and $\rho \ne 0$. The function is strictly increasing in both goods, so the budget binds. The Lagrangian is
$$\mathcal{L} = (\alpha x_1^{\rho} + \beta x_2^{\rho})^{1/\rho} - \lambda\,(p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 - M).$$
By the chain rule, the FOC for $x_1$ is
$$(\alpha x_1^{\rho} + \beta x_2^{\rho})^{\frac{1}{\rho} - 1}\, \alpha x_1^{\rho - 1} = \lambda p_1,$$
and the FOC for $x_2$ has $\beta x_2^{\rho-1}$ and $p_2$ in place of $\alpha x_1^{\rho-1}$ and $p_1$. Dividing the FOC for $x_1$ by the FOC for $x_2$ cancels the common factor and leaves
$$\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\left(\frac{x_1}{x_2}\right)^{\rho - 1} = \frac{p_1}{p_2}.$$
This is the tangency condition with the CES MRS. Isolating $(x_1/x_2)^{\rho-1}$, raising both sides to the power $1/(\rho-1)$, and solving for $x_2$ gives
$$x_2 = x_1 \left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{\rho-1}} \left(\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{\frac{1}{\rho-1}}.$$
Substitute into the budget line. Writing $p_2 = p_1 \cdot (p_2/p_1)$ collects the powers of $p_2/p_1$ into one term:
$$p_1 x_1 \left[1 + \left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{\rho-1}} \left(\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}}\right] = M.$$
Dividing by the bracket gives $x_1^*$ in the callout. Swapping the labels of the two goods, with $\alpha \leftrightarrow \beta$ and $p_1 \leftrightarrow p_2$, gives $x_2^*$. Both quantities are positive, so the dropped constraints $x_i \ge 0$ hold.

The SOC follows from the sign pattern. Direct differentiation gives
$$u''_{x_1x_1} = (\rho - 1)\,\alpha\beta\, x_1^{\rho-2} x_2^{\rho}\, (\alpha x_1^{\rho} + \beta x_2^{\rho})^{\frac{1}{\rho} - 2}, \qquad u''_{x_1x_2} = -\frac{x_1}{x_2}\, u''_{x_1x_1}.$$
For $\rho < 1$ the first is negative and the second positive, and $u''_{x_2x_2} < 0$ by symmetry, so $|H| > 0$. For $\rho > 1$ all three signs flip and $|H| < 0$. The same formula then returns a minimum, which is a case in [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions]]. As $\rho \to 0$, the factor $(\alpha/\beta)^{1/(\rho-1)}$ tends to $\beta/\alpha$ and $(p_2/p_1)^{\rho/(\rho-1)}$ tends to $1$, so $x_1^* \to \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\frac{M}{p_1}$, the Cobb-Douglas demand. [[6.4d CES Utility]] collects the other limits.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Coffee and Croissants
>A student spends $M = \$45$ a week on coffee at $p_1 = \$3$ a cup and croissants at $p_2 = \$5$ each. The student's utility is $u(x_1, x_2) = x_1^{1/3} x_2^{2/3}$.
>
>>[!example]- Solve for the optimal bundle with the Lagrangian, find $\lambda$, and check the tangency condition.
>>The Lagrangian is $\mathcal{L} = x_1^{1/3}x_2^{2/3} - \lambda(3x_1 + 5x_2 - 45)$. The FOCs are
>>$$\frac{1}{3}x_1^{-2/3}x_2^{2/3} = 3\lambda, \qquad \frac{2}{3}x_1^{1/3}x_2^{-1/3} = 5\lambda .$$
>>Dividing the first by the second gives $\frac{1}{2}\frac{x_2}{x_1} = \frac{3}{5}$, so $x_2 = 1.2\,x_1$. The budget line becomes $3x_1 + 6x_1 = 45$, so $x_1^* = 5$ cups and $x_2^* = 6$ croissants. Both are positive.
>>
>>The student spends $\$15$ on coffee and $\$30$ on croissants, one third and two thirds of the budget. From the first FOC, $\lambda = \frac{1}{9}(6/5)^{2/3} \approx 0.1255$. The second FOC gives $\frac{2}{15}(5/6)^{1/3} \approx 0.1255$, the same value. The last dollar on each good adds about $0.13$ units of utility. The MRS at $(5, 6)$ is $-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{6}{5} = -0.6 = -3/5$, the slope of the budget line.

>[!example] (Medium) Budget Shares of Cobb-Douglas
>A consumer has $u(x_1, x_2) = x_1^{\alpha}x_2^{1-\alpha}$ with $0 < \alpha < 1$, prices $p_1, p_2$, and income $M$.
>
>>[!example]- Derive the Marshallian demands and $\lambda$, and confirm the SOC.
>>The function $\ln u = \alpha \ln x_1 + (1-\alpha)\ln x_2$ is a strictly increasing transformation of $u$, so it has the same demand. With multiplier $\mu$ for this version, the FOCs are
>>$$\frac{\alpha}{x_1} = \mu p_1, \qquad \frac{1-\alpha}{x_2} = \mu p_2 .$$
>>So $p_1x_1 = \alpha/\mu$ and $p_2x_2 = (1-\alpha)/\mu$. Adding the two and using the budget line gives $M = 1/\mu$. Hence
>>$$x_1^* = \frac{\alpha M}{p_1}, \qquad x_2^* = \frac{(1-\alpha)M}{p_2}.$$
>>The consumer spends the share $\alpha$ of income on good $1$ at every price and income. The multiplier $\mu = 1/M$ belongs to $\ln u$. For $u$ itself, $\lambda = u'_{x_1}/p_1$ at the optimum:
>>$$\lambda = \frac{\alpha}{p_1}\left(\frac{x_2^*}{x_1^*}\right)^{1-\alpha} = \frac{\alpha^{\alpha}(1-\alpha)^{1-\alpha}}{p_1^{\alpha}p_2^{1-\alpha}} .$$
>>For the SOC, $u''_{x_1x_1} = \alpha(\alpha-1)u/x_1^2 < 0$, $u''_{x_2x_2} = -\alpha(1-\alpha)u/x_2^2 < 0$, and $u''_{x_1x_2} = \alpha(1-\alpha)u/(x_1x_2) > 0$. The sufficient pattern holds, so the tangency point is a maximum.

>[!example] (Hard) When the Sufficient Pattern Fails
>A student has $M = \$30$ for apples at $p_1 = \$1$ and granola bars at $p_2 = \$2$. The student's utility is $u(x_1, x_2) = \sqrt{x_1} + \sqrt{x_2}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the optimal bundle and decide whether it is a maximum, given that $u''_{x_1x_2} = 0$. Then check the answer against the CES formula.
>>The FOCs are $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x_1}} = \lambda$ and $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x_2}} = 2\lambda$. Dividing gives $\sqrt{x_2/x_1} = 1/2$, so $x_2 = x_1/4$. The budget line becomes $x_1 + x_1/2 = 30$, so $x_1^* = 20$ apples and $x_2^* = 5$ bars, with $\lambda = 1/(2\sqrt{20}) \approx 0.112$.
>>
>>The sufficient pattern needs $u''_{x_1x_2} > 0$, and here $u''_{x_1x_2} = 0$. The pattern is only sufficient, so we compute $|H|$ directly. With $u''_{x_1x_1} = -\frac{1}{4}x_1^{-3/2} \approx -0.0028$ and $u''_{x_2x_2} = -\frac{1}{4}x_2^{-3/2} \approx -0.0224$,
>>$$|H| = -p_1^2\, u''_{x_2x_2} + 0 - p_2^2\, u''_{x_1x_1} \approx 0.0224 + 4(0.0028) \approx 0.034 > 0 .$$
>>The point is a maximum. The utility is the square root of the CES function with $\alpha = \beta = 1$ and $\rho = 1/2$, a monotone transformation, so the CES formula applies. Here $\frac{1}{\rho-1} = -2$ and $\frac{\rho}{\rho-1} = -1$. The formula gives $x_1^* = 30/\big(1\cdot[1 + 2^{-1}]\big) = 20$ and $x_2^* = 30/\big(2\,[1 + (1/2)^{-1}]\big) = 5$. The student spends $\$20$ on apples and $\$10$ on bars. Unlike Cobb-Douglas, the spending shares depend on prices, and here the cheaper good gets the larger share.
