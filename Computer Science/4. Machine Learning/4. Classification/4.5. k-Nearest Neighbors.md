>[!note] k-Nearest Neighbors
>The **k-nearest neighbors** method (k-NN) predicts the output at a query $\mathbf{x}$ from the $k$ training points closest to it. It stores the training set $(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$, $i = 1, \dots, n$, and fits no parameters. Let $N_k(\mathbf{x})$ be the indices of the $k$ training inputs nearest to $\mathbf{x}$ under a distance $\operatorname{dist}$. Here $k$ counts neighbors, so classes are indexed by $c \in \{1, \dots, K\}$. Classification takes a majority vote, and regression takes an average:
>$$\hat y(\mathbf{x}) = \arg\max_{c} \sum_{i \in N_k(\mathbf{x})} \mathbb{1}[y_i = c], \qquad \hat f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \in N_k(\mathbf{x})} y_i.$$
>Here $\hat y$ is the predicted class and $\hat f$ the predicted number. The vote fractions $\hat P(y = c \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \in N_k(\mathbf{x})}\mathbb{1}[y_i = c]$ estimate the class probabilities. The vote is therefore a plug-in version of the [[4.1. Bayes Classifier|Bayes classifier]]. A **weighted** variant gives neighbor $i$ the weight $1/\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i)$. Five distances are common:
>
>- **Euclidean**: $\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{z}\|_2$, the default for continuous features.
>- **Manhattan**: $\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{z}\|_1$, less sensitive to one large coordinate difference.
>- **Minkowski**: $\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{z}\|_p$, which contains both as $p = 2$ and $p = 1$ ([[3.5b p-Norm]]).
>- **Cosine**: $1 - \mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{z}/(\|\mathbf{x}\|\,\|\mathbf{z}\|)$, for text and embeddings, where direction matters more than length.
>- **Hamming**: the number of coordinates that differ, for categorical or binary features.
>
>The choice of $k$ sets the bias-variance tradeoff:
>
>- **$k = 1$**: zero training error when inputs are distinct, and high variance. Each training point decides every query in its own Voronoi cell.
>- **Large $k$**: smoother predictions and higher bias. At $k = n$ every query gets the majority class or the global mean.
>- **In practice**: choose $k$ by [[2.3.2. Cross-Validation|cross-validation]], and use odd $k$ in binary problems to avoid tied votes.
>
>Features must be on comparable scales, so standardize them first. As $n \to \infty$, the 1-NN error rate satisfies $R^* \le R_{1\text{NN}} \le 2R^*$, where $R^*$ is the Bayes error (Cover and Hart 1967). Training costs nothing, and a brute-force prediction costs $O(nd)$ per query.

# Intuition

The method assumes that inputs close to each other tend to have similar outputs. To label a new patient, it finds the $k$ most similar past patients and lets them vote. Nothing is computed before a query arrives, so k-NN is called a **lazy** or **instance-based** learner. It is also **nonparametric**. The model is the training set itself, so its flexibility grows with $n$.

With $k = 1$, each training point decides every query in its **Voronoi cell**. This cell is the region of inputs closer to that point than to any other training point. The 1-NN boundary consists of the cell faces that separate points of different classes. Larger $k$ averages over more points and smooths this boundary. ISLR ch. 2 and ESL ch. 2 and 13 treat the method in detail.

# Properties

#### *(k Sets Bias and Variance)* In k-NN regression the variance falls as $\sigma^2/k$, while the bias tends to grow with $k$.

Fix the training inputs and let $y_i = f(\mathbf{x}_i) + \varepsilon_i$ with noise variance $\sigma^2$. Write $\mathbf{x}_{(l)}$ for the $l$-th nearest neighbor of a query $\mathbf{x}_0$. The expected squared error at $\mathbf{x}_0$ splits into three terms (ESL eq. 7.10):
$$\mathbb{E}\big[(y_0 - \hat f(\mathbf{x}_0))^2\big] = \sigma^2 + \Big[f(\mathbf{x}_0) - \frac{1}{k}\sum_{l=1}^k f(\mathbf{x}_{(l)})\Big]^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{k}.$$
The first term is the irreducible noise. The second is the squared bias, which grows as farther neighbors with different values of $f$ enter the average. The third is the variance of an average of $k$ independent noise terms. The effective number of parameters of k-NN is about $n/k$. With $n = 1{,}000$ and $k = 10$, the method behaves like a model with $100$ parameters. [[2.3.1. Bias-Variance Decomposition]] derives the general decomposition.

#### *(Twice the Bayes Error)* With unlimited data, 1-NN makes at most twice as many errors as the best possible classifier.

As $n \to \infty$, the nearest neighbor of $\mathbf{x}$ converges to $\mathbf{x}$. Its label then behaves like an independent draw from the same conditional distribution. For two classes, write $\eta(\mathbf{x}) = P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x})$. The 1-NN rule errs when the query's label and the neighbor's label differ, with probability $2\eta(1 - \eta)$. The Bayes rule errs with probability $\min(\eta, 1 - \eta)$. Since $2\eta(1-\eta) \le 2\min(\eta, 1-\eta)$, averaging over $\mathbf{x}$ gives the factor $2$. The full result for $K$ classes is
$$R^* \le R_{1\text{NN}} \le R^*\Big(2 - \frac{K}{K - 1}R^*\Big)$$
(Cover and Hart 1967). If $k \to \infty$ and $k/n \to 0$, the k-NN error converges to $R^*$ for every distribution (Stone 1977). Both results are asymptotic. At finite $n$ in high dimensions, the nearest neighbor can be far from the query, and neither result applies.

#### *(Scale and Irrelevant Features)* The distance weighs every feature equally, so units and useless features change which points count as neighbors.

A feature measured in large units dominates the Euclidean distance. In the Medium example, floor area in square meters outweighs bedroom count until both are standardized. Irrelevant features add random differences to every distance. The nearest points in the full space then differ from the nearest points in the relevant features. With many useless features, k-NN needs far more data than a method that can ignore them. Remedies include feature selection and learning a Mahalanobis distance $\sqrt{(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{z})^T\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{z})}$ with $\mathbf{M}$ [[4.2e Positive Semidefinite Matrix|positive semidefinite]]. Large-margin nearest neighbors (Weinberger and Saul 2009) learns $\mathbf{M}$ from labeled data.

#### *(Curse of Dimensionality)* In high dimensions the $k$ nearest training points lie far from the query, and k-NN needs exponentially many samples.

Take uniform data in the unit cube. A cubical neighborhood that holds a fraction $r$ of the points needs edge length $r^{1/d}$. In $d = 10$ dimensions, capturing $1\%$ of the data needs $63\%$ of each feature's range (ESL 2.5). The average over such a neighborhood mixes points with very different values of $f$, so the bias term above grows. Distances also concentrate, so the nearest and the farthest training points lie at nearly the same distance. k-NN works in high dimensions only when the data lie near a low-dimensional set. Images and text embeddings often do. [[7.2. Curse of Dimensionality]] treats these effects in full.

#### *(Prediction Cost)* Tree indexes speed up queries only in low dimensions, so large systems use approximate search.

Each query computes $n$ distances in $O(nd)$ time and selects the $k$ smallest in $O(n)$ time. The model also stores all $n$ training points. A KD-tree (Bentley 1975) cuts query time to about $O(\log n)$ in low dimensions, but not beyond about $20$ dimensions. Large systems use approximate nearest-neighbor search, which returns close points that are not always the closest. [[4.4. Feature Matching|Descriptor matching]] in computer vision uses such search. Leave-one-out cross-validation is cheap for k-NN. Removing a point only means excluding it from its own neighbor list, so no model is refit.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Voting on a New Patient
>A clinic records two standardized measurements per patient. Five past patients are sick at $(1, 0)$, $(2, 2)$ and $(0, -2)$, and healthy at $(0, 1.2)$ and $(-1.3, 0)$. A new patient measures $(0, 0)$.
>
>>[!example]- Classify the new patient with $k = 1$, $k = 3$ and $k = 5$.
>>From $(0, 0)$, the sick patients lie at Euclidean distances $1$, $2.83$ and $2$. The healthy ones lie at $1.2$ and $1.3$. In order, the neighbors are sick ($1$), healthy ($1.2$), healthy ($1.3$), sick ($2$) and sick ($2.83$). With $k = 1$ the nearest patient is sick, so the prediction is sick. With $k = 3$ the vote is two healthy against one sick, so the prediction is healthy, with $\hat P(\text{sick}) = 1/3$. With $k = 5$ every patient votes, three sick against two healthy, so the prediction is the overall majority, sick. The prediction depends on $k$, and cross-validation picks among these values.

>[!example] (Medium) House Prices Before and After Scaling
>An agent predicts the price of a $100$ m², 3-bedroom house with 3-NN regression. Six recent sales, as (area, bedrooms, price), are $(98, 1, 280)$, $(103, 1, 290)$, $(96, 2, 310)$, $(110, 3, 400)$, $(89, 3, 370)$ and $(116, 4, 450)$. Prices are in thousands of dollars.
>
>>[!example]- Predict the price with raw features and with standardized features.
>>With raw features the distances are $2.83$, $3.61$, $4.12$, $10$, $11$ and $16.03$. The three nearest sales are the first three, and the prediction is $(280 + 290 + 310)/3 = 293.3$. Area differences of a few square meters dominate these distances, so all three neighbors have one or two bedrooms. The sample standard deviations are $9.82$ m² for area and $1.21$ for bedrooms. Dividing each difference by its feature's standard deviation gives distances $1.66$, $1.68$, $0.92$, $1.02$, $1.12$ and $1.83$. The three nearest sales are now $(96, 2)$, $(110, 3)$ and $(89, 3)$. The prediction is $(310 + 400 + 370)/3 = 360$, about $67$ thousand dollars higher. The standardized neighbors are close to the query in both area and bedrooms.

>[!example] (Hard) Asymptotic Error in a Noisy Region
>A screening program classifies patients by a health profile. In one region of profiles, $10\%$ of patients are sick, so $\eta(\mathbf{x}) = 0.1$ throughout the region. The training set is very large. The $k$ nearest neighbors of any query in the region are then independent patients with the same $\eta$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the error rate in this region for the Bayes rule, 1-NN, 3-NN and 5-NN.
>>The Bayes rule always predicts healthy and errs on the sick $10\%$, so $R^* = 0.1$. 1-NN copies one neighbor's label. It errs when the query and the neighbor differ, with probability $2(0.1)(0.9) = 0.18$. This equals the two-class Cover-Hart bound $2R^*(1 - R^*) = 0.18$, because $\eta$ is constant. 3-NN predicts sick when at least two of three neighbors are sick, with probability $p_3 = 0.1^3 + 3(0.1)^2(0.9) = 0.028$. Its error is $0.1(1 - p_3) + 0.9\,p_3 = 0.0972 + 0.0252 = 0.1224$. For 5-NN, at least three of five neighbors must be sick, with probability $p_5 = 0.00856$. Its error is $0.1(1 - p_5) + 0.9\,p_5 = 0.1068$. Larger $k$ moves the error toward $R^*$, because a larger vote finds the majority class more reliably. This also requires $k/n \to 0$, so that the neighbors stay close to the query.
