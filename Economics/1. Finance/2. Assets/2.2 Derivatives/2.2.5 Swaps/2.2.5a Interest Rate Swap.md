>[!note] Interest Rate Swap
>An **interest rate swap** (IRS) is an over-the-counter contract in which two parties exchange interest payments. The payments are computed on a notional $L$, a principal that never changes hands. They fall on set dates $T_1, \dots, T_n$, often every six months. Let $\tau_i = T_i - T_{i-1}$ be the length of period $i$ in years. A swap has two legs.
>- **The fixed leg** pays $LR_S\tau_i$ at each $T_i$, where $R_S$ is the fixed rate written into the contract.
>- **The floating leg** pays $LR_M^{i-1,i}\tau_i$ at each $T_i$, where $R_M^{i-1,i}$ is the realized reference rate for $[T_{i-1}, T_i]$.
>
>Common reference rates are SOFR and IBOR rates such as EURIBOR. Only the difference between the legs changes hands. At each $T_i$ the fixed receiver gets the net amount below, and the fixed payer gets its negative,
>$$L\big(R_S - R_M^{i-1,i}\big)\tau_i.$$
>Both rates use compounding at the payment frequency, so a six-month rate of $4\%$ pays $0.02L$. The date on which the floating rate becomes known depends on the reference rate.
>- An IBOR or Term SOFR rate for $[T_{i-1}, T_i]$ is fixed at $T_{i-1}$ and paid at $T_i$. Traders call this **set in advance and paid in arrears**.
>- Compounded SOFR is built from the overnight rates realized during the period, so it is known only at $T_i$. A swap with this floating leg is an **overnight indexed swap (OIS)**.
>
>Let $\text{DF}_i$ be the discount factor from $T_i$ to today, taken from the discount curve (usually OIS). Let $R_F^{i-1,i}$ be today's forward rate for period $i$, except that the current period keeps the rate already set. The value to the fixed receiver has two equal forms.
>- **The FRA method** treats the swap as a strip of [[2.2.2e Forward Rate Agreement|forward rate agreements]] (FRAs) with one shared fixed rate $R_S$.
>- **The bond method** treats the swap as a long fixed-rate bond and a short floating-rate note (FRN). The fixed bond's coupon rate is $R_S$, and the FRN's coupons follow $R_M$.
>
>Written out, the two methods give
>$$V_{\text{swap}} = L\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(R_S - R_F^{i-1,i}\big)\tau_i\,\text{DF}_i = B_{\text{fix}} - B_{\text{fl}},$$
>$$B_{\text{fix}} = LR_S\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_i\,\text{DF}_i + L\,\text{DF}_n, \qquad B_{\text{fl}} = L\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_F^{i-1,i}\tau_i\,\text{DF}_i + L\,\text{DF}_n.$$
>Each term of the first sum is one FRA's value. The face values $L\,\text{DF}_n$ cancel between the two bonds. The fixed payer holds $-V_{\text{swap}}$. With one curve for the floating rate and the discounting, the FRN is worth par $L$ right after each reset. Between resets it is worth $B_{\text{fl}} = L\big(1 + R_M^{0,1}\tau_1\big)\text{DF}_1$, where $R_M^{0,1}$ is the rate already set for the current period.
>
>The **swap rate** $R_S^e$ is the fixed rate that makes a new swap worth zero. It is a weighted average of the forward rates,
>$$R_S^e = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_F^{i-1,i}\tau_i\,\text{DF}_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_i\,\text{DF}_i} \;\overset{\text{one curve}}{=}\; \frac{1 - \text{DF}_n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_i\,\text{DF}_i}.$$
>The weights are $\tau_i\text{DF}_i$, and their sum $A = \sum_i\tau_i\text{DF}_i$ is the **annuity factor**. A swap with $R_S = R_S^e$ is **on-market** and worth zero, though each FRA inside it can have a nonzero value. Once rates move, $R_S$ differs from the current swap rate and the swap is **off-market**.
>
>Firms use swaps to turn fixed-rate debt or assets into floating and back. They also use them to hedge rate risk and to bet on rates. Collateralized swaps are now usually discounted with OIS, which the market treats as risk free. So a swap on IBOR uses one curve for its forward rates and another for discounting. A swap is the multi-period version of an FRA, and a [[2.2.5b Currency Swap]] adds principal exchanges in two currencies.

>[!warning] Notation for the Notional and the Two Bonds
>Some texts write $N$ for the notional, $B(R_S, T_n, N)$ for the fixed-rate bond, and $FRN(T_n, N)$ for the floating-rate note. These notes write $L$, $B_{\text{fix}}$, and $B_{\text{fl}}$. The notional $L$ matches [[2.2.2e Forward Rate Agreement]], and $\text{DF}_i$ is the zero-coupon price that note writes as $P(0, T_i)$.

# Intuition

Firms often earn income at one kind of rate and owe interest at the other. A bank that funds fixed-rate mortgages with floating-rate deposits loses money when rates rise. Refinancing every loan would be slow and costly. A swap lets the bank change the rate it pays without rewriting any loan. It needs no cash at the start, so the bank can hedge without raising new funds.

# Properties

#### *(Reading the Timing)* The payment at $T_i$ uses the rate fixed at $T_{i-1}$, so the next IBOR payment is always known.

A common error is to use the rate observed on the payment date. With semiannual payments, the rate fixed at month 6 is paid at month 12. The rate fixed on the final date $T_n$ affects no payment, because no period follows it. In a valuation, the current period's rate was fixed at the last reset. It enters the FRA sum as it is, and only later periods use forward rates. The discount factor $\text{DF}_i$ uses the time from today to $T_i$, which differs from $\tau_i$ between resets. Under compounded SOFR, the amount due at $T_i$ stays unknown until $T_i$ itself. So a firm learns the size of each payment only on the day it is due.

#### *(Changing Fixed Into Floating)* A swap switches a loan or an investment between fixed and floating rates without changing the original contract.

A firm with a floating loan at $R_M + s$ can enter a swap that pays $R_S$ and receives $R_M$. Its cost per period is then
$$(R_M + s) + R_S - R_M = R_S + s.$$
The $R_M$ paid to the lender cancels the $R_M$ received in the swap. So the cost stays fixed whatever the floating rate does. The spread $s$ stays with the loan. A firm with fixed debt at rate $c$ makes the opposite trade. It receives $R_S$ and pays $R_M$, so its cost becomes $R_M + (c - R_S)$. An investor holding a fixed-rate bond can pay fixed and receive $R_M$, so its income floats. Swaps on many maturities trade so actively that dealers build yield curves from swap rates.

#### *(Comparative Advantage)* Two firms can both borrow more cheaply if each borrows where its cost gap is smaller and they swap.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules name comparative advantage only as a reason for currency swaps. The interest rate version here is the standard textbook argument.

Firm A can borrow at $3.80\%$ fixed or SOFR $+ 0.20\%$. Firm B can borrow at $5.30\%$ fixed or SOFR $+ 1.00\%$. B pays $1.50$ points more than A for fixed debt but only $0.80$ points more for floating debt. So B has a **comparative advantage** in floating debt, and A in fixed debt. The gap between the gaps, $0.70$ points, is the total saving the two firms can share. Suppose A wants floating debt and B wants fixed debt. A borrows fixed at $3.80\%$, and B borrows floating at SOFR $+ 1.00\%$. In the swap, B pays A $3.95\%$ fixed and A pays B SOFR. A's net cost is SOFR $- 0.15\%$, and B's is a fixed $4.95\%$. Each saves $0.35$ points. A bank in the middle can take part of the saving. Suppose the bank pays A $3.90\%$ and charges B $4.00\%$. Each firm then saves $0.30$ points, and the bank keeps $0.10\%$ a year. B's saving is less certain than it looks. Lenders reset B's floating loan every period and can raise the $1.00\%$ spread if B's credit worsens.

#### *(One Curve or Two)* The FRN is worth par at a reset only when one curve sets both the floating rate and the discounting.

A discount rate for derivatives must carry no default risk and be open to market participants. Treasury rates fail the second test, since only the US government borrows at them. SOFR and OIS rates come from secured overnight lending, so the market treats them as risk free. IBOR is a rate on unsecured loans between banks, so it carries bank credit risk. Before the 2008 crisis, banks built discount curves from LIBOR. After it, OIS and other secured rates replaced LIBOR for most collateralized derivatives. The discount rate can also depend on the collateral agreement, called the credit support annex (CSA). This practice is called differential discounting. A swap on IBOR discounted with OIS therefore uses two curves. Its IBOR forward rates come from the prices of traded IBOR swaps. Its FRN is worth $L\sum_i R_F^{i-1,i}\tau_i\text{DF}_i + L\,\text{DF}_n$, which is not $L$ at a reset. An OIS swap discounted on the OIS curve uses one curve, so the par shortcut holds for it.

#### *(Off-Market Value)* On a reset date, an old swap's value depends on the gap between its fixed rate and today's swap rate.

Take a reset date $t$, right after that day's payment. The rate set at $t$ equals today's forward rate for the first period. By the definition of the swap rate, $L\sum_i R_F^{i-1,i}\tau_i\text{DF}_i = LR_S^e(t)A(t)$ over the remaining dates. Substituting this into the FRA form gives
$$V_{\text{swap}}(t) = L\big(R_S - R_S^e(t)\big)A(t).$$
Here $R_S^e(t)$ is the swap rate for the remaining dates and $A(t)$ is their annuity factor. A fixed receiver gains when swap rates fall below its fixed rate, as a bondholder does. Between resets the first period pays a rate set earlier, so the formula fails and the FRA sum is needed. On an upward-sloping curve, the early forward rates sit below the swap rate and the late ones above it. So the fixed receiver of an on-market swap holds positive FRAs early and negative FRAs late.

#### *(Contract Variations)* Many swaps change the plain terms to fit a particular loan or view.

The notional can change over time, for example to follow a loan repaid in installments. The two legs can pay at different frequencies. The floating leg can carry a cap or a floor. A basis swap exchanges two floating rates. An extendable swap lets one side lengthen it, and a cancellable swap lets one side end it early. Firms use one swap instead of $n$ separate FRAs because one contract is easier to administer and cheaper to trade. Over-the-counter interest rate derivatives had about $\$668$ trillion of notional outstanding in June 2025. The figure comes from the Bank for International Settlements.

# Derivation

We want the two valuation forms and the swap rate, from the fixed receiver's side.

The swap is worth the present value of its net payments,
$$V_{\text{swap}} = L\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(R_S - R_M^{i-1,i}\big)\tau_i\,\text{DF}_i.$$
Here $\text{DF}_i$ discounts the payment at $T_i$ to today. The floating rates for later periods are unknown today. An FRA that pays $R_F^{i-1,i}$ and receives $R_M^{i-1,i}$ costs nothing to enter. So receiving $LR_M^{i-1,i}\tau_i$ at $T_i$ is worth the same today as receiving $LR_F^{i-1,i}\tau_i$. Replacing each unknown rate by its forward rate gives the FRA form. The current period keeps its known rate.

Next, add and subtract $L\,\text{DF}_n$. The fixed coupons plus $L\,\text{DF}_n$ form $B_{\text{fix}}$, a bond with coupon rate $R_S$ and face $L$. The floating coupons plus $L\,\text{DF}_n$ form $B_{\text{fl}}$. This gives $V_{\text{swap}} = B_{\text{fix}} - B_{\text{fl}}$.

With one curve, the forward rate satisfies $1 + R_F^{i-1,i}\tau_i = \text{DF}_{i-1}/\text{DF}_i$, so $R_F^{i-1,i}\tau_i\text{DF}_i = \text{DF}_{i-1} - \text{DF}_i$. The sum in the FRN then telescopes,
$$B_{\text{fl}} = LR_M^{0,1}\tau_1\text{DF}_1 + L\sum_{i=2}^{n}\big(\text{DF}_{i-1} - \text{DF}_i\big) + L\,\text{DF}_n = L\big(1 + R_M^{0,1}\tau_1\big)\text{DF}_1.$$
The middle sum collapses to $L(\text{DF}_1 - \text{DF}_n)$. On a reset date the newly set rate satisfies $1 + R_M^{0,1}\tau_1 = 1/\text{DF}_1$, so $B_{\text{fl}} = L$.

At inception, set the FRA form equal to zero and solve for the fixed rate. With one curve and $\text{DF}_0 = 1$, the numerator telescopes to $1 - \text{DF}_n$, so
$$R_S^e = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_F^{i-1,i}\tau_i\,\text{DF}_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_i\,\text{DF}_i} \;\overset{\text{one curve}}{=}\; \frac{1 - \text{DF}_n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_i\,\text{DF}_i}.$$
The denominator is the annuity factor, the value of receiving $\tau_i$ on every payment date.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Floating Loan Turned Fixed
>A manufacturer has a $\$28$ million loan that pays 6-month Term SOFR $+ 1.40\%$ every six months for 1.5 years. It enters a swap on the same notional and dates, paying $3.95\%$ fixed and receiving 6-month Term SOFR. All rates use semiannual compounding, so each payment is $L \times \text{rate} \times 0.5$. Term SOFR was $4.30\%$ at $t = 0$, $3.70\%$ at $t = 0.5$, $4.05\%$ at $t = 1$, and $3.50\%$ at $t = 1.5$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the firm's net swap payment and total interest on each date, and name the fixing that affects no payment.
>>Each payment uses the rate fixed six months earlier. The fixed leg costs $28{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0395 \times 0.5 = \$553{,}000$ each period. The firm's net swap receipt is $L(R_M - R_S)(0.5)$ on each date,
>>$$t = 0.5: +\$49{,}000, \qquad t = 1: -\$35{,}000, \qquad t = 1.5: +\$14{,}000.$$
>>For example, $28{,}000{,}000(0.0430 - 0.0395)(0.5) = \$49{,}000$. The loan interest at $t = 0.5$ is $28{,}000{,}000(0.0430 + 0.0140)(0.5) = \$798{,}000$. After the swap receipt the firm pays $798{,}000 - 49{,}000 = \$749{,}000$. The same total appears on every date, since $749{,}000 = 28{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0535 \times 0.5$. The all-in rate is a fixed $3.95\% + 1.40\% = 5.35\%$. The $3.50\%$ fixing at $t = 1.5$ affects no payment, because the swap ends that day.

>[!example] (Medium) Pricing a New Swap
>A dealer quotes a new 2-year swap on $\$15$ million with semiannual payments. Zero rates with continuous compounding are $2.60\%$, $2.90\%$, $3.10\%$, and $3.25\%$ for 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 years. One curve sets both the floating rate and the discount factors. Both legs use semiannual compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Find the swap rate, and value each of the four FRAs inside the swap for the fixed receiver.
>>The discount factors $\text{DF}_i = e^{-r_iT_i}$ are $0.987084$, $0.971416$, $0.954565$, and $0.937067$. Each period has $\tau_i = 0.5$, so the annuity factor is $A = 0.5 \times 3.850133 = 1.925066$. On one curve the swap rate is
>>$$R_S^e = \frac{1 - 0.937067}{1.925066} = 3.2691\%.$$
>>The forward rates follow from $R_F^{i-1,i} = (\text{DF}_{i-1}/\text{DF}_i - 1)/0.5$, with $\text{DF}_0 = 1$. With unrounded factors they are $2.6170\%$, $3.2257\%$, $3.5308\%$, and $3.7344\%$. FRA $i$ is worth $L(R_S^e - R_F^{i-1,i})(0.5)\,\text{DF}_i$ to the fixed receiver. With unrounded rates, the four values are
>>$$+\$48{,}278.53, \qquad +\$3{,}159.99, \qquad -\$18{,}735.31, \qquad -\$32{,}703.21.$$
>>The two gains add to $\$51{,}438.52$, and the two losses add to the same amount. So the swap is worth zero at inception, as an on-market swap must be. The curve slopes upward, so the fixed receiver gains on the early periods and loses on the late ones.

>[!example] (Hard) Valuing an Existing Swap Two Ways
>A utility pays $3.30\%$ fixed and receives 6-month floating on $\$25$ million, with semiannual payments. The swap has 13 months left, with payments in 1, 7, and 13 months. The floating rate fixed 5 months ago was $3.90\%$. Zero rates with continuous compounding are $3.60\%$ (1 month), $3.75\%$ (7 months), and $3.85\%$ (13 months). Both legs use semiannual compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Value the swap for the utility by the bond method and by the FRA method on one curve. Then revalue it with IBOR forwards of $4.20\%$ and $4.35\%$, treating the zero curve as OIS.
>>The discount factors are $e^{-0.036/12} = 0.997004$, $e^{-0.0375 \times 7/12} = 0.978363$, and $e^{-0.0385 \times 13/12} = 0.959149$. Every value below carries full precision. Recomputing from the rounded factors and rates shown can differ by up to about $\$15$.
>>
>>*Bond method.* The fixed coupon is $25{,}000{,}000 \times 0.033 \times 0.5 = \$412{,}500$, and the fixed bond is worth
>>$$B_{\text{fix}} = 412{,}500\,(0.997004 + 0.978363 + 0.959149) + 25{,}000{,}000 \times 0.959149 = \$25{,}189{,}225.35.$$
>>The next floating coupon is already set at $25{,}000{,}000 \times 0.039 \times 0.5 = \$487{,}500$. On one curve the FRN is worth par right after that payment. So the FRN is a sure $\$25{,}487{,}500$ in one month, worth
>>$$B_{\text{fl}} = 25{,}487{,}500 \times 0.997004 = \$25{,}411{,}152.08.$$
>>The utility pays fixed, so its swap is worth $B_{\text{fl}} - B_{\text{fix}} = \$221{,}926.73$.
>>
>>*FRA method.* The forward rates are $(0.997004/0.978363 - 1)/0.5 = 3.8109\%$ for months 1 to 7 and $(0.978363/0.959149 - 1)/0.5 = 4.0063\%$ for months 7 to 13. The first period uses the $3.90\%$ already set. The utility's net receipts $L(R - 0.033)(0.5)$ are $\$75{,}000.00$, $\$63{,}856.47$, and $\$88{,}283.02$. Their present values are $\$74{,}775.34$, $\$62{,}474.78$, and $\$84{,}676.61$, which sum to $\$221{,}926.73$. The two methods agree.
>>
>>*Two curves.* With IBOR forwards of $4.20\%$ and $4.35\%$, the later net receipts become $\$112{,}500$ and $\$131{,}250$. Discounted with OIS, they are worth $\$110{,}065.78$ and $\$125{,}888.37$. Adding the first period's $\$74{,}775.34$ gives $\$310{,}729.49$ to the utility. The FRN is now worth $L\sum_i R_F^{i-1,i}\tau_i\text{DF}_i + L\,\text{DF}_3 = \$25{,}499{,}954.84$, and $B_{\text{fl}} - B_{\text{fix}}$ gives the same $\$310{,}729.49$. The one-curve shortcut gave $\$25{,}411{,}152.08$, so it would miss the gap between IBOR and OIS forward rates.
