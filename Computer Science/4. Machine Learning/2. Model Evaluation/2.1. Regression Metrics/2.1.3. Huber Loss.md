>[!note] Huber Loss
>The **Huber loss** (Huber, 1964) is squared error for small residuals and absolute error for large ones. For a residual $e = y - \hat y$ and a threshold $\delta > 0$, it is
>$$\ell_\delta(e) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2}e^2 & \text{if } |e| \le \delta, \\ \delta\left(|e| - \frac{1}{2}\delta\right) & \text{if } |e| > \delta. \end{cases}$$
>The threshold $\delta$ has the units of $y$. Both pieces equal $\frac{1}{2}\delta^2$ at $|e| = \delta$, and both have slope $\pm\delta$ there. The derivative is the residual clipped to $[-\delta, \delta]$:
>$$\psi_\delta(e) = \ell_\delta'(e) = \max\big(-\delta, \min(e, \delta)\big).$$
>Here $\psi_\delta(e)$ is the pull that one point exerts on the fit. Huber loss has seven headline facts:
>
>- **Limits**: as $\delta \to \infty$ the loss becomes $\frac{1}{2}e^2$, half the [[2.1.1. Mean Squared Error|squared error]]. As $\delta \to 0$, $\ell_\delta(e)/\delta$ tends to $|e|$, the [[2.1.2. Mean Absolute Error|absolute error]].
>- **Smoothness**: the loss is convex with a continuous first derivative. Its second derivative jumps from $1$ to $0$ at $|e| = \delta$.
>- **Minimizer**: the best constant $c$ solves $\sum_i \psi_\delta(y_i - c) = 0$. It tends to a median as $\delta \to 0$ and equals the mean once $\delta$ exceeds every $|y_i - \bar y|$.
>- **Choosing $\delta$**: $\delta = 1.345\,\sigma$ gives $95\%$ asymptotic efficiency under Gaussian noise with standard deviation $\sigma$. In practice $\hat\sigma = \mathrm{MAD}/0.6745$, where MAD is the median absolute deviation of the residuals.
>- **Fitting**: no closed form exists. Iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) with weights $w_i = \min(1, \delta/|e_i|)$ or gradient descent solves the convex problem.
>- **Smooth L1**: deep learning libraries define $\mathrm{smooth}_{L1}(e) = \ell_\beta(e)/\beta$. It equals $0.5e^2/\beta$ for $|e| < \beta$ and $|e| - 0.5\beta$ otherwise. Fast R-CNN (Girshick, 2015) uses it with $\beta = 1$ for bounding-box regression.
>- **Noise model**: the density proportional to $\exp(-\ell_\delta(e))$ has a Gaussian center and Laplace tails.
>
>Huber loss is the usual training loss when the noise is mostly Gaussian with occasional outliers. It combines the efficiency of [[2.1.1. Mean Squared Error|MSE]] near zero with the robustness of [[2.1.2. Mean Absolute Error|MAE]] in the tails.

# Intuition

Peter Huber designed this loss in 1964 to estimate a mean from data with a few gross errors. It treats a residual smaller than $\delta$ as ordinary noise. It treats a larger residual as a possible outlier. Ordinary residuals get the squared penalty, which uses the data efficiently. Suspicious residuals get the absolute penalty, which caps the pull of each one at $\delta$. The threshold $\delta$ separates the two groups, so it must match the scale of the typical noise.

Each point pulls on the fit with a strength equal to its clipped residual. Under squared error the pull grows without limit as the residual grows. Under absolute error the pull is always $\pm 1$, even for a point that lies almost on the fit. Huber loss keeps the proportional pull for nearby points and caps it at $\delta$ for distant ones. Near the optimum it behaves like least squares, so gradient methods settle smoothly. Far from the optimum it behaves like absolute error, so one bad point cannot dominate a step.

The animation sweeps $\delta$ between $0.5$ and $2$ and writes $r$ for the residual. The loss is quadratic between the dashed lines and linear outside them. Its derivative is the residual clipped to $[-\delta, \delta]$.

# Properties

#### *(Bounded Influence)* No single residual can move the Huber fit by more than a bounded amount.

An **M-estimator** is defined by an equation $\sum_i \psi(e_i) = 0$ for some function $\psi$. In robust statistics, the **influence function** measures how much one added point with residual $e$ moves an estimate. For an M-estimator it is proportional to $\psi(e)$. Under squared error it grows linearly in $e$ without limit. Under Huber loss it grows linearly up to $\delta$ and then stays flat. Huber regression therefore behaves like least squares that clips each residual at $\pm\delta$ before using it. The IRLS weights $w_i = \psi_\delta(e_i)/e_i = \min(1, \delta/|e_i|)$ show the same rule. Points inside the threshold keep weight $1$, and a point at $|e| = 10\delta$ gets weight $0.1$.

#### *(Where 1.345 Comes From)* The constant $1.345$ gives up $5\%$ of Gaussian efficiency in exchange for bounded influence.

An M-estimator has asymptotic variance $\mathbb{E}[\psi(\varepsilon)^2]/(n\,\mathbb{E}[\psi'(\varepsilon)]^2)$, where $\varepsilon$ is the noise. For standard Gaussian noise and $\delta = k$, $\mathbb{E}[\psi'] = 2\Phi(k) - 1$, the probability that a residual falls inside the threshold. The efficiency is the variance of the mean divided by this variance. It is $0.79$ at $k = 0.5$, $0.90$ at $k = 1$, $0.95$ at $k = 1.345$ and $0.99$ at $k = 2$. A smaller $k$ gives more robustness and less efficiency. Common robust regression software, such as R's `rlm` and statsmodels, uses $k = 1.345$ as the default.

#### *(Minimax Robustness)* Huber's estimator minimizes the worst-case variance when a fraction of the data comes from an unknown distribution.

Huber (1964) modeled the noise as $(1 - \epsilon)\,\mathcal{N}(0, 1) + \epsilon H$, where $H$ is any symmetric distribution and $\epsilon$ is the contaminated fraction. Among M-estimators of location, the Huber estimator minimizes the largest asymptotic variance over all such $H$. Its optimal threshold $k$ solves
$$\frac{2\varphi(k)}{k} - 2\Phi(-k) = \frac{\epsilon}{1 - \epsilon},$$
where $\varphi$ and $\Phi$ are the standard normal density and CDF. The solution is $k = 1.95$ for $\epsilon = 0.01$, $k = 1.40$ for $\epsilon = 0.05$ and $k = 1.14$ for $\epsilon = 0.1$. The worst-case $H$ yields the density with a Gaussian center and Laplace tails. Huber loss is the maximum-likelihood loss for that density.

#### *(Scale Must Be Estimated)* The threshold $\delta$ has units, so the fit depends on an estimate of the noise scale.

Doubling every $y_i$ with $\delta$ held fixed changes which points count as outliers. Robust regression software therefore sets $\delta = k\hat\sigma$ with a scale estimate $\hat\sigma$. The sample standard deviation is a poor choice, since the outliers inflate it and raise $\delta$. The median absolute deviation (MAD) resists them. For Gaussian data $\mathrm{MAD} \approx 0.6745\,\sigma$, because $0.6745$ is the $75$th percentile of the standard normal. Hence $\hat\sigma = \mathrm{MAD}/0.6745 \approx 1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}$. Fitting procedures alternate between updating the coefficients and updating $\hat\sigma$ from the current residuals. Deep learning code usually standardizes the targets and fixes $\delta$ by hand.

#### *(Smooth L1 in Deep Learning)* Smooth L1 loss is a rescaled Huber loss whose gradient never exceeds $1$ in size.

Fast R-CNN predicts bounding-box offsets whose errors can be large early in training. Girshick (2015) reports that L2 loss then needs careful tuning of the learning rate to avoid exploding gradients. Smooth L1 has gradient $e/\beta$ inside the threshold and $\pm 1$ outside. Its gradient therefore never exceeds $1$ in size, which acts like gradient clipping inside the loss. PyTorch offers `HuberLoss(delta)` and `SmoothL1Loss(beta)`, and the two differ by the factor $\beta$. Near zero, the quadratic piece makes small corrections for small errors, where L1 loss would take full-size steps.

#### *(Bad Inputs Still Hurt)* Huber loss caps the pull of large residuals and leaves the pull of extreme inputs uncapped.

For a linear model, point $i$ contributes $-\psi_\delta(e_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i$ to the gradient with respect to $\boldsymbol\beta$. The factor $\psi_\delta(e_i)$ is capped at $\delta$. The factor $\mathbf{x}_i$ has no cap. A point with an extreme input can tilt the fit until its own residual is small. It then counts as an ordinary point. Huber regression therefore has the same breakdown point $1/n$ as least squares when inputs can be corrupted. Weighted variants, called GM-estimators, also down-weight extreme inputs. When outliers are a large fraction of the data, [[4.4.1. RANSAC]] is the safer method.

# Derivation

Huber loss follows from a model in which each point may carry an outlier shift. Write the residual as $e = z + r$, where $z$ is a gross error and $r$ is ordinary noise. Charge $\frac{1}{2}r^2$ for the noise and $\delta|z|$ for the shift, and pick the cheapest $z$:
$$\ell_\delta(e) = \min_{z \in \mathbb{R}} \left[\tfrac{1}{2}(e - z)^2 + \delta|z|\right].$$
The inner problem is a one-dimensional [[3.2.2. Lasso|lasso]]. Its solution is soft-thresholding, $z^* = \operatorname{sign}(e)\max(|e| - \delta, 0)$. If $|e| \le \delta$, then $z^* = 0$ and the cost is $\frac{1}{2}e^2$. If $|e| > \delta$, then $e - z^* = \pm\delta$ and the cost is
$$\tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2 + \delta(|e| - \delta) = \delta\left(|e| - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta\right).$$
Both cases match the definition. Differentiating the minimum value with respect to $e$ at the optimal $z^*$ gives $\ell_\delta'(e) = e - z^* = \psi_\delta(e)$, the clipped residual. Fitting a model with Huber loss therefore equals fitting least squares jointly with a sparse vector of outlier shifts $z_i$. Only points whose residual exceeds $\delta$ receive a nonzero shift.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Four Sensor Residuals
>A temperature model has residuals $e = [0.4, -0.8, 2.5, -6.0]$ degrees Celsius on four test readings. Use $\delta = 1$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the Huber loss of each residual and compare it with half the squared error. What share of the total pull comes from the worst reading?
>>The first two residuals lie inside the threshold, so their losses are $\frac{1}{2}(0.4)^2 = 0.08$ and $\frac{1}{2}(0.8)^2 = 0.32$. The other two lie outside, so their losses are $1 \cdot (2.5 - 0.5) = 2.0$ and $1 \cdot (6.0 - 0.5) = 5.5$. The Huber total is $7.9$. Half the squared error gives $0.08$, $0.32$, $3.125$ and $18$, a total of $21.525$. The pulls under Huber loss are $\psi_1(e) = [0.4, -0.8, 1, -1]$, with total size $3.2$, so the worst reading supplies $31\%$. Under squared error the pulls are the residuals themselves, with total size $9.7$, so the worst reading supplies $62\%$.

>[!example] (Medium) A Robust Typical Delivery Time
>Six deliveries on one route took $[22, 24, 25, 27, 28, 60]$ minutes. The $60$-minute trip hit a road closure. Use $\delta = 5$ minutes.
>
>>[!example]- Find the Huber estimate of the typical time and compare it with the mean and the median.
>>Guess that only the $60$-minute trip lies outside the threshold. Its pull is then $+5$, and the equation $\sum_i \psi_5(y_i - c) = 0$ becomes $(22 + 24 + 25 + 27 + 28 - 5c) + 5 = 0$. The solution is $c = 131/5 = 26.2$. The check confirms the guess. The other residuals are $-4.2, -2.2, -1.2, 0.8, 1.8$, all inside $\pm 5$, and the outlier's residual is $33.8$. The mean is $31.0$ and the median is $26.0$. The Huber estimate depends on $\delta$. It equals the median $26.0$ for $1 \le \delta \le 4$. Between $\delta = 4$ and $\delta = 29$ it equals $(126 + \delta)/5$, for example $27.2$ at $\delta = 10$. Here $126$ is the sum of the five ordinary trips. From $\delta = 29$ on, no residual exceeds the threshold, and the estimate is the mean $31.0$.

>[!example] (Hard) Setting the Threshold From the Data
>A delivery-time model has residuals $e = [-2.6, 1.2, 0.4, -0.9, 2.2, -1.5, 0.7, 14.0]$ minutes on eight training trips. The last trip was delayed by a road closure.
>
>>[!example]- Set $\delta = 1.345\,\hat\sigma$ with $\hat\sigma$ from the MAD, and find the IRLS weights. What happens if $\hat\sigma$ is the sample standard deviation instead?
>>The median residual is $(0.4 + 0.7)/2 = 0.55$. The absolute deviations from it, sorted, are $0.15, 0.15, 0.65, 1.45, 1.65, 2.05, 3.15, 13.45$. Their median is $\mathrm{MAD} = (1.45 + 1.65)/2 = 1.55$. Then $\hat\sigma = 1.55/0.6745 = 2.30$ and $\delta = 1.345 \cdot 2.30 = 3.09$ minutes. Only the residual $14.0$ exceeds $\delta$. Its weight is $3.09/14.0 = 0.22$, and every other trip keeps weight $1$. The sample standard deviation is $5.21$ minutes, against $1.67$ without the delayed trip. It gives $\delta = 7.01$ and a weight of $0.50$ for the delayed trip. The outlier inflates its own threshold and keeps more than twice the weight.
