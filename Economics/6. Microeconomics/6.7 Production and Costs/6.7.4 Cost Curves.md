>[!note] Cost Curves
>**Cost curves** show how the minimal cost of production changes with output $y$. They start from the cost function $c(w, r, y)$ of [[6.7.3 Cost Minimization]]. Input prices are held fixed, so we write $c(y)$.
>- **Fixed cost** $F$ is the part of cost that does not change with $y$ for $y > 0$, such as a **setup cost**. **Variable cost** is $VC(y) = c(y) - F$. A cost function from cost minimization with all inputs variable has $F = 0$.
>- **Marginal cost** $MC(y) = \partial c(w, r, y)/\partial y = VC'(y)$ is the cost of one more unit. **Average cost** $AC(y) = c(y)/y$ is the cost per unit. It splits into average fixed cost $AFC = F/y$ and average variable cost $AVC = VC(y)/y$.
>- In the graph of $c(y)$, $AC(y)$ is the slope of the ray from the origin to $(y, c(y))$, and $MC(y)$ is the slope of the tangent at that point.
>- Marginal cost decides the direction of average cost:
>$$\frac{d\,AC(y)}{dy} = \frac{MC(y) - AC(y)}{y}.$$
>- AC falls where $MC < AC$ and rises where $MC > AC$. At an interior minimum of AC, $MC = AC$. The same rule links MC and AVC.
>- A firm makes a positive profit at output $y$ exactly when $AC(y) < p$, since $\pi/y = p - AC(y)$. At an output with $p = MC$, the profit per unit is $MC - AC$.
>- One input, $y = L^{\alpha}$, wage $w$:
>$$c(y) = w\,y^{1/\alpha}, \qquad MC(y) = \frac{1}{\alpha}\,w\,y^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}, \qquad AC(y) = w\,y^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}, \qquad MC = \frac{AC}{\alpha}.$$
>- If $\alpha > 1$ (IRS), MC and AC fall and $MC < AC$. If $\alpha = 1$ (CRS), $MC = AC = w$. If $\alpha < 1$ (DRS), MC and AC rise and $MC > AC$.
>- Cobb-Douglas $y = AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta}$ gives $c(y) = B\,(y/A)^{1/(\alpha+\beta)}$, where $B$ depends on $w$, $r$, $\alpha$, and $\beta$ but not on $y$, and $MC = AC/(\alpha+\beta)$. Falling AC, called **economies of scale**, goes with IRS. Rising AC goes with DRS.
>- A setup cost adds $F/y$ to AC and leaves MC unchanged. It turns a rising AC into a U shape. A **non-convex technology**, whose marginal product first rises and then falls, gives a U-shaped MC.
>- Uses: supply is the MC curve above min AC in [[6.7.5 Profit Maximization and Supply]], and free entry drives price to min AC in [[6.8.7 Long-Run Equilibrium]].

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The names variable cost, AVC, and AFC, the order of the three minima, the formula $MC = w/f'(L)$, and the rule for returns to scale of any degree $s$ are extensions. Short-run cost curves with capital held fixed were not covered. In the lecture, the short run means a fixed number of firms, as in [[6.8.2 Short-Run Market Equilibrium]].

# Intuition

Cost minimization finds the cheapest way to produce each output level. The cost function collects these answers. A firm that decides how much to sell needs two numbers from it. Marginal cost tells it what one more unit adds to the bill. The firm compares it with the price to decide how far to expand. Average cost tells it what each unit costs on average. The firm compares it with the price to decide whether producing pays at all. The two numbers can differ a lot. A brewery with a large setup cost can have a low cost per extra barrel and a high cost per barrel on average.

The diagram plots $c(y) = 32 + y^3 - 6y^2 + 15y$ from the Medium example. At $y = 2$ the ray from the origin is steep, with $AC = 23$, and the tangent is almost flat, with $MC = 3$. As $y$ grows, the ray to the curve gets flatter until it touches the curve at $y = 4$. There the ray and the tangent are the same line, so $MC = AC = 15$. No ray from the origin to the curve is flatter, so $y = 4$ minimizes AC.

# Properties

#### *(MC Versus AC)* A unit that costs less than the current average lowers the average, and a unit that costs more raises it.

The quotient rule gives $\frac{d}{dy}\frac{c(y)}{y} = \frac{c'_y(y)\,y - c(y)}{y^2} = \frac{MC - AC}{y}$. A grade average works the same way: a new score below the average lowers it. An interior minimum of AC needs $d\,AC/dy = 0$, so $MC = AC$ there. Since $F$ has zero derivative, $VC'(y) = MC$ as well, and the same identity with $VC$ in place of $c$ shows that MC crosses AVC at the minimum of AVC. The rule needs an interior minimum. Under IRS, AC falls at every $y$ and has no minimum. Under CRS, $MC = AC$ at every $y$.

#### *(Scale and AC Shape)* For a technology with returns to scale of degree $s$, cost grows like $y^{1/s}$ and $MC/AC = 1/s$.

Suppose $f(tL, tK) = t^s f(L, K)$ for all $t > 0$. Scaling the cheapest input bundle for $y$ by $t$ produces $t^s y$ at cost $t\,c(y)$. No cheaper bundle for $t^s y$ exists. If one did, scaling it by $1/t$ would produce $y$ for less than $c(y)$. So $c(t^s y) = t\,c(y)$, and setting $y = 1$ gives $c(y) = c(1)\,y^{1/s}$. Then $AC = c(1)\,y^{1/s - 1}$, and $MC/AC = 1/s$ at every output. This ratio is the elasticity of cost with respect to output, $\varepsilon_{c,y} = MC\cdot y/c$. IRS ($s > 1$) means falling AC and $MC < AC$. CRS means flat AC equal to MC. DRS means rising AC and $MC > AC$. Cobb-Douglas has $s = \alpha + \beta$, and $y = L^{\alpha}$ has $s = \alpha$. The three panels below plot the one-input case with $w = 4$.

#### *(Fixed Costs Shift AC)* A fixed cost raises AC by $F/y$ and has no effect on MC.

The derivative of a constant is zero, so MC is the same with or without $F$. The gap $AC - AVC = F/y$ is large at small outputs and shrinks toward zero as $y$ grows. Spreading the fixed cost over more units gives economies of scale at small outputs, even when the variable part has DRS. With a rising AVC, AC becomes U-shaped: $F/y$ dominates at small $y$ and the rising AVC dominates at large $y$. The minimum of AC lies to the right of the minimum of AVC, because at that point MC must exceed AVC by $F/y$. With a U-shaped MC the order is: MC reaches its minimum first, then AVC, then AC. With a setup cost and constant variable cost per unit $b$, $c(y) = F + by$ and $AC = b + F/y$ falls at every output, so AC has no minimum. A setup cost is paid only if the firm produces, so $c(0) = 0$ and $c(y) = F + VC(y)$ for $y > 0$. [[6.7.5 Profit Maximization and Supply]] uses this to compare the price with min AC.

#### *(Cost Mirrors Productivity)* With one input, $MC = w/f'(L)$ and $AC = w/(y/L)$.

To produce $y$ the firm hires $L = f^{-1}(y)$, so $c(y) = w f^{-1}(y)$. The inverse function rule gives $MC = w/f'(L)$. Average cost is $wL/y$, the wage divided by the average product $y/L$. Diminishing marginal product therefore means rising MC. A non-convex technology has a marginal product that first rises, as workers specialize, and then falls. Its MC first falls and then rises. In the graph of $c(y)$ this is a curve that is first concave and then [[2.2 Convex Functions|convex]], as in the Intuition diagram.

# Derivation

We want the cost curves for $y = L^{\alpha}$ and for Cobb-Douglas. With one input, cost minimization has no choice to make: the firm needs $L^* = y^{1/\alpha}$, so $c(w, y) = wL^*(w, y) = w\,y^{1/\alpha}$. Differentiating gives $MC = \frac{1}{\alpha}\,w\,y^{1/\alpha - 1} = \frac{1}{\alpha}\,w\,y^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}$. Dividing $c$ by $y$ gives $AC = w\,y^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}$. The exponent $(1-\alpha)/\alpha$ is negative for $\alpha > 1$, zero for $\alpha = 1$, and positive for $\alpha < 1$, which gives the three shapes.

For $y = AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta}$, the conditional input demands of [[6.7.3 Cost Minimization]] give
$$c(w, r, y) = \left(\frac{y}{A}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}} B, \qquad B = w\left(\frac{w}{r}\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}} + r\left(\frac{w}{r}\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}.$$
Then $MC = \frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}\,\frac{B}{A}\left(\frac{y}{A}\right)^{\frac{1-(\alpha+\beta)}{\alpha+\beta}}$ and $AC = \frac{B}{A}\left(\frac{y}{A}\right)^{\frac{1-(\alpha+\beta)}{\alpha+\beta}}$, so $MC = AC/(\alpha+\beta)$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) One Input, Three Technologies
>A bakery uses only labor at wage $w = 4$. Its output is $y = L^{\alpha}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find $c(y)$, MC, and AC for $\alpha = 2$, $\alpha = 1$, and $\alpha = 1/2$. Which case has economies of scale?
>>To make $y$ the bakery needs $L = y^{1/\alpha}$, so $c(y) = 4y^{1/\alpha}$. For $\alpha = 2$, $c = 4\sqrt{y}$, $MC = 2/\sqrt{y}$, and $AC = 4/\sqrt{y}$. At $y = 4$, $MC = 1$ and $AC = 2$. Both fall with $y$ and MC lies below AC, so this case has economies of scale. For $\alpha = 1$, $c = 4y$ and $MC = AC = 4$ at every output. For $\alpha = 1/2$, $c = 4y^2$, $MC = 8y$, and $AC = 4y$. Both rise. In all three cases $MC/AC = 1/\alpha$. Only the last case has rising MC, which [[6.7.5 Profit Maximization and Supply]] needs for an interior optimum.

>[!example] (Medium) A Print Shop With a Lease
>A print shop pays a lease of $\$32$ per day and has variable cost $y^3 - 6y^2 + 15y$, where $y$ is thousands of pages.
>
>>[!example]- Find MC, AVC, AFC, and AC. Find the minima of MC, AVC, and AC, and check that MC passes through the last two.
>>Here $F = 32$, $MC = 3y^2 - 12y + 15$, $AVC = y^2 - 6y + 15$, $AFC = 32/y$, and $AC = y^2 - 6y + 15 + 32/y$. MC is smallest where $6y - 12 = 0$, at $y = 2$ with $MC = 3$. AVC is smallest where $2y - 6 = 0$, at $y = 3$ with $AVC = 6$, and $MC(3) = 27 - 36 + 15 = 6$. AC is smallest where $2y - 6 - 32/y^2 = 0$, that is $y^3 - 3y^2 - 16 = (y - 4)(y^2 + y + 4) = 0$. The quadratic has no real root, so $y = 4$, with $AC = 16 - 24 + 15 + 8 = 15$ and $MC(4) = 48 - 48 + 15 = 15$. The minima come in the order $2 < 3 < 4$. As a check of the identity at $y = 1$: $AC(1) = 42$, $MC(1) = 6$, and $AC'(1) = 2 - 6 - 32 = -36 = (6 - 42)/1$.

>[!example] (Hard) The Workers Behind the Print Shop
>The print shop of the Medium example pays $w = 1$ per worker-hour, so its variable cost $L(y) = y^3 - 6y^2 + 15y$ is also the number of hours needed to print $y$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the marginal product and the average product of labor as functions of $y$. Where is each largest? Link the answers to the cost curves.
>>The hours needed are the inverse of the production function, so the marginal product is $f'(L) = 1/L'(y) = 1/(3y^2 - 12y + 15)$. The average product is $y/L = 1/(y^2 - 6y + 15)$. The marginal product is $1/15$ at the first hour, rises to its peak of $1/3$ at $y = 2$ ($L = 14$ hours), and then falls, to $1/15$ again at $y = 4$. This is a non-convex technology: workers first help each other, then crowd each other. The average product peaks at $y = 3$ ($L = 18$), where it equals $1/6$, and the marginal product there is also $1/(27 - 36 + 15) = 1/6$. Since $MC = w/f'(L)$ and $AVC = w/(y/L)$, the peak of the marginal product is the minimum of MC, $3$ at $y = 2$. The peak of the average product is the minimum of AVC, $6$ at $y = 3$.
