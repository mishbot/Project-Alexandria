>[!note] JPEG Compression
>**JPEG** is the standard lossy compression method for photographs. It rests on three observations. Low-frequency content dominates images of the real world. The human visual system is less sensitive to detail in chromaticity than in luminance. It is also less sensitive to error at high frequencies. JPEG therefore puts its errors in the chroma channels and at high frequencies.
>
>The encoder first converts RGB to **Y'CbCr**. The luma $Y'$ is the lightness, with the prime marking gamma encoding. The chroma channels $C_b$ and $C_r$ measure the blue-yellow and red-green difference from gray. The encoder then subsamples the chroma. In **4:2:2**, $C_b$ and $C_r$ keep half the horizontal resolution, so $1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 2$ of every $3$ values remain, a fraction of $2/3$. In **4:2:0**, they keep half the resolution in both directions, so $1 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = 1.5$ of every $3$ remain, a fraction of $1/2$. The luma always stays at full resolution.
>
>Each channel is then cut into $8 \times 8$ blocks, and the **discrete cosine transform** (DCT) writes each block as a weighted sum of $64$ basis patterns,
>$$\text{basis}[i, j](x, y) = \cos\Big[\pi\, \frac{i}{N}\Big(x + \frac12\Big)\Big] \cos\Big[\pi\, \frac{j}{N}\Big(y + \frac12\Big)\Big], \qquad N = 8.$$
>Here $(x, y)$ is a pixel position in the block, with $x$ counting rows, and $i, j, x, y \in \{0, \dots, 7\}$. The indices $i$ and $j$ count roughly how many half-periods the pattern makes down and across the block. The weight of the constant pattern $\text{basis}[0, 0]$, the DC coefficient, is proportional to the block's average. Under 4:2:0 an $8 \times 8$ chroma block covers $16 \times 16$ image pixels. Each DCT coefficient $F[i, j]$ is then **quantized** with a quantization matrix $Q$:
>$$F_Q[i, j] = \operatorname{round}\big(F[i, j] \,/\, Q[i, j]\big).$$
>The entries of $Q$ grow toward high frequencies, so most high-frequency coefficients become $0$. The quality setting in a photo app changes $Q$, and lower quality means larger entries. Last, the encoder reorders each block's coefficients in a zigzag from low to high frequency. It then run-length encodes the runs of zeros and Huffman encodes the nonzero values. These last steps are lossless. Information is lost in exactly two places, chroma subsampling and quantization. The decoder runs the steps backward and multiplies each $F_Q[i, j]$ by $Q[i, j]$ before the inverse DCT. The lecture shows one photo saved at $89$ kB and at $12$ kB.

# Intuition

A $12$-megapixel photo stored as 8-bit RGB takes $36$ MB. Most of those bytes describe differences that a viewer cannot see. JPEG is named after the Joint Photographic Experts Group, the committee that wrote the standard. The lecture uses it as an example of a broader theme, in which visual computing systems gain efficiency by exploiting human perception. Color detail is cheap to discard, so it is subsampled. Fine detail is cheap to approximate, so it is rounded coarsely.

The DCT step is a [[2.8b Change of Basis|change of basis]], like the [[2.2. Fourier Transform in Images|Fourier transform]]. A smooth block is described almost entirely by a few low-frequency patterns. Quantization rounds the weights of all $64$ patterns, more coarsely for patterns the eye notices less. Most weights become $0$, and long runs of zeros cost almost nothing to store.

# Properties

#### *(Where Information Is Lost)* The DCT and the entropy coding are invertible, while rounding and chroma subsampling are not.

The DCT basis is orthogonal, so the transform is an invertible change of coordinates ([[2.9c Matrix Orthonormality]]). It loses nothing apart from floating-point rounding. Quantization maps every value within $Q[i, j]/2$ of a multiple of $Q[i, j]$ to the same integer. After the decoder multiplies back by $Q[i, j]$, each coefficient is off by at most $Q[i, j]/2$. Chroma subsampling discards half or three quarters of the chroma samples, and the decoder can only [[2.3. Resampling|upsample]] what remains. Zigzag reordering, run-length coding and Huffman coding are exactly reversible.

#### *(Zigzag Order)* Reading the block diagonal by diagonal gathers the zeros into one long run at the end.

After quantization the nonzero coefficients cluster in the top-left corner, at low frequencies. The zigzag visits coefficients in order of increasing $i + j$, which is roughly increasing frequency. The figure shades the $20$ nonzero coefficients of the lecture's example block and draws the zigzag. The solid part ends at the last nonzero coefficient, the $26$th visited. The remaining $38$ coefficients are all $0$ and need only a single end-of-block code.

#### *(Blocking and Ringing)* At low quality, the block structure and the missing high frequencies become visible.

Each $8 \times 8$ block is quantized on its own. At strong compression many blocks keep little more than their DC coefficient, so they turn into flat tiles. Neighboring tiles get slightly different levels, and the $8 \times 8$ grid shows. This artifact is called **blocking**. A sharp edge needs many high-frequency cosines to stay sharp. When quantization removes them, ripples appear beside the edge, an artifact called **ringing**. Text, line art and other images with hard edges show both artifacts most strongly.

#### *(Why Cosines)* The DCT uses real cosines that treat each block as if it were mirrored at its borders.

The Fourier transform treats a block as one period of a repeating pattern. Its left edge then meets its right edge, which usually creates a false jump and spreads energy into high frequencies. The cosine basis corresponds to reflecting the block at its borders, which creates no jump. A smooth block then concentrates its energy in a few low-frequency coefficients, which is what quantization needs. The coefficients are also real numbers, with no separate phase to store.

#### *(Chroma Block Footprint)* Under 4:2:0, a $16 \times 16$ pixel region is coded as six $8 \times 8$ blocks.

The region contains four $8 \times 8$ blocks of $Y'$. Its chroma, at half resolution in each direction, fills one $8 \times 8$ block of $C_b$ and one of $C_r$. That is $6 \times 64 = 384$ values, against $16 \times 16 \times 3 = 768$ values in RGB. The ratio is $1/2$, the 4:2:0 fraction. A color error in chroma can therefore spread over $16 \times 16$ pixels, while luma errors stay within $8 \times 8$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Counting Values in a Phone Photo
>A camera takes a $4000 \times 3000$ photo with 8-bit RGB values.
>
>>[!example]- How many values does it hold in RGB, after 4:2:2 and after 4:2:0? How many $8 \times 8$ blocks does the encoder transform under 4:2:0?
>>The image has $12{,}000{,}000$ pixels, so RGB holds $36{,}000{,}000$ values. Under 4:2:2 it keeps $2/3$ of them, $24{,}000{,}000$. Under 4:2:0 it keeps $1/2$, $18{,}000{,}000$.
>>
>>The luma has $12{,}000{,}000 / 64 = 187{,}500$ blocks. Under 4:2:0 each chroma channel is $2000 \times 1500$, which gives $46{,}875$ blocks per channel. The total is $187{,}500 + 2 \times 46{,}875 = 281{,}250$ blocks. Subsampling alone only halves the data. Most of the saving comes later, when quantization turns most coefficients into zeros.

>[!example] (Medium) Quantizing the Lecture's Block
>The lecture's example block has DCT coefficients $F[0,0] = -415$, $F[2,2] = 77$, $F[3,2] = 34$ and $F[7,7] = 2$ (rows and columns counted from $0$). The quantization matrix has $Q[0,0] = 16$, $Q[2,2] = 16$, $Q[3,2] = 22$ and $Q[7,7] = 99$.
>
>>[!example]- Quantize these four coefficients, decode them, and check the error bound. What does the DC value say about the block?
>>Dividing and rounding gives $-415/16 = -25.94 \to -26$, $\ 77/16 = 4.81 \to 5$, $\ 34/22 = 1.55 \to 2$, and $2/99 = 0.02 \to 0$. Decoding multiplies back: $-416$, $80$, $44$ and $0$. The errors are $1$, $3$, $10$ and $2$. Each stays within $Q[i,j]/2$, which is $8$, $8$, $11$ and $49.5$. The large divisors at high frequencies allow large errors exactly where the eye is least sensitive. Across the whole block, $20$ of the $64$ quantized coefficients are nonzero.
>>
>>Standard JPEG subtracts $128$ from each 8-bit value before the DCT, and with its scaling the DC coefficient equals $8$ times the block's mean. A DC of $-415$ therefore means a mean of about $128 - 415/8 \approx 76$, a darker-than-middle gray. The slide's coefficients are rounded integers, so a few divisions give exact ties, such as $-20/40 = -0.5$. The slide rounds those away from zero.

>[!example] (Hard) Zigzag and Run-Length Coding
>The lecture's quantized block has these nonzero rows, with every other entry $0$:
>$$\begin{aligned} &\text{row } 0: -26, -3, -6, 2, 2, -1, 0, 0 \qquad &&\text{row } 1: 0, -2, -4, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0 \\ &\text{row } 2: -3, 1, 5, -1, -1, 0, 0, 0 \qquad &&\text{row } 3: -4, 1, 2, -1, 0, 0, 0, 0 \\ &\text{row } 4: 1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0 && \end{aligned}$$
>
>>[!example]- List the values in zigzag order up to the last nonzero one. How many (zero-run, value) pairs describe the AC coefficients? Compare with reading the block row by row.
>>The zigzag starts at $(0,0)$, moves right to $(0,1)$, then down-left to $(1,0)$, and continues diagonal by diagonal. The sequence is
>>$$-26,\ -3,\ 0,\ -3,\ -2,\ -6,\ 2,\ -4,\ 1,\ -4,\ 1,\ 1,\ 5,\ 1,\ 2,\ -1,\ 1,\ -1,\ 2,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ -1,\ -1,$$
>>followed by $38$ zeros. The first value is the DC coefficient. The $19$ nonzero AC values each become one (zero-run, value) pair. Only two pairs have a nonzero run: $(1, -3)$ and $(5, -1)$. One end-of-block code covers the final $38$ zeros.
>>
>>Row-by-row reading also has $19$ nonzero AC values, but its last nonzero value comes $33$rd. The final run shrinks to $31$ zeros, and four interior runs of $3$, $3$, $3$ and $4$ zeros appear. The zigzag keeps the same values together because coefficients with similar $i + j$ have similar frequency and similar size.
