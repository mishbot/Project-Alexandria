>[!note] Rivest–Shamir–Adleman (RSA) Cryptosystem
>**RSA** is a public-key scheme that turns a pair of **large primes** into a trapdoor for modular exponentiation.
>
>Let $p$ and $q$ be distinct 512-bit (or larger) primes that are kept secret and set $N=pq$. Also, let $e$ be any number that is relatively prime to $\varphi(N)=(p-1)(q-1)$, such that  $\gcd(e,\varphi(N))=1$. Then the **public key** is the tuple $(N, e)$, and the **private key** is the inverse $d\equiv e^{-1}\pmod{\varphi(N)}$. Note that $d$ is guaranteed to exist since $p$ and $q$ are coprime.
>
>With the keys in place, RSA communication reduces to two mirror-image steps:
>- The sender **encrypts** a message $0\le x \le N-1$ as the ciphertext $E(x)\equiv x^{e}\pmod N$.
>- The receiver **decrypts** $y=E(x)$ by computing $D(y) \equiv y^d \pmod{N}$. 
>
>The security of RSA hinges upon the fact that $p$ and $q$ are kept private by the correspondents. Given only $N, e$, and $y \equiv x^e \pmod{N}$, there is no efficient algorithm to compute $d \pmod{\varphi(N)}$.
# Correctness
The premise of RSA boils down to one simple fact:
- For every possible message $x \in \{ 0, 1, \ldots, N-1 \}$, it holds that $D(E(x)) \equiv x \pmod{N}$.
We will now go ahead and prove this fact using Fermat's Little Theorem, described in 4.2. We restate it here for convenience:
- **Fermat' Little Theorem**: For all primes $p$, $a^{p-1} \pmod{p} \equiv 1$ if $a  \ne 0$, and $a^p \equiv a \pmod{p}$ for all $a$.

# Examples
>[!example] (Very Easy) RSA Warm-Up
>Consider an RSA scheme with modulus $N = pq$, where $p$ and $q$ are distinct prime numbers larger than $3$.
>>[!example]- What is wrong with using the exponent $e = 2$ in an RSA public key?
>>A value $e=2$ will not be relatively prime to $(p-1)(q-1)$, which is necessarily even for all $p,q > 3$, and therefore an inverse $d$ will not exist for the private key.
>
>Now suppose that $p= 5$, $q= 17$, and $e = 3$. 
>>[!example]- What is the public key and private key?
>>We have $N=5 \cdot 17=85$. Then, the public key is $(85, 3)$, whereas we find private key using a quick application of EGCD, such that $d \equiv 43 \pmod{64}$. 
>
>>[!example]- Alice wants to send a message $x = 10$ to Bob. What is the encrypted message $E(x)$ she sends using the public key?
>>We have that $E(x)\equiv10^{3}\equiv65 \pmod{85}$.
>
>>[!example]- Suppose Bob receives the message $y= 19$ from Alice. What equation would he use to decrypt the message? What is the decrypted message?
>>He would use $D(y) \equiv 19^{43} \equiv 59 \pmod{85}$.
>

>[!example] (Easy) RSA Security with Multiple Keys
>A secret society repeatedly sends the same secret $x$ (with $0\le x<N_i$) using RSA keys $(N_i,e)$ that all share the _same_ exponent $e$ but have pair-wise distinct moduli $N_i$. Eve hears every ciphertext and the public keys; she knows the RSA setup but _cannot_ factor large numbers.
>
>Eve observes two keys $(p_1q_1,7)$ and $(p_1q_2,7)$ and their own distinct messages $x_{1}$ and $x_{2}$, where $p_1,q_1,q_2$ are 1024-bit primes.
>>[!example]- Can this information alone let her recover $x$?
>>Since Eve knows that $p_{1}$ is a factor in both $N_{1}$ and $N_{2}$, she computes $\gcd(N_{1}, N_{2})=p_{1}$. Then she divides both moduli by $p_{1}$, such that $N_{1}/p_{1}=q_{1}$ and $N_{2} / p_{1} = q_{2}$. Knowing the primes, she can construct the private keys $d_{1}$ and $d_{2}$ and recovers the secret.
>
>The secret society sniffs Eve out and switches to a new word and to three keys $(p_1q_1,3)$, $(p_2q_2,3)$, $(p_3q_3,3)$ with six distinct primes and three distinct messages $x_{i}$. 
>>[!example]- Explain briefly why Eve’s previous method no longer works.
>>For all pairs ($N_{i}, N_{j}$) such that $i \neq j$, $\gcd(N_{i}, N_{j}) = 1$ since all primes are distinct and therefore no pair shares a common factor. As such, Eve's method no longer works.
>
>Now, assume the secret $x$ stays the same and the public exponent is still $e = 3$.
>>[!example]- The group keeps using the three public keys from part (b) but sends only that single repeated ciphertext. Explain how Eve can recover $x$.
>Eve observes the following system of equations:
>$$\begin{cases} E_{1}(x) \equiv x^3 \pmod{p_{1}q_{1}} \\ E_{2}(x) \equiv x^3 \pmod{p_{2}q_{2}} \\ E_{3}(x) \equiv x^3 \pmod{p_{3}q_{3}}. \end{cases}$$
>She must find $N_{i}^{-1}$, and use CRT to compute $x^3 \pmod{N_{1}N_{2}N_{3}}$. Since $x^3 < \min(N_{i})$, then $x^3 = x^3 \pmod{N_{1}N_{2}N_{3}}$, and therefore she can simply take the cube root to recover $x$.

>[!example] (Medium) RSA for Concert Tickets
>Alice’s concert ticket number is an integer $m \in [0, 100]$. She wishes to tell Bob this number over an insecure channel that Eve can monitor, but she does not want Eve to learn $m$.
>
>Bob first announces his RSA public key, which consists of a 512-bit modulus $N = pq$ and an exponent $e$. Alice encrypts her ticket number $m$ with $(N,e)$ and transmits the resulting ciphertext to Bob. Eve sees the encrypted message, and she is able to deduce $m$.
>>[!example]- Explain the method that allows Eve to recover $m$ under these conditions.
>>Eve uses brute force to find a number in the small interval $[0, 100]$ that, paired with the public key $(N, e)$, results in $E(x)$. 
>
>Alice now adopts a more elaborate approach. She chooses a random 256-bit integer $r$ that is coprime to $N$. She then sends Bob two separate ciphertexts, both encrypted with the same public key as before $(N,e)$. The first ciphertext is $C_{1} \equiv r^{e} \pmod{N}$, and the second ciphertext is $C_{2} \equiv (r\,m)^{e} \pmod{N}$. Eve knows the protocol in use and can observe both $C_{1}$ and $C_{2},$ but she does not know the value of $r$. 
>>[!example]- Describe how Eve can nevertheless determine the ticket number $m$.
>>Eve can define $(rm)^e \equiv C_{1}m^e \pmod{N}$. In other words, $C_{1}m^e \equiv C_{2} \pmod{N}$, and since $\gcd(C_{1}, N)=1$ there exists a modular inverse such that $m^e \equiv C_{1}^{-1}C_{2} \pmod{N}$. Then, Eve can use the brute force method explained in the previous part to deduce $m$.
