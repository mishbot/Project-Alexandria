>[!note] Electric Field of a Continuous Charge Distribution
>A **continuous charge distribution** is charge spread smoothly over a line, a surface, or a volume. It is described by a **charge density**, the charge per unit length, area, or volume:
>- **Linear charge density** $\lambda = dq/ds$, in $\text{C/m}$, where $s$ is the arc length along the line.
>- **Surface charge density** $\sigma = dq/dA$, in $\text{C/m}^2$.
>- **Volume charge density** $\rho = dq/dV$, in $\text{C/m}^3$.
>
>Each density can be constant or can vary from place to place. The field is found by the **slice-and-dice method**. Cut the distribution into pieces $dq$ small enough to act as [[3.2.1. Electric Field|point charges]]. Each piece adds a small field
>$$d\vec E = k\,\frac{dq}{r^2}\,\hat r,$$
>where $r$ is the distance from the piece to the field point and $\hat r$ points from the piece toward it. The total field is the sum of all the pieces, which becomes an integral:
>$$\vec E = \int k\,\frac{dq}{r^2}\,\hat r.$$
>The steps of the method:
>- Draw a picture. Mark one typical piece $dq$, the line from it to the field point, and its small field $d\vec E$.
>- Use [[3.2.3. Symmetry Arguments|symmetry]] to decide which components cancel. Keep only the others.
>- Write the surviving component, such as $dE_x = dE\cos\theta$, with $dE = k\,dq/r^2$ and the angle read from the right triangle in the picture.
>- Write $dq$ in terms of one variable, such as $dq = \lambda\,dy$, $dq = \lambda a\,d\theta$, or $dq = \sigma\,dA$.
>- Set the limits so that the integral covers every piece of charge exactly once.
>- Underline the integration variable. Move everything else outside the integral as a constant.
>- Integrate, then check the units and a limiting case.

# Intuition

Coulomb's law handles point charges, but real charged objects are rods, rings, plates, and spheres. The fix is to treat a large object as many small ones. Cut it into pieces so small that each piece is a point charge. The field of each piece is known. Superposition then says the total field is the sum over pieces. Making the pieces smaller and their number larger turns the sum into an integral.

The animation runs the whole method on an infinite line of charge. It dices the line, keeps the component perpendicular to the line, adds the pieces, and substitutes. The right side writes each step as an equation.

The method takes practice, and most mistakes are bookkeeping mistakes. The integrand contains several letters. They include the size of the object, the distance to the field point, and the position of the piece. Only the position of the piece changes from one piece to the next. Identify that variable and pull everything else out as a constant. The integral is then usually a standard one.

# Properties

#### *(Limits Cover All Charge)* A field integral always has limits, chosen so that every piece of charge is counted once.

An integral without limits gives an answer plus an arbitrary constant. A field equal to "this expression plus an unknown constant" answers nothing. The limits come from asking where the charge is. An infinite line needs $-\infty$ to $\infty$. A ring needs a full turn, $0$ to $2\pi$. A sphere sliced into rings needs the polar angle from $0$ to $\pi$. Missing part of the range leaves out charge. Doubling part of the range counts some charge twice.

#### *(Slice Into Known Shapes)* Cutting an object into rings or strips whose field is already known avoids double integrals.

A charged sphere can be cut into point-like patches, but that needs a double integral. Cutting it into thin rings instead uses the [[3.2.4b Ring of Charge|ring result]] for each slice. A single integral over the rings remains. The same idea cuts a disk into rings or a plane into strips. The hard part is translating the variables of the known result to the slice. The Hard example in [[3.2.4b Ring of Charge]] slices a sphere this way. With a well-chosen slice, a single integral is enough for every shape in these notes.

#### *(Substitutions Keep Units)* A substitution must keep both sides in the same units.

The integral over an infinite line contains $(y^2 + R^2)^{-3/2}$. The substitution $y = R\tan u$ works, while $y = \tan u$ does not. A tangent has no units, and $y$ is a length. The only length in the problem is $R$, so it must multiply the tangent. This check finds the right substitution before any algebra is done.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Charge Densities
>A rod $0.30\ \text{m}$ long carries $12\ \text{nC}$ spread evenly. A square plate $0.10\ \text{m}$ on a side carries $4.0\ \text{nC}$ spread evenly over one face.
>
>>[!example]- Find $\lambda$ for the rod and $\sigma$ for the plate.
>>The rod has
>>$$\lambda = \frac{Q}{L} = \frac{12 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{C}}{0.30\ \text{m}} = 4.0 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{C/m} = 40\ \text{nC/m}.$$
>>The plate face has area $0.010\ \text{m}^2$, so
>>$$\sigma = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{C}}{0.010\ \text{m}^2} = 4.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{C/m}^2.$$

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not work out the next two shapes, the end of a rod and a semicircle. They apply the method as in Giancoli section 21.7.

>[!example] (Medium) Beyond the End of a Rod
>The rod from the previous example lies on the $x$-axis from $x = 0$ to $x = L = 0.30\ \text{m}$. Point P sits on the axis at $x = L + d$, with $d = 0.10\ \text{m}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field at P.
>>Every piece of the rod lies on the axis, so every $d\vec E$ points along $+x$. No components cancel, and none need computing. A piece at position $x$ with length $dx$ carries $dq = \lambda\,dx$ and sits $L + d - x$ from P. Its field is
>>$$dE = k\,\frac{\lambda\,dx}{(L + d - x)^2}.$$
>>The charge runs from $x = 0$ to $x = L$, and the integration variable is $x$:
>>$$E = k\lambda \int_0^L \frac{dx}{(L + d - x)^2} = k\lambda\left[\frac{1}{L + d - x}\right]_0^L = k\lambda\left(\frac{1}{d} - \frac{1}{L + d}\right) = \frac{kQ}{d\,(L + d)}.$$
>>With $Q = 12\ \text{nC}$, the field is
>>$$E = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(12 \times 10^{-9})}{(0.10)(0.40)} = 2.7 \times 10^{3}\ \text{N/C},$$
>>pointing along $+x$. When $d \gg L$, the result becomes $kQ/d^2$, the field of a point charge.

>[!example] (Hard) A Semicircle at Its Center
>Charge $Q = 5.0\ \text{nC}$ is spread evenly over a thin semicircular wire of radius $a = 0.10\ \text{m}$. The wire is the upper half of a circle centered at the origin.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field at the center.
>>Locate a piece by its angle $\theta$, measured from the $+x$ axis. The piece has arc length $ds = a\,d\theta$ and charge $dq = \lambda a\,d\theta$, with $\lambda = Q/(\pi a)$. Every piece is the same distance $a$ from the center, so
>>$$dE = k\,\frac{\lambda a\,d\theta}{a^2} = \frac{k\lambda}{a}\,d\theta.$$
>>The wire is symmetric about the $y$-axis, so the $x$-components cancel in pairs. Each $d\vec E$ points from the piece toward the center, so its $y$-component is $-dE\sin\theta$. The wire runs from $\theta = 0$ to $\theta = \pi$:
>>$$E_y = -\frac{k\lambda}{a}\int_0^{\pi} \sin\theta\,d\theta = -\frac{2k\lambda}{a} = -\frac{2kQ}{\pi a^2}.$$
>>With the numbers,
>>$$|E_y| = \frac{2(8.99 \times 10^{9})(5.0 \times 10^{-9})}{\pi (0.10)^2} = 2.9 \times 10^{3}\ \text{N/C},$$
>>pointing down, away from the wire.
