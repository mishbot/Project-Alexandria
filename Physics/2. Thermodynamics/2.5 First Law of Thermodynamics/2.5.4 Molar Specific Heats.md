>[!note] Molar Specific Heats
>The heat a gas needs per kelvin depends on the process, so a gas has more than one specific heat. Two are standard. The **molar specific heat at constant volume** $C_V$ and the **molar specific heat at constant pressure** $C_P$ are defined by
>$$Q_V = n\, C_V\, \Delta T, \qquad Q_P = n\, C_P\, \Delta T,$$
>for $n$ moles warmed by $\Delta T$ in an [[2.5.3a Isovolumetric Process|isovolumetric]] and an [[2.5.3b Isobaric Process|isobaric]] process. Both are in $\text{J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K})$. For an ideal gas with $d$ accessible [[2.3.4 Equipartition of Energy|degrees of freedom]],
>$$C_V = \tfrac{d}{2}\, R, \qquad C_P = \left( \tfrac{d}{2} + 1 \right) R, \qquad C_P - C_V = R, \qquad \gamma = \frac{C_P}{C_V} = 1 + \frac{2}{d}.$$
>The difference $R$ is the work per mole per kelvin that an expanding gas does at constant pressure. The ratio $\gamma$ is the [[2.5.3d Adiabatic Process|adiabatic constant]]. Neither $C_V$ nor $C_P$ describes an isothermal or an adiabatic process, and no constant does. For any other path the heat is computed from the first law.
>
>| Gas | Rigid-molecule $d$ | $C_V$ predicted | $C_V$ measured | $C_P$ measured | $\gamma$ measured | $d$ from measured $C_V$ |
>|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
>| He | $3$ | $12.5$ | $12.5$ | $20.8$ | $1.67$ | $3.0$ |
>| Ne | $3$ | $12.5$ | $12.7$ | $20.8$ | $1.64$ | $3.1$ |
>| N$_2$ | $5$ | $20.8$ | $20.8$ | $29.1$ | $1.40$ | $5.0$ |
>| O$_2$ | $5$ | $20.8$ | $21.0$ | $29.3$ | $1.40$ | $5.1$ |
>| CO$_2$ | $5$ | $20.8$ | $28.5$ | $36.9$ | $1.30$ | $6.9$ |
>| H$_2$O vapor ($100\ ^\circ\text{C}$) | $6$ | $24.9$ | $27.0$ | $35.4$ | $1.31$ | $6.5$ |
>
>Values are at room temperature in $\text{J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K})$. The monatomic and diatomic rows match the rigid-molecule count. The last two rows are polyatomic gases whose vibrations are partly active at room temperature, so the $d$ read from the measurement exceeds the rigid count and is not an integer. The last column is $2 C_V / R$. The course rarely needs $C_V$ and $C_P$ by name, because writing $\tfrac{d}{2} Nk$ and $\left(\tfrac{d}{2} + 1\right) Nk$ says the same thing and keeps the physics visible.

# Intuition

Warm one mole of a gas by one kelvin in a rigid box. Every joule goes into the motion of the molecules, and the bill is $\tfrac{d}{2} R$, about $12.5\ \text{J}$ for helium and $20.8\ \text{J}$ for nitrogen. Now warm the same mole by one kelvin under a free piston. The molecules need the same $\tfrac{d}{2} R$ to move faster, but the gas also expands and lifts the piston, and that costs $P\,\Delta V = R\,\Delta T$, another $8.3\ \text{J}$. The heat capacity at constant pressure is larger by exactly the work of expansion. The difference is the same $R$ for every ideal gas because the work of expansion per mole per kelvin does not care what the molecules are. The ratio $\gamma = C_P / C_V$ does care, because $C_V$ counts the modes, and the ratio is a fingerprint of the molecule.

# Properties

#### *(The Difference Is Always $R$)* $C_P - C_V = R$ for every ideal gas, monatomic or not.

The work done in an isobaric expansion is $P\,\Delta V$, and the ideal gas law makes that $nR\,\Delta T$. Dividing by $n\,\Delta T$ gives $R$, with no reference to $d$. Real gases obey this closely at ordinary pressures. Solids and liquids do not, because they barely expand; their two specific heats differ by a few percent and are usually quoted as one value $c$.

#### *(The Ratio Counts Modes)* $\gamma = 1 + 2/d$ decreases as molecules get more complex.

A monatomic gas has $\gamma = 5/3$, a diatomic gas $7/5$, and a polyatomic gas with all seven modes active $9/7$. Measuring $\gamma$, for example from the [[1.1w.1 Sound|speed of sound]] $\sqrt{\gamma kT / m}$, therefore reveals how many degrees of freedom are active. The nineteenth-century puzzle that diatomic gases gave $1.40$ rather than the $1.29$ expected from seven modes was one of the first signs of quantum freeze-out.

#### *(Two Processes Only)* $C_V$ and $C_P$ apply to two paths and to no others.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover the effective heat capacity of a $PV^x$ path at the end of this property.

An isothermal process has $Q \neq 0$ with $\Delta T = 0$, and an adiabatic process has $Q = 0$ with $\Delta T \neq 0$. Neither fits $Q = nC\,\Delta T$ with a finite constant. A path drawn at an angle on the $P$-$V$ diagram has its own effective heat capacity, which can even be negative if the gas does more work than the heat it takes in. The safe rule is the recipe: work from the path, $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$ from the endpoints, heat from the [[2.5.2 First Law of Thermodynamics|first law]]. On a path $PV^x = \text{const}$, [[2.5.3d Adiabatic Process]] gives the heat as $Q = (\tfrac{d}{2} - \tfrac{1}{x-1})\,Nk\,\Delta T$. Per mole, that path has the effective heat capacity
$$C = C_V - \frac{R}{x - 1}.$$
It gives $C_P$ at $x = 0$, zero at $x = \gamma$, and $C_V$ as $x \to \infty$. It is negative for $1 < x < \gamma$.

#### *(Heat Capacity of the Whole Gas)* A sample of $n$ moles has heat capacity $nC_V = \tfrac{d}{2} nR = \tfrac{d}{2} Nk$ at constant volume, in joules per kelvin.

The molar value $C_V$ is per mole. Multiplying by the number of moles gives the heat capacity of the whole sample. It is the gas version of $C = mc$ in [[2.4.3 Specific Heat]]. Then $Q_V = (nC_V)\,\Delta T$. Problems sometimes write $C_v$ or $C_V$ for this whole-sample quantity. The units tell which one is meant. Joules per kelvin means the whole sample, and joules per mole-kelvin means one mole. A problem that says "$C_V = 3R$" for one mole means $d = 6$, since $C_V = \tfrac{d}{2} R$.

#### *(Per Mole or Per Molecule)* $C_V = \tfrac{d}{2} R$ per mole is the same statement as $\tfrac{d}{2} k$ per molecule.

Multiplying $\tfrac{d}{2} k$ by Avogadro's number gives $\tfrac{d}{2} R$, since $R = N_A k$. The molar form is the chemist's habit and appears in tables. The per-molecule form keeps the count of degrees of freedom in view. These notes therefore write $\tfrac{d}{2} Nk\,\Delta T$ and rarely write $C_V$.

# Derivation

We want $C_V$ and $C_P$ from the first law. At constant volume no work is done, so the heat equals the change in [[2.4.1 Internal Energy|internal energy]],
$$Q_V = \Delta E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2}\, nR\, \Delta T,$$
and comparing with $Q_V = n C_V\,\Delta T$ gives $C_V = \tfrac{d}{2} R$. At constant pressure the gas also does work $P\,\Delta V$, and the ideal gas law at fixed $P$ gives $P\,\Delta V = nR\,\Delta T$. The internal energy change is the same as before, because it depends only on $\Delta T$, so
$$Q_P = \Delta E_{\text{int}} + W = \tfrac{d}{2}\, nR\, \Delta T + nR\, \Delta T = \left( \tfrac{d}{2} + 1 \right) nR\, \Delta T,$$
and $C_P = \left(\tfrac{d}{2} + 1\right) R$. Subtracting gives $C_P - C_V = R$, and dividing gives $\gamma = (d + 2)/d = 1 + 2/d$, the headline results.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Warming Nitrogen Two Ways
>Two moles of nitrogen are warmed by $50\ \text{K}$, once at constant volume and once at constant pressure.
>
>>[!example]- Find the heat needed in each case and account for the difference.
>>With $d = 5$, $C_V = 20.8$ and $C_P = 29.1\ \text{J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K})$,
>>$$Q_V = (2.00)(20.8)(50) = 2080\ \text{J}, \qquad Q_P = (2.00)(29.1)(50) = 2910\ \text{J}.$$
>>The difference of $830\ \text{J}$ is the work of expansion, $nR\,\Delta T = (2.00)(8.314)(50) = 831\ \text{J}$. The internal energy rose by $2080\ \text{J}$ in both cases.

>[!example] (Medium) Reading $d$ from $\gamma$
>A gas is found to have $\gamma = 1.30$.
>
>>[!example]- How many degrees of freedom are active, and what kind of molecule is it?
>>Solving $\gamma = 1 + 2/d$ gives
>>$$d = \frac{2}{\gamma - 1} = \frac{2}{0.30} = 6.7.$$
>>A monatomic gas would give $3$ and a rigid diatomic $5$, so this is a polyatomic molecule with some vibration active. Carbon dioxide has $\gamma = 1.30$ at room temperature. The non-integer value means the vibrational modes are partly excited, which the [[2.3.4 Equipartition of Energy|equipartition]] note describes as a gradual rather than sudden freeze-out.

>[!example] (Hard) A Mixture of Gases
>A container holds one mole of helium and one mole of nitrogen.
>
>>[!example]- Find the effective $C_V$ and $\gamma$ of the mixture.
>>Internal energies add. Warming the mixture by $\Delta T$ takes $\tfrac{3}{2} R\,\Delta T$ for the helium and $\tfrac{5}{2} R\,\Delta T$ for the nitrogen, a total of $4R\,\Delta T$ for two moles. Per mole of mixture,
>>$$C_V = \frac{\tfrac{3}{2} R + \tfrac{5}{2} R}{2} = 2R = 16.6\ \text{J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}).$$
>>The difference $C_P - C_V = R$ still holds, because the work of expansion per mole per kelvin does not depend on the molecules, so $C_P = 3R$ and
>>$$\gamma = \frac{C_P}{C_V} = \frac{3R}{2R} = 1.50.$$
>>The effective $d$ is $4$, the average of $3$ and $5$. The mixture behaves like a gas whose molecules have four degrees of freedom each, though no such molecule exists.
