>[!note] Electric Current
>An **electric current** is a flow of charge. To measure it, pick a surface that the charge crosses, such as a cross-section of a wire. Let $Q$ be the total charge that has crossed the surface by time $t$. The current is the rate at which this charge grows:
>$$I = \frac{dQ}{dt}.$$
>Here $I$ is the current, $Q$ is the charge that has crossed the surface, and $t$ is the time. A steady current $I$ carries the charge $Q = It$ across the surface in a time $t$. When the current changes, the charge that crosses during an interval, divided by the interval, gives the average current. The unit of current is the **ampere**, $1\ \text{A} = 1\ \text{C/s}$.
>
>The **direction** of a current is the direction in which positive charge would move. In a metal the moving charges are electrons, and they move opposite to the current. A flow of electrons to the left is a current to the right. Every current is therefore described as the motion of an equivalent positive charge.
>
>A current inside a conductor needs an electric field inside it. In [[3.3.3. Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium|electrostatic equilibrium]] the field inside a conductor is zero, and no charge moves. A current flows only while something, such as a power supply, keeps a field inside the conductor.

# Intuition

The word *current* comes from the flow of water. A river's current describes how much water passes a point each second. An electric current describes how much charge passes a surface each second. Its direction is set by convention as the way a positive charge would travel.

Inside a metal, the free electrons do not speed up without limit. The field pushes an electron, and it speeds up. Then it hits an atom and bounces off, and the field speeds it up again. A classroom demonstration showed the same pattern with a pachinko board. Marbles roll down a tilted board covered in nails. The nails stand for the atoms, and the marbles stand for the electrons. The pull of gravity along the board stands for the electric force. Each marble keeps stopping and starting, but its average speed down the board stays constant. Electrons in a wire also move at a constant average speed, set by the field. [[3.6.2. Current Density]] calls this average velocity the drift velocity.

Electrons move left through a wire while a counter tracks how many cross a surface. The same current then comes from positive charge moving right. Last, the Medium example's electrons and ions both give a current to the right.

# Properties

#### *(A Conductor Between Plates)* A conductor between charged plates carries a current only once it touches them, because then a field remains inside.

Two ideal metal plates connect to a power supply through a switch. Ideal plates let charge move through them with no opposition. A cylinder of real metal sits in the gap without touching either plate. Close the switch, and the plates take charges $+Q$ and $-Q$. The plates' field pulls free electrons in the cylinder toward the end that faces the positive plate. That end becomes negative, and the far end becomes positive. These [[3.1.6. Induced Charge|induced charges]] cancel the plates' field inside the cylinder, and the electrons stop. Now move the plates until they press on the ends of the cylinder. The electrons at the negative end cross onto the positive plate. Electrons from the negative plate cross onto the positive end. The induced charges disappear, so nothing cancels the field inside the metal. The electrons inside feel a net force and keep moving. Electrons leave one end and new ones enter the other end, as long as the switch stays closed. This steady flow of charge is a current.

#### *(Electrons Move Against the Current)* Electrons moving against the current change the charge at each end in the same way as positive charge moving with it.

Suppose electrons flow from right to left through a wire. The right end loses negative charge, and the left end gains it. The net charge of the right end rises, and that of the left end falls. A flow of positive charge from left to right would cause the same changes. For circuits the two descriptions give the same results. Circuit work uses the positive-charge description. A current therefore leaves the positive terminal of a supply and returns to its negative terminal through the outside circuit. [[3.6.2. Current Density]] gives a deeper reason for this choice.

#### *(The Same Current Along a Wire)* In a steady state, the same current crosses every cross-section of a single wire.

Charge is never created or destroyed, by [[3.1.2. Conservation of Charge|conservation of charge]]. In a steady state, no charge piles up anywhere in the wire either. The charge that enters a piece of wire each second must therefore leave it in the same second. The current into the piece equals the current out of it. This holds for thick and thin parts of a wire alike. It also holds for any surface that the whole current must cross, as in the spherical resistor of [[3.6.4. Ohm's Law]]. At a point where several wires meet, the same argument gives [[3.7.4. Kirchhoff's Junction Rule]].

#### *(Sizes of Currents)* One ampere is a large current for household wiring and for the human body.

Each electron carries $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$. A current of $1\ \text{A}$ moves $1\ \text{C}$ each second. It therefore moves $1/(1.602 \times 10^{-19}) = 6.24 \times 10^{18}$ electrons each second. A phone charger delivers one to three amperes. A household branch circuit serves several outlets, and its circuit breaker usually limits it to $20\ \text{A}$. A current of about $0.15\ \text{A}$ through the human body can kill, depending on its path.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Charging a Phone
>A charging cable carries a steady $2.4\ \text{A}$ for $45$ minutes.
>
>>[!example]- Find the charge that passes through the cable and the number of electrons. Which way do the electrons move?
>>The time is $t = 45 \times 60 = 2700\ \text{s}$. The charge is
>>$$Q = It = (2.4\ \text{A})(2700\ \text{s}) = 6.48 \times 10^{3}\ \text{C}.$$
>>Each electron carries $1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$, so the number of electrons is
>>$$N = \frac{Q}{e} = \frac{6.48 \times 10^{3}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}} = 4.04 \times 10^{22}.$$
>>Here $N$ is the number of electrons and $e$ is the charge of each. The electrons move opposite to the current. In the conductor that carries current toward the phone, they move away from the phone.

>[!example] (Medium) Two Kinds of Carriers
>A gas discharge tube conducts through both electrons and positive ions. Each second, $2.5 \times 10^{18}$ electrons cross one cross-section toward the left end. In the same second, $4.0 \times 10^{17}$ positive ions cross it toward the right end. Each ion carries a charge $+e$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the size and direction of the current.
>>Electrons moving left count as a current to the right. Their part of the current has size
>>$$I_e = (2.5 \times 10^{18}\ \text{s}^{-1})(1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}) = 0.401\ \text{A}.$$
>>Here $I_e$ is the current carried by the electrons. The positive ions moving right are also a current to the right. Their part has size
>>$$I_+ = (4.0 \times 10^{17}\ \text{s}^{-1})(1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}) = 0.064\ \text{A}.$$
>>Here $I_+$ is the current carried by the ions. Both parts point the same way, so they add. The current is $I = 0.401 + 0.064 = 0.465\ \text{A}$, to the right. Subtracting the parts because the carriers move in opposite directions gives the wrong answer, $0.336\ \text{A}$.

>[!example] (Hard) A Current That Dies Away
>The current in a wire starts at $I_0 = 3.0\ \text{A}$ at $t = 0$ and then falls as
>$$I(t) = I_0\,e^{-t/\tau},$$
>with $\tau = 0.20\ \text{s}$. Here $I_0$ is the starting current, and $\tau$ is the decay time, which sets how fast the current falls.
>
>>[!example]- Find the charge that has passed by time $t$, the total charge, and the time by which $90\%$ of it has passed.
>>The charge is the integral of the current over time. The tilde marks the integration variable, to keep it apart from the upper limit $t$:
>>$$Q(t) = \int_0^t I_0\,e^{-\tilde t/\tau}\,d\tilde t = I_0\tau\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right).$$
>>Here $Q(t)$ is the charge that has crossed by time $t$. As $t$ grows, $e^{-t/\tau}$ goes to zero, so the total charge is
>>$$Q_{\text{tot}} = I_0\tau = (3.0\ \text{A})(0.20\ \text{s}) = 0.60\ \text{C}.$$
>>Here $Q_{\text{tot}}$ is the charge that passes over all time. A steady current $I_0$ would carry the same charge in the time $\tau$. Ninety percent has passed when $1 - e^{-t/\tau} = 0.90$, so $e^{-t/\tau} = 0.10$. Taking the natural logarithm gives
>>$$t = \tau\ln 10 = (0.20\ \text{s})(2.303) = 0.46\ \text{s}.$$
>>Here $\ln 10$ is the natural logarithm of ten. At that time the current is $0.30\ \text{A}$, a tenth of its starting value.
