>[!note] Maximum a Posteriori (MAP) Estimate 
>The **maximum‑a posteriori (MAP) estimate** chooses the [[1.1 Estimators|estimator]] under which the parameter $\theta$ is most probable _after_ observing the data, by maximizing the posterior distribution. Let $X=(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ be observed data with likelihood $p_X(x \mid \theta)$, and assume a prior distribution $p_{\Theta}(\theta)$ on the random parameter $\Theta$. Then, the **MAP estimate** of $\theta$ is the value that maximizes the posterior $p_{\Theta}(\theta\mid X)$, such that
>$$\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}(x)=\arg\max_{\theta} p_{\Theta \mid X}(\theta \mid x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n})=\arg\max_{\theta} \{ p_{X \mid \Theta}(x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n} \mid \theta) \cdot p_{\Theta}(\theta) \}.$$

# Examples
>[!example] I. Poisson Process MAP
>Customers arrive to a store according to a Poisson process with rate $\lambda=1$. The store manager learns of a rumor that one of the employees is sending every other customer to the rival store, so that deterministically, every odd-numbered customer is sent away.
>
>Let $X = 1$ be the hypothesis that the rumor is true and $X = 0$ the rumor is false, assuming that both hypotheses are equally likely. Suppose a customer arrives to the store at time $0$. After that, the manager observes $T_{1}, \ldots,T_{n}$, where $T_{i}$ is the time of the $i$-th subsequent sale, $i = 1, \ldots, n$. Derive the MAP rule to determine whether the rumor was true or not.
>
>>[!example]- Solution.
>>Since both hypotheses are equally likely, MAP is equivalent to MLE. We form the posterior 
>>$$f_{T_{1}, \ldots, T_{n} \mid X}(t_{1}, \ldots, t_{n} \mid 1)=\prod^{n}_{i=1}(t_{i}-t_{i-1})e^{-(t_{i}-t_{i-1})}$$

>[!example] II. Laplace Prior and $\ell^1$-Regularization (LASSO)
>Suppose you draw $n$ i.i.d. data points $(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n})$, where the true relationship is given by $Y = WX + \epsilon$ for $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$. In other words, $Y$ has a linear dependence on $X$ with additive Gaussian noise. Further suppose that $W$ has a Laplace prior distribution,
>$$f_{W}(w)=\frac{1}{2\beta}e^{-|w|/\beta}.$$
>Show that finding the MAP estimate of $W$ given the data points $\{ x_{i}, y_{i} \}_{i=1}^n$ is equivalent to minimizing the cost function
>$$J(w)=\sum^{n}_{i=1}(y_{i}-wx_{i})^2+\lambda|w|,$$
>and determine $\lambda$.
>>[!example]- Solution.
>>Since we know $f_{W}(w)$, we need to find $f_{X, Y \mid W}(x_{i}, y_{i} \mid w)$, which is equivalent to $\epsilon=Y-WX \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$. As such, 
>>$$f_{E \mid W}(\epsilon \mid w)=\prod^{n}_{i=1}f_{E_{i}}(\epsilon_{i})=\prod^{n}_{i=1} \frac{1}{\sqrt{ 2\pi }\sigma}e^{-(y_{i}-wx_{i})^2/(2\sigma^2)}.$$
>>This means that our likelihood function is 
>>$$L(w)=\frac{1}{2\beta}e^{-|w|/\beta}\prod^{n}_{i=1} \frac{1}{\sqrt{ 2\pi }\sigma}e^{-(y_{i}-wx_{i})^2/(2\sigma^2)} \propto e^{-|w|/\beta}\prod^{n}_{i=1}e^{-(y_{i}-wx_{i})^2/(2\sigma^2)}.$$
>>Taking the log-likelihood, we have
>>$$LL(w)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum^{n}_{i=1}(y_{i}-wx_{i})^2-\frac{|w|}{\beta}.$$
>>Since we are maximizing for $LL(w)$, which is concave on $w$ (positive absolute values are convex, and we have a negative absolute value), then we are minimizing for 
>>$$-LL(w)=\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum^{n}_{i=1}(y_{i}-wx_{i})^2+\frac{|w|}{\beta},$$
>>where $J(w)=-2\sigma^2LL(w)$ with $\lambda=2\sigma^2/\beta$.

>[!example] III. Bayesian Estimation of Exponential Distribution
>Suppose that $X$ is Exponential with unknown rate $\Lambda$. As a Bayesian practitioner, you have a prior belief that the random variable $\Lambda$ is equally likely to be $\lambda_{1}$ or $\lambda_{2}$.
>
>**A)** You collect one sample $X_{1}$ from $X$. Find the posterior distribution $\mathbb{P}(\Lambda = \lambda_{1} \mid X_{1} = x_{1})$.
>>[!example]- A) Solution.
>>We know by Bayes that 
>>$$\mathbb{P}(\Lambda=\lambda_{1} \mid X_{1} = x_{1}) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(X_{1} = x_{1} \mid \Lambda = \lambda_{1})\mathbb{P}(\Lambda=\lambda_{1})}{\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=x_{1})}$$
>>Here, by definition we have that the prior is $\mathbb{P}(\Lambda = \lambda_{1})=1/2$, and $\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=x_{1} \mid \Lambda = \lambda_{1})=\lambda_{1}e^{-\lambda_{1}x_{1}}$. Then, by total probability, we also have $\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=x_{1})=1/2 \cdot[\lambda_{1}e^{-\lambda_{1}x_{1}}+\lambda_{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}x_{1}}]$. As such,
>>$$\mathbb{P}(\Lambda=\lambda_{1} \mid X_{1}=x_{1})=\frac{\lambda_{1}e^{-\lambda_{1}x_{1}}}{\lambda_{1}e^{-\lambda_{1}x_{1}}+\lambda_{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}x_{1}}}.$$
>
>**B)** Instead of using MAP or MLE and fixing some $\lambda$ of $\Lambda$, we will keep the full information of the posterior distribution of $Λ$. Compute the distribution of $X$ as
>$$f_{X}(x)=\sum_{\lambda \in \{ \lambda_{1}, \lambda_{2} \}}f_{X \mid \Lambda}(x \mid \lambda) \cdot \mathbb{P}(\Lambda=\lambda \mid X_{1} = x_{1}).$$
>>[!example]- B) Solution.
>>We find that $f_{X \mid \Lambda}(x \mid \lambda)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$. Using the answer from the previous part, we have
>>$$f_{X}(x)=\frac{\lambda_{1}^2e^{-\lambda_{1}(x_{1}+x)}+\lambda_{2}^2e^{-\lambda_{2}(x_{1}+x)}}{\lambda_{1}e^{-\lambda_{1}x_{1}}+\lambda_{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}x_{1}}} \cdot $$
>
>**C)** The fully Bayesian approach from part **B)** is often computationally intractable, which is one of the main reasons why point estimates are common in practice. Supposing that $\lambda_{1} > \lambda_{2}$, compute the MAP estimate for $\Lambda$, and calculate $f_{X}(x)$ again using the MAP rule.
>>[!example]- C) Solution.
>>We formulate our MAP/ML (equivalent here, since the prior of $\lambda$ is uniform) estimate as
>>$$\hat{\lambda}_{\text{MAP}}=\arg\max_{\lambda \in \{ \lambda_{1}, \lambda_{2} \}}\mathbb{P}(X=x_{1} \mid \Lambda=\lambda) \cdot \mathbb{P}(\Lambda=\lambda)=\arg\max_{\lambda \in \{ \lambda_{1}, \lambda_{2} \}} \mathbb{P}(X=x_{1} \mid \Lambda = \lambda),$$
>>where our likelihood function $\mathbb{P}(X=x_{1} \mid \Lambda=\lambda)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x_{1}}$ turns into $\ln \lambda-\lambda x_{1}$ when log-likelihood is applied. Given $\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}$, it also follows that $\ln \lambda_{1}-\lambda_{1}x_{1} > \ln \lambda_{2}-\lambda_{2}x_{1}$ only if 
>>$$x_{1} < \frac{\ln(\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{2})}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}=\alpha.$$
>>Since the value of $X_{1}$ determines the parameter $\lambda$ for all subsequent observations, this result implies that 
>>$$\hat{\lambda}_{\text{MAP}}=\begin{cases}
\lambda_{1} & \text{if } x_{1} < \alpha \\
\lambda_{2} & \text{if } x_{1} \geq \alpha.
\end{cases}$$
>>As such, we get that
>>$$f_{X}(x)=\begin{cases}
\lambda_{1}e^{-\lambda_{1}x} & \text{if } x_{1} < \alpha \\
\lambda_{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}x} & \text{if } x_{1} \geq \alpha,
\end{cases}$$
where $\alpha = \dfrac{\ln(\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{2})}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}$ is the intersection of the distributions with parameters $\lambda_{1}$ and $\lambda_{2}$.
