>[!note] Stackelberg Competition
>In **Stackelberg competition**, two firms choose quantities in sequence. Firm 1, the **leader**, chooses $y_1$ first. Firm 2, the **follower**, observes $y_1$ and then chooses $y_2$. Both have cost $c(y) = cy$ with $c > 0$, and inverse demand is $p(Y) = a - bY$ with $a > c > 0$ and $b > 0$. The solution concept is [[3.2b Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibrium|subgame perfect equilibrium]], found by backward induction.
>- Step 1 solves the follower's problem $\max_{y_2} (a - by_2 - by_1)\,y_2 - cy_2$ for every $y_1$. Its FOC $a - 2by_2 - by_1 - c = 0$ gives the best response
>$$y_2^*(y_1) = \frac{a-c}{2b} - \frac{y_1}{2}.$$
>- Step 2 solves the leader's problem $\max_{y_1} \big(a - by_1 - b\,y_2^*(y_1)\big)\,y_1 - cy_1$, which builds in the follower's reaction. The solution is
>$$y_1^* = \frac{a-c}{2b}, \qquad y_2^* = \frac{a-c}{4b}, \qquad Y_D^* = 3\,\frac{a-c}{4b}, \qquad p^* = \frac14 a + \frac34 c .$$
>- Profits are $\pi_1^* = \frac{(a-c)^2}{8b}$ for the leader and $\pi_2^* = \frac{(a-c)^2}{16b}$ for the follower. In [[6.9.3a Cournot Competition|Cournot]] each firm earns $\frac{(a-c)^2}{9b}$, so moving first raises the leader's profit and lowers the follower's. This is the **first-mover advantage**.
>- Geometrically, the equilibrium is the point on the follower's best-response line that maximizes the leader's profit. There the leader's iso-profit curve is tangent to that line.
>- The leader produces the [[6.9.1 Monopoly|monopoly]] output $\frac{a-c}{2b}$, and the follower produces half as much as the leader. Compared with Cournot, total output is higher ($\frac34$ versus $\frac23$ of $\frac{a-c}{b}$) and the price is lower. Unlike [[6.9.3b Bertrand Competition|Bertrand]], the price stays above $c$.
>- The follower's strategy is a full plan $y_2(\cdot)$ that names a quantity for every possible $y_1$. The equilibrium outcome is the pair $\big(y_1^*, y_2^*(y_1^*)\big)$.

# Intuition

A large cement firm has built its plant and fixed its output for the year. A newcomer sees that output and then decides how much to produce. The newcomer produces less when the incumbent produces more, because quantities are strategic substitutes. The incumbent knows this and chooses a large output to push the newcomer back. The advantage rests on commitment. Once the newcomer has chosen, the incumbent would like to cut its output, but the output is already fixed.

In a dynamic game one player moves after the other. The game is a decision tree with decision nodes ([[3.1 Extensive Form Game]]). A strategy is a plan of action at each decision node, so the follower's strategy says what it does after every possible $y_1$. In a subgame perfect equilibrium, at each node the player chooses the action with the highest payoff, given the other players' strategies ([[3.2 Sequential Rationality]]). Backward induction finds it: solve the last mover's choice first, then work backward. Here the follower's best response is the last step, and the leader maximizes with that response built in. The figure uses $a = 100$, $b = 1$, $c = 10$. The leader slides along the follower's line $y_2^*(y_1)$ and stops at $S$, where it touches its lowest reachable iso-profit curve.

# Properties

#### *(Iso-Profit Curves)* Each iso-profit curve of the leader is a hill that peaks on the leader's own best-response line, and lower hills mean higher profit.

The curve with profit $\bar\Pi_1$ satisfies $\bar\Pi_1 = (a-c)\,y_1 - by_1y_2 - by_1^2$. Solving for $y_2$ gives
$$y_2 = \frac{a-c}{b} - y_1 - \frac{\bar\Pi_1}{b\,y_1}, \qquad \frac{dy_2}{dy_1} = -1 + \frac{\bar\Pi_1}{b\,y_1^2}.$$
The slope is zero at $y_1 = \sqrt{\bar\Pi_1/b}$, where $y_2 = \frac{a-c}{b} - 2y_1$. This is the leader's best-response line, so every hill peaks on it. At fixed $y_1$, a smaller $y_2$ means a higher price, so hills closer to the $y_1$-axis carry more profit. In Cournot, firm 1 takes $y_2$ as given and picks the peak of its hill, which gives $C$. In Stackelberg it chooses the lowest hill that still touches the follower's line, at $S$. At $S$ the slope is $-1 + \frac{(a-c)^2/(8b)}{b\,(a-c)^2/(4b^2)} = -\frac12$, the slope of $y_2^*(y_1)$, so the curve is tangent there.

#### *(Half the Monopoly Problem)* With linear demand, the leader's profit after the follower's reaction is half the monopoly profit at the same output.

Substituting $y_2^*(y_1)$ into the price gives $p = \frac{a+c}{2} - \frac{b}{2}\,y_1$. The leader's profit becomes $\big(\frac{a-c}{2} - \frac{b}{2}y_1\big)\,y_1 = \frac12\,(a - c - by_1)\,y_1$, half the profit of a monopolist who sells $y_1$. This holds for every $y_1 \le \frac{a-c}{b}$, the range where the follower's best response is not zero. Both functions peak at the same output, so $y_1^* = \frac{a-c}{2b}$, and the leader earns half the monopoly profit $\frac{(a-c)^2}{4b}$. The follower earns a quarter of it. The equal outputs of leader and monopolist come from linear demand and constant marginal cost, and they do not hold in general. The surface shows $\pi_1(y_1, y_2)$ for the figure's numbers. The thin curves are slices at fixed $y_2$, and their peaks form the leader's best response (red). The blue curve lifts the follower's line onto the surface, and the dashed line is its shadow on the floor. $S$ is the highest point on the blue curve, and $C$ is where the blue and red curves meet.

#### *(Commitment)* The leader's output is not a best response to the follower's output, so the advantage needs a choice that cannot be revised.

Firm 1's best response to $y_2 = \frac{a-c}{4b}$ is $\frac{a-c}{2b} - \frac{a-c}{8b} = \frac{3(a-c)}{8b}$, less than $\frac{a-c}{2b}$. If the leader could revise its output after seeing $y_2$, it would cut back to $\frac{3(a-c)}{8b}$. The follower would foresee the cut, and the first announcement would carry no weight. A plant already built, output already produced, or a binding contract can make the large output stick. Timing changes outcomes in other games too: a coordination game with two equilibria is resolved when one player moves first.

>The slides did not discuss non-credible threats or Nash equilibria of the sequential game that fail subgame perfection.

#### *(Only Credible Responses)* Subgame perfection rules out follower threats that the follower would not carry out.

Suppose firm 2 announces that it will produce $\frac{a-c}{3b}$ if $y_1 = \frac{a-c}{3b}$, and $\max\{0, \frac{a-c}{b} - y_1\}$ otherwise. The second part floods the market and pushes the price to $c$ or below, so every other $y_1$ earns firm 1 zero or a loss. Firm 1's best reply is the Cournot output, and the Cournot outcome is a Nash equilibrium of the sequential game. The threat is not credible. If firm 1 produced $\frac{a-c}{2b}$ anyway, firm 2's best move would be $\frac{a-c}{4b}$, and flooding would cut its own profit to zero. Subgame perfection requires a best response at every node, including nodes that are never reached, and it leaves only the Stackelberg outcome.

# Derivation

We want the subgame perfect equilibrium by backward induction. The follower's step is in the callout. For the leader, substitute $y_2^*(y_1)$ into its profit:
$$\pi_1 = \left(a - by_1 - b\left(\frac{a-c}{2b} - \frac{y_1}{2}\right)\right)y_1 - cy_1 = \left(\frac{a-c}{2} - \frac{b}{2}\,y_1\right)y_1 .$$
The FOC is $\frac{a-c}{2} - b\,y_1 = 0$, so $y_1^* = \frac{a-c}{2b}$. The second derivative is $-b < 0$, so this is a maximum by [[7.1 Unconstrained Optimality Conditions]]. The follower then produces $y_2^* = \frac{a-c}{2b} - \frac{a-c}{4b} = \frac{a-c}{4b}$. Total output is $Y_D^* = \frac{3(a-c)}{4b}$ and the price is $p^* = a - \frac34(a-c) = \frac14 a + \frac34 c$. The markup is $p^* - c = \frac{a-c}{4}$, so $\pi_1^* = \frac{a-c}{4}\cdot\frac{a-c}{2b} = \frac{(a-c)^2}{8b}$ and $\pi_2^* = \frac{a-c}{4}\cdot\frac{a-c}{4b} = \frac{(a-c)^2}{16b}$.

>
>>[!example]- Find the outputs, the price, and the profits. Compare with Cournot, where each firm produces $30$ at price $40$.
>>The follower's best response is $y_2 = 45 - y_1/2$. The leader produces $90/2 = 45$, and the follower $45 - 22.5 = 22.5$. Total output is $67.5$ and the price is $32.5$. The leader earns $22.5 \cdot 45 = 1{,}012.5$ and the follower $22.5 \cdot 22.5 = 506.25$. Against Cournot, the leader gains $112.5$, the follower loses $393.75$, and buyers pay $\$7.50$ less per ton. Industry profit falls from $1{,}800$ to $1{,}518.75$.

>[!example] (Medium) A Two-Option Game Tree
>Use the demand and costs of the Easy example. Firm 1 can produce $30$ or $45$. Firm 2 sees firm 1's choice and can produce $22.5$ or $30$.
>
>>[!example]- (a) Solve the game by backward induction. (b) Solve the same game when both firms choose at the same time.
>>(a) After $y_1 = 30$, firm 2 compares $843.75$ with $900$ and produces $30$. After $y_1 = 45$, it compares $506.25$ with $450$ and produces $22.5$. Firm 1 therefore compares $900$ with $1{,}012.5$ and produces $45$. The equilibrium strategies are $y_1 = 45$ for firm 1 and "$30$ after $30$, $22.5$ after $45$" for firm 2.
>>
>>(b) At the same time, $30$ is a dominant strategy for firm 1: it earns $1{,}125 > 1{,}012.5$ against $22.5$ and $900 > 675$ against $30$. Firm 2 best-responds with $30$, and the equilibrium is the Cournot pair $(30, 30)$. The output $45$ is dominated when firms move together and optimal when firm 1 moves first. Moving first works because firm 2 sees $45$ and shrinks.

>[!example] (Hard) Leading With Higher Costs
>Demand is $p = 100 - Y$. Firm 1 is the leader but has an older plant, $c_1 = 20$. The follower has $c_2 = 10$.
>
>>[!example]- (a) Find the Stackelberg outcome. (b) Compare it with the Cournot outcome for the same costs, where $y_1 = 23.33$ and $y_2 = 33.33$. (c) What is the most firm 1 would pay for the right to move first?
>>(a) The follower's best response is $y_2 = \frac{90 - y_1}{2}$. The leader maximizes $\big(100 - y_1 - \frac{90 - y_1}{2} - 20\big)\,y_1 = \big(35 - \frac{y_1}{2}\big)\,y_1$. The FOC $35 - y_1 = 0$ gives $y_1^* = 35$, and then $y_2^* = 27.5$. Total output is $62.5$ and the price is $37.5$. The leader earns $(37.5 - 20)(35) = 612.5$ and the follower $(37.5 - 10)(27.5) = 756.25$.
>>
>>(b) In Cournot the price is $43.33$, the high-cost firm earns $(43.33 - 20)(23.33) = 544.4$, and the low-cost firm earns $1{,}111.1$. Leadership lets the high-cost firm outproduce its cheaper rival, $35$ against $27.5$, and raises its profit by $68.1$. The follower still earns more, because its margin per ton is $27.5$ against the leader's $17.5$.
>>
>>(c) Firm 1 gains $612.5 - 544.4 = 68.1$ by leading, so it would pay up to about $\$68$, for instance to build capacity early.
