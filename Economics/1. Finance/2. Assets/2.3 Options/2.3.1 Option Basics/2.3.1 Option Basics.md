>[!note] Option Basics
>An option gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy (a call) or sell (a put) an asset at a fixed strike $K$. [[2.3.1a Option Contract]] defines calls and puts, European and American exercise, moneyness, intrinsic value, and time value. [[2.3.1b Option Price Bounds]] uses no-arbitrage to bound prices without any model, for example $\max(S - Ke^{-rT}, 0) \le c \le S$. [[2.3.1c Early Exercise]] shows that an American call on a stock with no dividends is never exercised early, while an American put may be. [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity]] links European call and put prices through $c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S$.
>
>None of these notes needs a model of how the stock price moves. Models enter in [[2.3.3 Option Pricing]].

# Contents

- [[2.3.1a Option Contract]]: calls and puts, European and American styles, moneyness, intrinsic and time value, positions, and exchange-traded contracts.
- [[2.3.1b Option Price Bounds]]: the factors that move option prices, upper and lower bounds with their arbitrage proofs, and strike convexity. **Not taught:** the factor-table rows for $\sigma$, $r$, and dividends and the European entries for $T$; the dividend versions of the bounds; strike convexity (homework only).
- [[2.3.1c Early Exercise]]: when to exercise American calls and puts, with and without dividends, and the check at each node of a tree. **Not taught:** why a call is best exercised immediately before an ex-dividend date and at no other time.
- [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity]]: the parity for European options, its dividend versions, synthetic positions, and the band for American options. **Not taught:** the names of synthetic positions, conversions, and reversals.
