>[!note] Volatility Surface
>The **volatility surface** $\sigma_I(T, K)$ gives the [[2.3.5a Implied Volatility|implied volatility]] of a stock's European options at each expiry $T$ and strike $K$. The Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) model prices every option on the stock with one constant $\sigma$. If the model held, the surface would be flat. Market surfaces are not flat, and two slices describe their shape.
>- The **volatility term structure** $\sigma(T)$ is implied volatility against expiry at a fixed strike, usually at the money. Different tenors (times to expiry) can carry different amounts of uncertainty, so the term structure is expected to slope. It is **normal** when it rises with $T$ and **inverted** when it falls.
>- The **volatility smile** $\sigma(K)$ is implied volatility against strike at a fixed expiry. A smile is higher at low and high strikes than at the money. A **volatility skew** is highest at low strikes and falls as $K$ rises.
>
>Before the crash of October 1987 ("Black Monday"), implied volatility was close to flat across strikes. Since then, options at low strikes carry an extra premium for downside risk, which produces a skew. The BSM delta hedge fails when the stock price jumps. Sellers of low-strike puts therefore charge for this jump risk. Stock prices and volatility are also negatively correlated. Volatility tends to rise after the stock falls, which is when low-strike options pay. The BSM model rules out both jumps and this correlation, so the skew is evidence that the model is inadequate.
>
>No arbitrage limits the shape of the surface. Write $\tau = T - t$ for the time to expiry and $r$ for the continuously compounded risk-free rate.
>- **Vertical spread arbitrage** limits the steepness of the skew. At one expiry, a call price falls as $K$ rises, by at most $e^{-r\tau}$ per dollar of strike ([[2.3.2a Vertical Spread]]).
>- **Calendar spread arbitrage** limits how far the term structure can invert. A call on a stock with no dividends, with $r \ge 0$, is worth at least an otherwise equal call that expires earlier ([[2.3.2f Calendar Spread]]).
>
>Traders read the surface to sell expensive volatility and buy cheap volatility. The surface also moves over time. The market's expectation of realized volatility changes, and so does its premium at each strike. A model in which volatility itself is random is a **stochastic volatility** model.

# Intuition

If the BSM model were right, one number would price every option on a stock. A trader could read it from any single option and price all the others with it. Market prices do not allow this. Each option has its own implied volatility, and the surface collects them in one picture. The picture shows which options the market prices above a flat BSM level and which below. A trader uses it to compare options of different strikes and expiries on one scale.

# Properties

#### *(Shapes in Market Data)* Options on a single stock show a skew at long expiries and often a smile at short ones.

TSLA option quotes sampled on four dates between October 2023 and September 2026 show both shapes. In the October 2023 sample, every expiry shows a skew. Implied volatility falls steadily from low strikes to high strikes, and the shortest expiries fall most steeply. In the 2024, 2025 and 2026 samples, the shortest expiries show smiles. Their implied volatility is highest at the lowest strikes. It reaches a minimum near or above the stock price and rises again at high strikes. Longer expiries are flatter and slope gently downward. A smile prices large moves in both directions above the flat BSM level. A skew prices large falls above that level and large rises below it.

#### *(Vertical Spread Bound)* The price bounds on vertical spreads cap how fast implied volatility can change with strike.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules state that vertical spread arbitrage limits the skew. The slope formula below is an extension.

A bull call spread with strikes $K$ and $K + h$ pays between $0$ and $h$, so $0 \le c(K) - c(K + h) \le he^{-r\tau}$. Dividing by $h$ and letting $h \to 0$ gives $-e^{-r\tau} \le dc/dK \le 0$. With a smile, the market price at each strike is the BSM price at that strike's own volatility, $c_{\text{BSM}}(K, \sigma_I(K))$. The chain rule splits its slope into two parts,
$$\frac{dc}{dK} = -e^{-r\tau}N(d_2) + \mathcal{V}\,\frac{d\sigma_I}{dK}.$$
The first term is the BSM slope at a fixed volatility. The second is the effect of the smile through vega $\mathcal{V}$ ([[2.3.4c Vega and Rho]]). The two price bounds then become bounds on the slope of the smile,
$$-\frac{e^{-r\tau}N(-d_2)}{\mathcal{V}} \le \frac{d\sigma_I}{dK} \le \frac{e^{-r\tau}N(d_2)}{\mathcal{V}}.$$
The left bound limits a downward skew. Past it, a put at a lower strike would cost more than a put at a higher strike. The right bound limits a rising wing. Past it, a call at a higher strike would cost more than a call at a lower strike. The (Hard) example applies the left bound.

#### *(Calendar Spread Bound)* The term structure may invert, but the total implied variance $\sigma_I^2T$ cannot fall as the expiry grows.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules state that calendar spread arbitrage limits the inversion of the term structure. The total-variance form below is an extension.

Take $r = q = 0$, one strike $K$, and expiries measured from today, so $\tau = T$. A call expiring at $T_2$ is worth at least the call expiring at $T_1 < T_2$, by the argument in [[2.3.2f Calendar Spread]]. With zero rates, the BSM price depends on $\sigma$ and $T$ only through the total variance $w = \sigma^2T$, since
$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + w/2}{\sqrt{w}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sqrt{w}.$$
The call price rises with $w$, since $\partial c/\partial w = S\,n(d_1)/(2\sqrt{w}) > 0$ with $n$ the standard normal density. So the two calls are correctly ordered exactly when $\sigma_I(T_2)^2\,T_2 \ge \sigma_I(T_1)^2\,T_1$. Solving for the later volatility gives a floor,
$$\sigma_I(T_2) \ge \sigma_I(T_1)\sqrt{T_1/T_2}.$$
At the floor, the market would expect no variance at all between $T_1$ and $T_2$. A one-month volatility of $50.7\%$ allows a three-month volatility as low as $29.3\%$ but no lower. With nonzero $r$ and $q$, the rule holds when each strike is the same fraction of its expiry's forward price.

#### *(Trading the Shape)* Traders sell volatility where the surface is high and buy it where the surface is low.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules state the rule "sell expensive vol, buy cheap vol". Its match to particular strategies below is an extension.

An option is expensive when its implied volatility exceeds the trader's forecast of realized volatility. It can also be expensive relative to nearby points on the surface. The strategies of [[2.3.2 Option Strategies]] each trade a different part of the surface.
- A straddle at the money buys or sells the level of at-the-money volatility ([[2.3.2c Straddle]]).
- A calendar spread sells short-dated volatility and buys long-dated volatility. It suits an inverted term structure, where the short-dated options are the expensive ones.
- A bear put spread buys a put near the money and sells a put at a lower strike. Under a skew, the sold put carries the higher implied volatility.

Each of these trades also carries a view on the stock price. A delta-hedged position removes that view, and its profit then depends on the gap between implied and realized volatility ([[2.3.5c Volatility Trading]]).

#### *(Dynamics)* The surface moves over time, so a hedge against stock moves still leaves exposure to moves in volatility.

The four TSLA samples differ in level and in shape. In the September 2026 sample, the at-the-money term structure was normal. Short-dated options had lower implied volatility than long-dated ones. In the September 2024 sample it was strongly inverted, and in the September 2025 sample it was mostly inverted. In the October 2023 sample it was close to flat near the money, while the skew was steep. A stochastic volatility model lets $\sigma$ follow its own random process, so its surface moves as well. A delta-hedged book still gains or loses when the surface shifts. Vega measures that exposure for a parallel shift ([[2.3.4c Vega and Rho]]).

# Examples

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The (Medium) and (Hard) examples use the total-variance and slope forms of the bounds, which the modules do not cover.

>[!example] (Easy) What the Skew Adds to a Put
>A stock trades at \$60 and pays no dividends. At-the-money two-month options trade at an implied volatility of $24\%$. A two-month European put with strike \$54 trades at an implied volatility of $31\%$. The risk-free rate is $3.5\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Price the \$54 put at the at-the-money volatility and at its own implied volatility, and find the share of its market price that comes from the skew.
>>Here $\tau = 2/12$, and the discounted strike is $54e^{-0.035/6} = \$53.69$. The put formula is $p = Ke^{-r\tau}N(-d_2) - SN(-d_1)$. At $\sigma = 24\%$, $d_1 = 1.1839$ and $d_2 = 1.0859$, which give $p = \$0.36$. At $\sigma = 31\%$, $d_1 = 0.9419$ and $d_2 = 0.8153$, which give $p = \$0.75$.
>>
>>The skew adds $0.75 - 0.36 = \$0.39$ to the put, about $52\%$ of its market price. A dealer who priced every strike at the at-the-money volatility would sell this put for less than half its market price. The extra premium is the charge for downside risk.

>[!example] (Medium) A Term Structure Inverted Too Far
>A stock trades at \$50 and pays no dividends. The risk-free rate is zero, so no compounding convention is needed. At-the-money European calls with strike \$50 trade at implied volatilities of $64\%$ for one month, $42\%$ for two months, and $38\%$ for four months.
>
>>[!example]- Check the term structure for calendar spread arbitrage, find the lowest two-month implied volatility consistent with the one-month quote, and build the trade at the quoted prices.
>>The total variance $\sigma_I^2T$ for each expiry is
>>$$0.64^2 \times \frac{1}{12} = 0.0341, \qquad 0.42^2 \times \frac{2}{12} = 0.0294, \qquad 0.38^2 \times \frac{4}{12} = 0.0481.$$
>>The total variance falls from one month to two months, so those two quotes allow an arbitrage. The four-month quote is consistent with both. The lowest two-month volatility consistent with the one-month quote is $64\% \times \sqrt{1/2} = 45.25\%$.
>>
>>The BSM prices confirm the arbitrage. The one-month call costs \$3.68 and the two-month call costs \$3.42. Sell the one-month call and buy the two-month call, which brings in \$0.26 today. At one month, the sold call pays $\max(S - 50, 0)$. The bought call then has one month left. With zero rates it is worth at least $S - 50$, and it is always worth at least zero. So the position is never worth less than zero at one month. The trade keeps \$0.26 with no risk of loss. If the stock sits at \$50 and the remaining call trades at $42\%$, the position is worth a further \$2.42.

>[!example] (Hard) A Skew Too Steep
>A stock trades at \$100 and pays no dividends. A three-month European put with strike \$90 trades at an implied volatility of $30\%$. A dealer quotes the three-month put with strike \$85 at $42\%$. The risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Find the largest implied volatility at strike \$85 that allows no arbitrage, compare it with the slope bound, and build the arbitrage at the $42\%$ quote.
>>A put with a lower strike pays no more than the other put in any state, so it cannot cost more. The \$90 put at $30\%$ costs \$1.79. The \$85 put's price rises with its volatility, so the largest allowed volatility solves $p(85, \sigma) = \$1.79$. Bisection gives $\sigma = 38.61\%$.
>>
>>The slope bound gives nearly the same answer. At $K = 90$ and $\sigma = 30\%$, $d_2 = 0.6941$, so $N(-d_2) = 0.2438$. Vega is $13.97$ per unit of $\sigma$, and $e^{-r\tau} = e^{-0.01} = 0.9900$. The left bound is
>>$$\frac{d\sigma_I}{dK} \ge -\frac{0.9900 \times 0.2438}{13.97} = -0.01728 \text{ per dollar of strike}.$$
>>Moving \$5 down in strike, implied volatility can rise by at most $5 \times 0.01728 = 0.0864$, or $8.64$ points. That gives $38.64\%$ at $K = 85$. The bound is a straight-line estimate, while $N(-d_2)$ and vega change between the strikes, so the two answers differ slightly.
>>
>>At $42\%$ the \$85 put costs \$2.22, more than the \$90 put. Sell the \$85 put and buy the \$90 put, which brings in $2.22 - 1.79 = \$0.43$ today. At expiry the position pays $\max(90 - S_T, 0) - \max(85 - S_T, 0)$, which lies between $0$ and \$5. The trader keeps \$0.43 and can only gain more at expiry. In call terms, the quotes make the \$85 / \$90 bull call spread cost \$5.38. That is above its ceiling of $5e^{-0.01} = \$4.95$ by the same \$0.43.
