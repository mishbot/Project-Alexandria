>[!note] Ideal Gas
>This section builds the equation that describes a gas at low density. Kinetic energy cannot be negative, so temperature has a floor, [[2.2.1 Absolute Temperature|absolute zero]] at $-273.15\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Extrapolating the volume of a real gas to zero finds the same point. It defines the Kelvin scale, which every gas formula uses. The [[2.2.2 Gas Laws|gas laws]] are the three experimental rules that came first. Each holds one of pressure, volume, and temperature fixed and relates the other two, and each needs temperature in kelvins.
>
>The [[2.2.3 Ideal Gas Law|ideal gas law]] $PV = NkT$ combines the three laws and adds the amount of gas. It is the equation of state that the rest of thermodynamics applies to gases, and the $P$-$V$ diagram it defines is where thermodynamic processes are drawn. [[2.2.4 Avogadro's Number|Avogadro's number]] converts between moles and molecules and turns the gas constant $R$ into Boltzmann's constant $k$, so the same law also reads $PV = nRT$.

# Contents

- [[2.2.2 Gas Laws]]: Boyle's, Charles's, and Gay-Lussac's laws, the combined gas law, and why gauge pressure and Celsius readings break them. **Not taught:** the law names, the combined gas law, and gauge pressure.

# Recommended Path

Read the notes in order. Each one uses the one before it: the gas laws need kelvins, the ideal gas law combines the gas laws, and Avogadro's number relates the two forms of the ideal gas law. A reader who already knows $PV = NkT$ can start at [[2.2.3 Ideal Gas Law]], which most problems use directly.
