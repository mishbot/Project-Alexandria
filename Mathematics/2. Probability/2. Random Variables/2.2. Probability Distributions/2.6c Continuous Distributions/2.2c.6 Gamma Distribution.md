> [!note] Gamma Distribution
>A continuous random variable $X \in (0,\infty)$ follows the **Gamma distribution** $X \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)$ with **shape** $\alpha>0$ and **rate** $\beta>0$ (equivalently, scale $\theta = 1/\beta$), defined for all $x > 0$ by the [[2.2c.1 Probability Density Function|probability density function]]
>$$f_X(x)=\frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x},$$
>where $\Gamma(\alpha)$ is the **Gamma function**, defined by the integral
>$$\Gamma(\alpha)=\int_0^\infty t^{\alpha-1}e^{-t}\,dt, \qquad \alpha > 0.$$
>The Gamma function satisfies $\Gamma(\alpha+1)=\alpha\,\Gamma(\alpha)$, and for positive integers $\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!$, making it a continuous extension of the factorial.
>
>The Gamma distribution models **nonnegative, right-skewed** quantities such as waiting times and accumulated amounts. A key interpretation: if events arrive as a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$, then the waiting time to the $\alpha$-th event is $\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\lambda)$ (for integer $\alpha$).
>
>Special cases:
>- If $\alpha=1$, then $X\sim \mathrm{Exp}(\beta)$ ([[2.2c.4 Exponential Distribution|exponential]]).
>- If $\alpha=k/2$ and $\beta=1/2$, then $X \sim \mathcal{X}^2_k$ ([[2.2c.7 Chi-Square Distribution|chi-square]]).
>
>The cumulative distribution function of the Gamma distribution is
>$$F_X(x)=\frac{\gamma(\alpha,\beta x)}{\Gamma(\alpha)},$$
>where $\gamma(\alpha,z)$ is the lower incomplete gamma function.
>
>A key characteristic of the Gamma family is **shape control**: $\alpha$ governs skewness/peakedness and $\beta$ sets the time/scale. For $\alpha>1$, the density has a mode at $(\alpha-1)/\beta$; for $0<\alpha\le 1$, it is decreasing from $+\infty$ at $0$.
>
>>[!warning] Rate vs. Scale Parameterization
>>Some sources use **scale** $\theta>0$ instead of rate $\beta$. Then,
>>$$X\sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\theta)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f_X(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)\theta^\alpha}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x/\theta},$$
>>with $\theta=1/\beta$. Check which convention a class/text uses.

# Intuition
The Gamma distribution generalizes the [[2.2c.4 Exponential Distribution|Exponential]] from "time until the first event" to **"time until the $\alpha$-th event"** in a [[4.2 Poisson Processes|Poisson process]]. If you are waiting for a single event at rate $\lambda$, the wait is $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$. If you need $\alpha$ events to occur, the total wait is $\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \lambda)$—the sum of $\alpha$ independent exponential waiting times.

Beyond waiting times, the Gamma is a flexible family for modeling any **nonnegative, right-skewed** quantity. Small $\alpha$ produces a sharply peaked density near $0$ with a long tail; large $\alpha$ yields a bell-shaped density shifted away from $0$ that increasingly resembles a [[2.2c.3 Gaussian Distribution|Gaussian]] (by the [[3.3 Central Limit Theorem|central limit theorem]], since the Gamma with integer $\alpha$ is a sum of $\alpha$ i.i.d. exponentials).

# Properties
#### (*Mean*) $\mathbb{E}[X \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)] = \dfrac{\alpha}{\beta}$.
#### (*Variance*) $\mathrm{Var}[X \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)] = \dfrac{\alpha}{\beta^2}$.
#### (*Mode*) If $\alpha > 1$, $\mathrm{mode}(X) = \dfrac{\alpha-1}{\beta}$. (If $0 < \alpha \leq 1$, the mode is at $0$.)
#### (*MGF*) For $t < \beta$,
$$M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}] = \left(\frac{\beta}{\beta - t}\right)^{\alpha}.$$

#### (*Sum*) If $X_i \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha_i, \beta)$ are independent with a common rate $\beta$, then
$$\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \sim \mathrm{Gamma}\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i,\; \beta\right).$$
This follows directly from the MGF: the product of individual MGFs yields a Gamma MGF with summed shape parameters.

#### (*Scaling*) If $X \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ and $c > 0$, then $cX \sim \mathrm{Gamma}\!\left(\alpha,\; \beta/c\right)$ in rate form. Equivalently, in scale form: $cX \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha, c\theta)$.
#### (*Memorylessness?*) Only the $\alpha = 1$ case ([[2.2c.4 Exponential Distribution|exponential]]) is memoryless; for $\alpha \neq 1$, the Gamma is not memoryless.
#### (*Poisson Process Waiting Time*) If $N(t)$ is Poisson with rate $\lambda$ and $T_k$ is the waiting time to the $k$-th arrival, then $T_k \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(k, \lambda)$.
#### (*Conjugacy with Poisson*) If $X_1, \dots, X_n \mid \lambda \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$ and $\lambda \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ (rate form), then
$$\lambda \mid X_{1:n} \sim \mathrm{Gamma}\!\left(\alpha + \sum_{i=1}^n X_i,\; \beta + n\right).$$
This makes the Gamma the **conjugate prior** for the [[2.2b.9 Poisson Distribution|Poisson]] likelihood, analogous to how the [[2.2c.9 Beta Distribution|Beta]] is conjugate to the [[2.2b.2 Bernoulli Distribution|Bernoulli]].

#### (*Beta Connection*) If $Y_1 \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha, 1)$ and $Y_2 \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\beta, 1)$ are independent, then $\frac{Y_1}{Y_1 + Y_2} \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ and $Y_1 + Y_2 \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha + \beta, 1)$, with the ratio and the sum being independent.

# Examples
>[!example] Erlang Waiting Time
>Customers arrive at a service desk according to a Poisson process with rate $\lambda = 3$ per hour. The manager wants to know the distribution of the time until the $5$th customer arrives.
>>[!example]- What is the distribution, expected value, and standard deviation of the waiting time?
>>Since the arrival process is Poisson with rate $\lambda = 3$, the waiting time to the $5$th arrival is $T_5 \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(5, 3)$. The expected waiting time is
>>$$\mathbb{E}[T_5] = \frac{\alpha}{\beta} = \frac{5}{3} \approx 1.67 \text{ hours},$$
>>and the variance is
>>$$\mathrm{Var}(T_5) = \frac{\alpha}{\beta^2} = \frac{5}{9} \approx 0.556,$$
>>giving a standard deviation of $\sqrt{5/9} \approx 0.745$ hours. Since $\alpha = 5$ is an integer, this is also an [[2.2c.5 Erlang Distribution|Erlang]] distribution: the sum of $5$ independent $\mathrm{Exp}(3)$ waiting times.

>[!example] Bayesian Estimation of a Poisson Rate
>A scientist counts radioactive decays and observes $x_1 = 4, x_2 = 7, x_3 = 5$ decays in three one-second intervals. She assumes $X_i \mid \lambda \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$ with prior $\lambda \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(2, 1)$.
>>[!example]- What is the posterior distribution of $\lambda$, and what is the Bayes estimator under squared error loss?
>>By the conjugacy property, the posterior is
>>$$\lambda \mid X_{1:3} \sim \mathrm{Gamma}\!\left(2 + (4 + 7 + 5),\; 1 + 3\right) = \mathrm{Gamma}(18, 4).$$
>>The Bayes estimator under squared error loss is the posterior mean:
>>$$\hat\lambda_{\text{Bayes}} = \mathbb{E}[\lambda \mid X_{1:3}] = \frac{18}{4} = 4.5.$$
>>For comparison, the MLE is $\bar X = \frac{4 + 7 + 5}{3} \approx 5.33$. The posterior mean is shrunk toward the prior mean of $2/1 = 2$, reflecting the influence of the prior. The posterior variance is $18/16 = 1.125$, so a $95\%$ credible interval can be obtained from the Gamma quantiles.
