> [!note] Rodrigues' Formula
> **Rodrigues' formula** is the closed-form expression for the matrix exponential $e^{\hat{\omega}\theta}$, converting an axis–angle pair $(\omega, \theta)$ into a rotation matrix $R \in SO(3)$. It collapses the infinite Taylor series into a finite expression by exploiting the fact that powers of a skew-symmetric matrix **cycle** with period 2 through $\hat{\omega}$ and $\hat{\omega}^2$.
>
> Given a unit rotation axis $\omega \in \mathbb{R}^3$ with $\|\omega\| = 1$ and angle $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$, the rotation matrix is:
>
> $$e^{\hat{\omega}\theta} = I + \hat{\omega}\sin\theta + \hat{\omega}^2(1 - \cos\theta)$$
>
> where $\hat{\omega} \in so(3)$ is the $3 \times 3$ skew-symmetric **cross-product matrix**:
>
> $$\hat{\omega} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -\omega_z & \omega_y \\ \omega_z & 0 & -\omega_x \\ -\omega_y & \omega_x & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{such that } \omega \times v = \hat{\omega}\, v$$
>
> For non-unit $\omega$:
>
> $$e^{\hat{\omega}\theta} = I + \frac{\hat{\omega}}{\|\omega\|}\sin(\|\omega\|\theta) + \frac{\hat{\omega}^2}{\|\omega\|^2}(1 - \cos(\|\omega\|\theta))$$
>
> This is the 3D matrix analogue of Euler's formula $e^{j\phi} = \cos\phi + j\sin\phi$, where the role of $j$ (with $j^2 = -1$) is played by $\hat{\omega}$ (with $\hat{\omega}^3 = -\hat{\omega}$).
# Intuition
The formula decomposes the rotation of a point $q$ about axis $\omega$ by angle $\theta$ into three geometric contributions:

| Term | What it does |
|---|---|
| $I \cdot q$ | Keeps the original point |
| $\hat{\omega}\sin\theta \cdot q$ | Swings $q$ perpendicular to both $\omega$ and $q$ — the "sine" component of circular motion |
| $\hat{\omega}^2(1-\cos\theta) \cdot q$ | Pulls $q$ toward the rotation axis — the "versine" radial correction |

Together, the $\sin\theta$ and $(1-\cos\theta)$ terms trace out a circle in the plane perpendicular to $\omega$, exactly as $\cos$ and $\sin$ parameterize the unit circle in 2D.

The reason this works is that higher powers of $\hat{\omega}$ don't produce new directions — they cycle back through $\hat{\omega}$ and $\hat{\omega}^2$. So the infinite series naturally groups into the Taylor series for $\sin$ and $1 - \cos$.
# Properties

## Power identities for $\hat{\omega}$ (Lemma A)

These two identities are the engine behind the proof. For any $\omega \in \mathbb{R}^3$:

1. $\hat{\omega}^2 = \omega\omega^T - \|\omega\|^2 I_3$, from the triple cross product $\omega \times (\omega \times v) = (\omega^T v)\omega - \|\omega\|^2 v$.

2. $\hat{\omega}^3 = -\|\omega\|^2 \hat{\omega}$, since $\omega \times (\hat{\omega}^2 v)$ kills the $\omega \times \omega$ term, leaving $-\|\omega\|^2 \hat{\omega} v$.

When $\|\omega\| = 1$ these simplify to $\hat{\omega}^2 = \omega\omega^T - I$ and $\hat{\omega}^3 = -\hat{\omega}$, producing the cycling:

$$\hat{\omega}^3 = -\hat{\omega}, \quad \hat{\omega}^4 = -\hat{\omega}^2, \quad \hat{\omega}^5 = \hat{\omega}, \quad \hat{\omega}^6 = \hat{\omega}^2, \quad \ldots$$

## Hat conjugation identity

$$\widehat{(R\omega)} = R\,\hat{\omega}\,R^T$$

To find the skew-symmetric matrix of a rotated vector, conjugate the original by $R$. This is used to prove rotation matrices are orientation-preserving RBTs, and it recurs throughout [[Forward Kinematics]] and the [[Spatial Jacobian]].

## Special cases

| Input | Output |
|---|---|
| $\theta = 0$ | $e^{\hat{\omega} \cdot 0} = I$ (no rotation) |
| $\theta = \pi$ | $e^{\hat{\omega}\pi} = I + 2\hat{\omega}^2 = 2\omega\omega^T - I$ (180° flip) |
| $\theta = 2\pi$ | $e^{\hat{\omega} \cdot 2\pi} = I$ (full revolution) |
| $\omega = \hat{z}$, any $\theta$ | Recovers the standard 2D rotation matrix $R_z(\theta)$ |

## Proof sketch

Expand $e^{\hat{\omega}\theta}$, substitute the cycling pattern from Lemma A, and group terms:

$$e^{\hat{\omega}\theta} = I + \underbrace{\left(\theta - \frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \frac{\theta^5}{5!} - \cdots\right)}_{\sin\theta}\hat{\omega} \;+\; \underbrace{\left(\frac{\theta^2}{2!} - \frac{\theta^4}{4!} + \frac{\theta^6}{6!} - \cdots\right)}_{1 - \cos\theta}\hat{\omega}^2 \qquad \blacksquare$$

# Examples

## Rotation about $z$-axis by $\theta$

Let $\omega = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Then $\hat{\omega} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\hat{\omega}^2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

$$e^{\hat{\omega}\theta} = I + \sin\theta\begin{bmatrix} 0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&0 \end{bmatrix} + (1-\cos\theta)\begin{bmatrix} -1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

This recovers the standard $R_z(\theta)$, confirming that elemental rotations used in [[Euler Angles]] are special cases of Rodrigues'.

## 180° about an arbitrary axis

With $\theta = \pi$: $\sin\pi = 0$ and $1-\cos\pi = 2$, so:

$$e^{\hat{\omega}\pi} = I + 2\hat{\omega}^2 = I + 2(\omega\omega^T - I) = 2\omega\omega^T - I$$

This rank-1 structure ($R + I = 2\omega\omega^T$) is exactly what the [[Exponential Coordinates]] log map exploits in Case 3 ($\text{tr}(R) = -1$) to recover $\omega$ by normalizing any nonzero column of $R + I$.

## Implementation

```python
import numpy as np

def skew(w):
    return np.array([[0, -w[2], w[1]], [w[2], 0, -w[0]], [-w[1], w[0], 0]])

def rodrigues(omega, theta):
    """omega: unit vector (3,), theta: angle in radians -> R in SO(3)"""
    w_hat = skew(omega)
    return np.eye(3) + w_hat * np.sin(theta) + w_hat @ w_hat * (1 - np.cos(theta))
```

The inverse operation — recovering $(\omega, \theta)$ from $R$ — is the **log map**, covered in [[Exponential Coordinates]].
