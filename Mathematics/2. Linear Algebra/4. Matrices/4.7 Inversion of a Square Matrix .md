>[!note] Matrix Inverse
>A [[4.2c Square Matrix|square matrix]] $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is said to be **invertible** if it is nonsingular (i.e. $\operatorname{Det}(A) \neq 0$), such that there exists a matrix $A^{-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ for which
>$$AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I_n.$$
>If only one of the above conditions hold, we say that matrix $A$ is left-invertible (such that $A^{-1}A=I$), or right-invertible (such that $AA^{-1}=I$).
>
>We can find the inverse of a matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}$ by dividing its transpose by its [[4.6 Determinant of a Square Matrix|determinant]] so that
>$$A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{det}(A)}\begin{bmatrix}d & -c \\ -b & a\end{bmatrix}.$$
>
>Furthermore, if two square matrices $A,B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ are nonsingular, it holds that $(AB)^{-1} = A^{-1}B^{-1}$.
# Intuition
The matrix inversion formula just says that given a linear transformation of the n-space $A$, its inverse will reverse that transformation so that there is no net change for any vector in the space by yielding the[[4.2a Identity Matrix| identity matrix]] $I_n$.
