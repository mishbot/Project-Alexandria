>[!note] Homogeneity of Demand
>A function $g(\mathbf{z})$ is **homogeneous of degree $k$** if $g(t\mathbf{z}) = t^k g(\mathbf{z})$ for every $t > 0$. [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|Marshallian demand]] is homogeneous of degree $0$ in prices and income together. For every $t > 0$ and each good $i$,
>$$x_i^*(tp_1, tp_2, tM) = x_i^*(p_1, p_2, M).$$
>Here $x_i^*$ is the utility-maximizing quantity of good $i$ at prices $p_1, p_2$ and income $M$, and $t$ is a common scaling factor. Doubling every price and income leaves demand unchanged. For Cobb-Douglas utility $x_1^\alpha x_2^\beta$, demand $x_1^* = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\frac{M}{p_1}$ becomes $\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\frac{tM}{tp_1}$, and $t$ cancels. The result holds for any preferences, convex or not, differentiable or not, and also when the optimal bundle is not unique. It uses only one fact: scaling all prices and income by the same $t$ leaves the [[6.2.1 Budget Constraint|budget set]] unchanged.
>
>Demand therefore depends only on **relative prices** such as $p_2/p_1$ and on **real income** such as $M/p_1$, income measured in units of a good. A consumer whose demand has this property has **no money illusion**, meaning the consumer reacts only to what money can buy. The same argument gives three related facts. [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function|Indirect utility]] $v(\mathbf{p}, M)$ is homogeneous of degree $0$ in $(\mathbf{p}, M)$. [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization|Hicksian demand]] $h_i(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$ is homogeneous of degree $0$ in $\mathbf{p}$. The expenditure function $e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$ is homogeneous of degree $1$ in $\mathbf{p}$, so doubling all prices doubles the cost of reaching $\bar u$.

# Intuition

A budget constraint describes trade-offs. Giving up one unit of good 1 frees $p_1$ dollars, which buy $p_1/p_2$ units of good 2. Income $M$ buys at most $M/p_1$ units of good 1. When every price and income double, none of these ratios change. The consumer faces the same set of affordable bundles and picks the same bundle. The currency unit is a label with no effect on the choice.

The result is the simplest comparative statics fact about demand, and it needs no calculus. It also works as a test. Any demand formula derived on an exam must pass it. Replace every $p_j$ by $tp_j$ and $M$ by $tM$, and every $t$ must cancel.

# Properties

#### *(Normalize One Price)* One price can be set to $1$ without changing any conclusion.

Choosing $t = 1/p_1$ gives $x_i^*(p_1, p_2, M) = x_i^*(1, p_2/p_1, M/p_1)$. Demand is a function of two numbers, the relative price and real income, instead of three. This is why [[6.5.3 Intertemporal Choice]] can set the price of consumption when young to $1$. Setting the price when old to $1$ as well is a separate assumption, a constant price level, so that $r$ is a real interest rate ([[1.1 Fisher's Equation]]). It is also why the labor supply solution in [[6.5.2 Labor Supply]] depends on $w$, $M$, and $p$ only through the real wage $w/p$ and real non-labor income $M/p$.

#### *(Where Homogeneity Fails)* The result needs every money amount in the problem to scale with prices.

A pension fixed in dollars, cash savings, and debts do not scale when prices rise. If all prices double while income stays at $M$, homogeneity with $t = 1/2$ gives $x^*(2\mathbf{p}, M) = x^*(\mathbf{p}, M/2)$. Inflation with a fixed nominal income has the same effect as a cut in income. In the other direction, raising income by a factor $t$ has the same effect on demand as dividing every price by $t$.

#### *(Derivative Check)* The price and income derivatives of demand, each weighted by its own variable, sum to zero.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>This identity is Euler's theorem for homogeneous functions applied to demand. The lecture stated homogeneity but did not derive this consequence.

Differentiate $x_i^*(tp_1, tp_2, tM) = x_i^*(p_1, p_2, M)$ with respect to $t$ and set $t = 1$:
$$p_1\frac{\partial x_i^*}{\partial p_1} + p_2\frac{\partial x_i^*}{\partial p_2} + M\frac{\partial x_i^*}{\partial M} = 0.$$
For Cobb-Douglas demand $x_1^* = \alpha M/p_1$ the three terms are $-\alpha M/p_1$, $0$, and $\alpha M/p_1$. The identity gives a quick check on a derived demand function.

# Derivation

We want to show that $x^*(t\mathbf{p}, tM) = x^*(\mathbf{p}, M)$ for every $t > 0$. The budget set at prices $t\mathbf{p}$ and income $tM$ is
$$B(t\mathbf{p}, tM) = \{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^2_+ : tp_1x_1 + tp_2x_2 \le tM\}.$$
Dividing the inequality by $t > 0$ keeps its direction, so the condition is $p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 \le M$. Hence $B(t\mathbf{p}, tM) = B(\mathbf{p}, M)$. The utility function does not depend on prices or income. The consumer maximizes the same function over the same set, so the set of maximizers is the same. The maximum utility is also the same, which is the statement for $v$.

For [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization|expenditure minimization]], scaling prices by $t$ multiplies the objective $p_1x_1 + p_2x_2$ by $t$ and leaves the constraint $u(\mathbf{x}) \ge \bar u$ unchanged. Multiplying an objective by a positive constant does not move its minimizer, so $h$ is unchanged. The minimum value is multiplied by $t$, so $e(t\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = t\,e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Removing Three Zeros
>On 5 July 1999 Bulgaria redenominated its currency: 1000 old leva became 1 new lev. Every price, wage, pension, and bank balance was divided by $1000$ on the same day.
>
>>[!example]- How does a household's demand change? What if some shops round their new prices up while others do not?
>>The change scales every price and income by $t = 1/1000$. By homogeneity of degree $0$, the household buys exactly the same bundle as before.
>>
>>Rounding breaks the equal scaling. If bread goes from 1,494 old leva to 1.50 new leva, its price rises by about $0.4$ percent relative to the goods whose prices were not rounded. That is a change in a relative price, and demand can respond to it. Homogeneity says nothing about such a change.

>[!example] (Medium) A Worker and a Retiree
>A worker and a retiree have the same utility $u = x_1^{0.3}x_2^{0.7}$ over food $x_1$ and other goods $x_2$. Food costs $p_1 = 10$ and other goods cost $p_2 = 20$. The worker earns $M = 2000$ a month and the retiree receives a pension of $2000$ a month. Over a decade every price and every wage doubles. The pension is fixed in dollars.
>
>>[!example]- Find each person's demand before and after the decade.
>>Cobb-Douglas demand is $x_1^* = 0.3M/p_1$ and $x_2^* = 0.7M/p_2$. Before the decade both people buy $x_1^* = 600/10 = 60$ and $x_2^* = 1400/20 = 70$.
>>
>>After the decade the worker faces $(2p_1, 2p_2, 2M)$. By homogeneity with $t = 2$, the worker still buys $(60, 70)$. The retiree faces $(2p_1, 2p_2, M)$. Homogeneity with $t = 1/2$ turns this into $(p_1, p_2, M/2)$: old prices with half the income. The retiree buys $x_1^* = 0.3 \cdot 2000/20 = 30$ and $x_2^* = 0.7 \cdot 2000/40 = 35$, half of the original bundle.

>[!example] (Hard) Testing Proposed Demand Functions
>An analyst fits two formulas for the demand for good 1 to data:
>$$\text{(a)}\;\; x_1 = \frac{M p_2}{p_1(p_1 + p_2)}, \qquad \text{(b)}\;\; x_1 = \frac{M - 10}{2p_1}.$$
>In (b), the number $10$ is a fixed monthly phone bill in dollars.
>
>>[!example]- Which formula can be a Marshallian demand function? Repair the one that cannot.
>>For (a), replace $p_1, p_2, M$ by $tp_1, tp_2, tM$. The numerator gains $t^2$ and the denominator gains $t \cdot t = t^2$, so $t$ cancels. Formula (a) passes the test. It is in fact the [[6.4d CES Utility|CES]] demand from [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]] with $\alpha = \beta = 1$ and $\rho = 1/2$.
>>
>>For (b), scaling gives $(tM - 10)/(2tp_1)$, which differs from the original unless $t = 1$. If all prices and income doubled, the phone bill would stay at $10$ in this formula, which is money illusion. The fixed amount must be a quantity times a price. If the consumer must buy $10$ units of good 2 first, the formula becomes $x_1 = (M - 10p_2)/(2p_1)$, which is homogeneous of degree $0$. This is the demand for $u = x_1^{1/2}(x_2 - 10)^{1/2}$.
>>
>>Passing the test does not prove that a formula comes from utility maximization. Homogeneity is only a necessary condition.
