>[!note] Dirichlet-Multinomial Conjugacy
>Let $\boldsymbol\theta = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_K)$ be an unknown probability vector on a $K$-category simplex ($\theta_k \ge 0$, $\sum_k \theta_k = 1$). The **Dirichlet-Multinomial conjugate pair** is
>$$\boldsymbol\theta \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol\alpha), \qquad \mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol\theta \sim \text{Multinomial}(n, \boldsymbol\theta),$$
>where $\boldsymbol\alpha = (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_K)$ with $\alpha_k > 0$ and $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_K)$ with $\sum_k y_k = n$. The posterior is
>$$\boldsymbol\theta \mid \mathbf{y} \;\sim\; \text{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol\alpha + \mathbf{y}) \;=\; \text{Dirichlet}(\alpha_1 + y_1,\ \dots,\ \alpha_K + y_K).$$
>The prior hyperparameters update componentwise: add the observed count in each category to the prior's concentration for that category. The prior is interpretable as $\alpha_k$ "pseudo-observations" in category $k$, for a total prior sample size $\alpha_0 := \sum_k \alpha_k$.
>
>**Posterior summaries:** writing $\alpha_0 = \sum_k \alpha_k$,
>- **Posterior mean** of $\theta_k$: $\mathbb{E}[\theta_k \mid \mathbf{y}] = \dfrac{\alpha_k + y_k}{\alpha_0 + n}$ — a weighted average of the prior mean $\alpha_k/\alpha_0$ and the MLE $y_k/n$.
>- **Posterior mode (MAP)** of $\theta_k$: $\dfrac{\alpha_k + y_k - 1}{\alpha_0 + n - K}$ when $\alpha_k + y_k \ge 1$ for all $k$.
>- **Posterior marginal** of a single $\theta_k$: $\text{Beta}(\alpha_k + y_k,\ \alpha_0 + n - \alpha_k - y_k)$ — reduces to [[3.5.1 Beta-Binomial Model|Beta-Binomial]] when $K = 2$.
>- **Posterior covariance**: $\text{Cov}(\theta_j, \theta_k \mid \mathbf{y}) = -\dfrac{(\alpha_j + y_j)(\alpha_k + y_k)}{(\alpha_0 + n)^2 (\alpha_0 + n + 1)}$ for $j \ne k$. Components are always negatively correlated because they share the unit budget.
>
>**Common prior choices:**
>- $\text{Dirichlet}(1, \dots, 1) = \text{Uniform}$ on the simplex: flat prior; posterior mode equals the MLE $y_k/n$ (with a small shift).
>- $\text{Dirichlet}(1/K, \dots, 1/K)$ (Perks-Jeffreys-like, sparse): small $\alpha_0 = 1$ favors mass concentrating on a few categories.
>- $\text{Dirichlet}(1/2, \dots, 1/2)$ (Jeffreys prior): reparametrization-invariant; gentle shrinkage toward the simplex center.
>
>**Posterior predictive** for a new observation $\tilde y$ (single draw from one of $K$ categories):
>$$P(\tilde y = k \mid \mathbf{y}) = \mathbb{E}[\theta_k \mid \mathbf{y}] = \dfrac{\alpha_k + y_k}{\alpha_0 + n}.$$
>For $m$ new i.i.d. draws $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$, the predictive is **Dirichlet-Multinomial** (also called Pólya / multivariate hypergeometric): $\tilde{\mathbf{y}} \mid \mathbf{y} \sim \text{DirMult}(m,\ \boldsymbol\alpha + \mathbf{y})$, with variance strictly larger than the plug-in Multinomial.

# Intuition

The Dirichlet distribution is the multivariate generalization of the Beta: instead of one probability on $[0, 1]$, it models a full probability vector on the simplex. Its density is proportional to $\prod_k \theta_k^{\alpha_k - 1}$, exactly the kernel of the Multinomial likelihood viewed as a function of $\boldsymbol\theta$. Multiplying prior by likelihood adds exponents componentwise, and the result is again Dirichlet with concentrations incremented by the observed category counts.

The shape parameters $\alpha_k$ play two roles at once. First, their *ratios* $\alpha_k/\alpha_0$ set the prior mean (expected probability for each category). Second, their *total* $\alpha_0 = \sum_k \alpha_k$ sets the prior's concentration — a large $\alpha_0$ produces a prior tightly focused around its mean vector, and a small $\alpha_0$ produces a prior that is dispersed across the simplex. This two-parameter control is why the Dirichlet is the standard prior over discrete distributions: it separates "what I expect" from "how confident I am."

The negative posterior correlation between components is an unavoidable structural fact. Evidence that category $j$ has more probability mass than thought necessarily implies that some other category(s) have less, because the components must sum to one. No conjugate prior on the simplex could avoid this; it is a property of simplex geometry, not of Dirichlet specifically.

# Properties

#### *(Reduces to Beta-Binomial When $K=2$)* The two-category Dirichlet-Multinomial is the Beta-Binomial model.

With $K = 2$, $\boldsymbol\alpha = (\alpha, \beta)$, and $\mathbf{y} = (y, n-y)$, the Dirichlet prior is $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ and the Multinomial likelihood is $\text{Binomial}(n, \theta_1)$. The posterior $\text{Dirichlet}(\alpha + y,\ \beta + n - y)$ is exactly the [[3.5.1 Beta-Binomial Model|Beta-Binomial]] posterior. All Beta-Binomial results lift to the multicategory case.

#### *(Posterior Marginal of Each $\theta_k$ is Beta)* Marginalizing over the other $K-1$ components leaves a Beta distribution.

$$\theta_k \mid \mathbf{y} \;\sim\; \text{Beta}(\alpha_k + y_k,\ \alpha_0 + n - \alpha_k - y_k).$$
This follows from the aggregation property of the Dirichlet: merging all categories other than $k$ into one aggregate category collapses the Dirichlet to a Beta on $(\theta_k, 1 - \theta_k)$. Credible intervals for a single category probability reuse the Beta machinery from the [[3.5.1 Beta-Binomial Model]].

#### *(Aggregation and Subcomposition)* Merging categories keeps the posterior Dirichlet; conditioning on a subset is also Dirichlet.

If categories $\{1, \dots, K\}$ are partitioned into groups $G_1, \dots, G_m$ with $\tilde\theta_j = \sum_{k \in G_j} \theta_k$ and $\tilde y_j = \sum_{k \in G_j} y_k$, then $(\tilde\theta_1, \dots, \tilde\theta_m) \mid \mathbf{y} \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\tilde\alpha_1 + \tilde y_1, \dots, \tilde\alpha_m + \tilde y_m)$. Conditional on the total probability in a subset, the within-subset probabilities (renormalized) are independently Dirichlet distributed. This is why Dirichlet is the natural model for hierarchical categorical data.

#### *(Posterior Mean as Simplex Shrinkage)* Posterior mean lies between the prior mean vector and the MLE, weighted by effective sample sizes.

$$\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol\theta \mid \mathbf{y}] = w\,\frac{\boldsymbol\alpha}{\alpha_0} + (1-w)\,\frac{\mathbf{y}}{n}, \qquad w = \frac{\alpha_0}{\alpha_0 + n}.$$
A Dirichlet prior with total mass $\alpha_0$ acts like $\alpha_0$ prior observations distributed across the $K$ categories. A category with zero observed count still receives nonzero posterior mass $\alpha_k/(\alpha_0 + n)$, a form of **Laplace smoothing** that prevents posterior probabilities from collapsing to zero on unseen categories. This is widely used in natural-language models under the name "additive smoothing."

#### *(Sequential Updating)* Componentwise accumulation of category counts yields the same posterior as batch updating.

After seeing $\mathbf{y}^{(1)}$ of total size $n_1$ and then $\mathbf{y}^{(2)}$ of size $n_2$, the posterior is $\text{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol\alpha + \mathbf{y}^{(1)} + \mathbf{y}^{(2)})$ — associative in the count vector. See [[3.1 Bayesian Framework]].

#### *(Posterior Predictive is Dirichlet-Multinomial)* Predictive for $m$ new draws has extra variance compared to the plug-in Multinomial.

$$\tilde{\mathbf{y}} \mid \mathbf{y} \sim \text{DirMult}(m,\ \boldsymbol\alpha + \mathbf{y}).$$
The marginal for each component is Beta-Binomial, and the variance of $\tilde y_k$ is inflated above $m p_k(1 - p_k)$ (the Multinomial marginal variance) by a factor $(\alpha_0 + n + m)/(\alpha_0 + n + 1)$ that approaches 1 only as the posterior concentrates. The Dirichlet-Multinomial is the multicategory analog of the Beta-Binomial predictive distribution.

#### *(Negative Covariance is Structural)* $\text{Cov}(\theta_j, \theta_k \mid \mathbf{y}) < 0$ for all $j \ne k$.

Because $\sum_k \theta_k = 1$ is a hard constraint, any Bayesian model on the simplex must assign negative posterior covariance between distinct components. The exact formula is
$$\text{Cov}(\theta_j, \theta_k \mid \mathbf{y}) = -\frac{(\alpha_j + y_j)(\alpha_k + y_k)}{(\alpha_0 + n)^2 (\alpha_0 + n + 1)}.$$
If a model's inferential goal depends on independent category probabilities, this is a sign that the underlying problem is miscast — the simplex constraint is real.

#### *(Dirichlet Multinomial as a Count-Data Alternative)* Marginalizing $\boldsymbol\theta$ away yields a Dirichlet-Multinomial likelihood.

For applications where the parameter is a nuisance, integrating out $\boldsymbol\theta$ gives the marginal distribution of the counts, $p(\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol\alpha, n)$, known as the Dirichlet-Multinomial or Pólya distribution. This serves as a more flexible likelihood than Multinomial itself in overdispersed count-category data, and is the basis of hyperparameter tuning via [[3.7 Marginal Likelihood|empirical Bayes]].

# Examples

>[!example]- Example 1: Survey on a Three-Option Question
>A survey asks $n = 120$ respondents to choose among three options A, B, C. A weakly informative uniform prior is $\boldsymbol\alpha = (1, 1, 1)$ (with $\alpha_0 = 3$). Observed counts are $\mathbf{y} = (55, 40, 25)$.
>
>*Posterior:* $\boldsymbol\theta \mid \mathbf{y} \sim \text{Dirichlet}(56, 41, 26)$ with total $\alpha_0 + n = 123$.
>*Posterior means:* $(56/123,\ 41/123,\ 26/123) = (0.455,\ 0.333,\ 0.211)$ — essentially the sample proportions because the prior is very weak.
>*Marginal credible interval for $\theta_A$:* $\theta_A \mid \mathbf{y} \sim \text{Beta}(56, 67)$, 95% equal-tail CI $\approx [0.367,\ 0.545]$.
>*Posterior predictive for one new respondent:* $P(\tilde y = A) = 56/123 = 0.455$, $P(\tilde y = B) = 0.333$, $P(\tilde y = C) = 0.211$ — the predictive plug-in *uses the posterior mean*, not the MLE.

>[!example]- Example 2: Laplace Smoothing in a Language Model
>Over a vocabulary of $K = 10{,}000$ words, a corpus of $n = 10^6$ words is counted. Many words appear zero times. An MLE would assign probability zero to these words — disastrous for computing the likelihood of any future text containing one of them.
>
>Using a $\text{Dirichlet}(\alpha, \dots, \alpha)$ prior with $\alpha = 1$ (equivalent to adding one pseudo-count per word):
>$$\hat\theta_k = \frac{y_k + 1}{n + K} = \frac{y_k + 1}{10^6 + 10^4} \approx \frac{y_k + 1}{1.01 \times 10^6}.$$
>For an unseen word ($y_k = 0$), the posterior mean is $1/(n + K) \approx 10^{-6}$ — nonzero. For a common word appearing $y_k = 1000$ times, $(1001)/(1.01 \times 10^6) \approx 9.91 \times 10^{-4}$, only slightly pulled toward uniform from the raw rate $10^{-3}$. This is add-one (Laplace) smoothing, a standard regularization in $n$-gram language models derived directly from the Dirichlet-Multinomial posterior mean.

>[!example]- Example 3: A/B/C Test with Sparse Data
>A trial of three website variants records no conversions for variant C out of 20 visitors: $\mathbf{y} = (4, 6, 0)$. Naively, the MLE says variant C has zero conversion rate. Using a uniform Dirichlet prior $\boldsymbol\alpha = (1, 1, 1)$:
>
>*Posterior:* $\text{Dirichlet}(5, 7, 1)$.
>*Posterior means:* $(5/13,\ 7/13,\ 1/13) = (0.385,\ 0.538,\ 0.077)$.
>*Credible interval for $\theta_C$ (Beta marginal $\text{Beta}(1, 12)$):* 95% equal-tail CI $\approx [0.0021,\ 0.2647]$ — wide, reflecting genuine uncertainty.
>
>A frequentist Wald confidence interval for $\theta_C$ would be undefined (standard error zero with $\hat\theta_C = 0$) or degenerate; the Bayesian analysis gives a usable interval directly. Running variant C further to resolve the uncertainty is the correct next step.

# Derivation

Goal: derive the $\text{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol\alpha + \mathbf{y})$ posterior.

The Dirichlet prior is
$$p(\boldsymbol\theta) = \frac{1}{B(\boldsymbol\alpha)} \prod_{k=1}^K \theta_k^{\alpha_k - 1}, \qquad B(\boldsymbol\alpha) = \frac{\prod_k \Gamma(\alpha_k)}{\Gamma(\alpha_0)},$$
supported on the simplex. The Multinomial likelihood for $\mathbf{y}$ with $\sum_k y_k = n$ is
$$p(\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol\theta) = \binom{n}{y_1, \dots, y_K} \prod_k \theta_k^{y_k} \;\propto\; \prod_k \theta_k^{y_k}.$$
Multiplying and dropping $\boldsymbol\theta$-free constants:
$$p(\boldsymbol\theta \mid \mathbf{y}) \;\propto\; \prod_k \theta_k^{\alpha_k - 1} \cdot \prod_k \theta_k^{y_k} = \prod_k \theta_k^{(\alpha_k + y_k) - 1},$$
the kernel of $\text{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol\alpha + \mathbf{y})$, with normalizing constant $1/B(\boldsymbol\alpha + \mathbf{y})$.

*Posterior predictive:* integrating out $\boldsymbol\theta$ for a single new category draw,
$$P(\tilde y = k \mid \mathbf{y}) = \int \theta_k\, p(\boldsymbol\theta \mid \mathbf{y})\, d\boldsymbol\theta = \mathbb{E}[\theta_k \mid \mathbf{y}] = \frac{\alpha_k + y_k}{\alpha_0 + n}.$$
For $m$ new draws $\tilde{\mathbf{y}}$, the integral of $\text{Multinomial}(m, \boldsymbol\theta)$ against the Dirichlet posterior yields the closed-form Dirichlet-Multinomial $\text{DirMult}(m, \boldsymbol\alpha + \mathbf{y})$.
