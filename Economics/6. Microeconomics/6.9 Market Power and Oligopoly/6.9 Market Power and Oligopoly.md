>[!note] Market Power and Oligopoly
>This section drops the assumption that firms take the price as given. [[6.9.1 Monopoly]] studies one firm that faces the whole demand curve and solves $\max_y\ p(y)\,y - c(y)$. Selling one more unit lowers the price on every unit, so marginal revenue $p + p'(y)\,y$ lies below the price. The firm sets $MR = MC$ and reads its price off demand. The markup rule $(p - c'_y)/p = -1/\varepsilon_{y,p}$ ties the markup to the elasticity of demand. With $p = a - by$ and constant marginal cost $c$, the monopoly sells $(a-c)/(2b)$ at $(a+c)/2$, half the competitive output, which creates a deadweight loss. [[6.9.2 Price Discrimination]] relaxes the rule of one price for all units. First-degree pricing charges each buyer her value, which is efficient and gives all surplus to the firm. Second-degree pricing offers one menu and lets buyers sort themselves. Third-degree pricing separates markets and sets $p_A(1 + 1/\varepsilon_A) = c = p_B(1 + 1/\varepsilon_B)$, so the less elastic market pays more.
>
>An **oligopoly** has a few firms. Firm $i$ solves $\max_{y_i}\ p(y_i + y_{-i})\,y_i - c(y_i)$, and its best output depends on the rivals' output $y_{-i}$. This needs game theory. A game lists players, strategies $s_i \in S_i$, and payoffs $U_i(s_i, s_{-i})$ ([[2.1 Strategic Form Game]]). In the Prisoner's Dilemma, defecting is a dominant strategy ([[2.2b Strategic Dominance]]), and the equilibrium gives each player $-4$ although mutual cooperation gives $-2$. A [[2.2c Nash Equilibrium]] asks only that each strategy be a best response to the others. The Battle of the Sexes has two in pure strategies. Matching pennies, the penalty kick, has none in pure strategies, but the kicker and goalie each going left with probability $1/2$ is an equilibrium in [[2.5a Mixed Strategies]]. In dynamic games, backward induction finds the [[3.2b Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibrium]]. A ten-round Prisoner's Dilemma unravels to defection in every round, while infinite repetition with a chance of ending can sustain cooperation ([[4.1 Infinitely Repeated Game]]).
>
>[[6.9.3 Oligopoly]] compares the models with $p = a - bY$ and marginal cost $c$. In [[6.9.3a Cournot Competition]] firms choose quantities at the same time. Each produces $(a-c)/(3b)$, and the price $(a + 2c)/3$ is above marginal cost. In [[6.9.3b Bertrand Competition]] firms choose prices at the same time. The unique Nash equilibrium is $p_1 = p_2 = c$, so two firms are enough for the competitive price. In [[6.9.3c Stackelberg Competition]] firm 1 chooses its quantity first. Backward induction gives $(a-c)/(2b)$ for the leader and $(a-c)/(4b)$ for the follower, with price $(a + 3c)/4$. Total output rises from monopoly to Cournot to Stackelberg to Bertrand. [[6.9.4 Second-Price Auctions]] applies dominance to selling one object. When the winner pays the second-highest bid, bidding one's value is weakly dominant.

# Contents

- [[6.9.1 Monopoly]]: marginal revenue, $MR = MC$, the markup rule, the linear case, half pass-through of cost changes, zero-profit monopoly, and the deadweight loss. **Not taught:** pricing on the elastic part of demand, the closed-form surplus areas, and comparative statics for non-linear demand.
- [[6.9.2 Price Discrimination]]: first, second, and third degree, the photocopy menu, equal marginal revenue across markets, profit as a relaxed constraint (with a 3D profit surface), and resale. **Not taught:** the two-part tariff calculation and the welfare comparison for third-degree pricing.
- [[6.9.3 Oligopoly]]: comparison hub for quantities, prices, and profits under monopoly, Cournot, Stackelberg, Bertrand, and perfect competition with linear demand.
  - [[6.9.3a Cournot Competition]]: simultaneous quantity choice, best-response functions, and pricing above marginal cost.
  - [[6.9.3b Bertrand Competition]]: simultaneous price choice, the unique equilibrium $p = c$, and the Bertrand paradox.
  - [[6.9.3c Stackelberg Competition]]: sequential quantity choice, backward induction, and the first-mover advantage.
- [[6.9.4 Second-Price Auctions]]: payoffs, the three-case proof that bidding one's value is weakly dominant, and efficiency. **Not taught:** more than two bidders, the first-price contrast, efficiency and proxy bidding, and inefficient Nash equilibria.

# Recommended Path

Read [[6.9.1 Monopoly]] first, since every later note compares with it. [[6.9.2 Price Discrimination]] applies the monopoly markup rule market by market. Before the oligopoly notes, review [[2.2c Nash Equilibrium]] and [[3.2b Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibrium]]. Then read the hub [[6.9.3 Oligopoly]] and its siblings in the order Cournot, Bertrand, Stackelberg. Stackelberg reuses the Cournot best-response function. [[6.9.4 Second-Price Auctions]] needs only [[2.2b Strategic Dominance]] and can be read at any point. If short on time, read the callout of 6.9.1 with its Easy example, the comparison table in 6.9.3, and the three-case derivation in 6.9.4.
