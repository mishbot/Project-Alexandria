>[!note] Short-Run Market Equilibrium
>In the **short run** the number of firms $J$ is fixed. A **market equilibrium** for good $i$ is a price $p_i^*$ with no excess supply and no excess demand:
>$$Y^* = Y_i^S(p_i^*, w, r) = X_i^D(p_1^*, \dots, p_n^*, M^1, \dots, M^J).$$
>- $Y_i^S$ is industry supply and $X_i^D$ is market demand, both from [[6.8.1 Aggregation]]. The prices of other goods stay fixed.
>- Firms can earn positive profits in the short run. Each firm earns $y^{j*}(p^* - AC)$, and no new firm can enter to compete it away. Entry is the subject of [[6.8.7 Long-Run Equilibrium]].
>- For comparative statics, a parameter $\alpha$ enters supply, demand, or both: $Y_i^S(p_i, w, r, \alpha)$ and $X_i^D(\mathbf{p}, M, \alpha)$. Write the equilibrium as $Y_i^S - X_i^D = 0$. The [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] gives the effect of $\alpha$ on the price and on the quantity:
>$$\frac{\partial p^*}{\partial \alpha} = -\frac{\dfrac{\partial Y^S}{\partial \alpha} - \dfrac{\partial X^D}{\partial \alpha}}{\dfrac{\partial Y^S}{\partial p} - \dfrac{\partial X^D}{\partial p}}, \qquad \frac{\partial Y^*}{\partial \alpha} = \frac{\dfrac{\partial Y^S}{\partial p}\dfrac{\partial X^D}{\partial \alpha} - \dfrac{\partial X^D}{\partial p}\dfrac{\partial Y^S}{\partial \alpha}}{\dfrac{\partial Y^S}{\partial p} - \dfrac{\partial X^D}{\partial p}}.$$
>- With supply sloping up and demand sloping down, the denominator is positive. The sign of $\partial p^*/\partial \alpha$ is the opposite of the sign of the numerator, which is the excess supply that $\alpha$ creates at the old price.
>- A demand shift moves $p^*$ and $Y^*$ in the same direction. A supply shift moves them in opposite directions.
>- Four cases. *Fad*, $\partial X^D/\partial \alpha > 0$: $p^*$ rises. *Recession abroad*, a negative demand shock for domestic firms, $\partial X^D/\partial \alpha < 0$: $p^*$ falls. *Oil shock*, higher import prices, $\partial Y^S/\partial \alpha < 0$: $p^*$ rises. *Computerization*, better technology, $\partial Y^S/\partial \alpha > 0$: $p^*$ falls.
>- A rise in $w$ or $r$ that raises marginal cost shifts supply left. A change in the income distribution shifts demand. [[6.8.3 Elasticities]] gives the unit-free version, and [[6.8.4 Tax Incidence]] applies the formula to a tax.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture asks how $\alpha$ affects both $p^*$ and $Y^*$ but derives only $\partial p^*/\partial \alpha$. The formula for $\partial Y^*/\partial \alpha$ follows from the chain rule, the same step the lecture uses for the quantity effect of a tax.

# Intuition

No one sets the price in a competitive market. At a price above $p^*$, firms want to sell more than consumers want to buy, which is **excess supply**. At a price below $p^*$, consumers want more than firms offer, which is **excess demand**. Only at $p^*$ do the two plans match. The diagram uses the market $Y^S = 25p$ and $X^D = 100 - 50p$. At $p = 1.8$ there are $35$ units of excess supply. At $p = 0.8$ there are $40$ units of excess demand. At $p^* = 4/3$ both sides trade $100/3$ units.

# Properties

#### *(Close the Gap)* The price moves by the gap that the shock opens at the old price, divided by how fast a price change closes it.

At the old price, a shock creates excess supply $(\partial Y^S/\partial \alpha - \partial X^D/\partial \alpha)\,d\alpha$. A price rise of $dp$ adds $(\partial Y^S/\partial p - \partial X^D/\partial p)\,dp$ to excess supply, since firms sell more and consumers buy less. The new equilibrium needs the two to cancel, which is the formula for $\partial p^*/\partial \alpha$. Steep curves give a small denominator, so the same shock moves the price a lot. The formula is local, like all [[6.3.1 Comparative Statics|comparative statics]]. It fails at the kinks and jumps of [[6.8.1 Aggregation]]. The formula needs a nonzero denominator, and the sign rules need a positive one. The denominator is negative if quantity demanded rises with price faster than quantity supplied, $\partial X^D/\partial p > \partial Y^S/\partial p$, as with a strong Giffen good.

#### *(Read Shifts From Data)* Price and quantity rise together after a demand increase and move apart after a supply decrease.

When only demand shifts, $\partial Y^S/\partial \alpha = 0$ and $\partial Y^*/\partial \alpha$ has the sign of $\partial X^D/\partial \alpha$. The economy moves along the supply curve. When only supply shifts, $\partial Y^*/\partial \alpha$ has the sign of $\partial Y^S/\partial \alpha$, and the economy moves along the demand curve. A fad and an oil shock both raise the price, but the fad raises the quantity and the oil shock lowers it. Observed price and quantity data reveal which curve moved.

# Derivation

We want $\partial p^*/\partial \alpha$ and $\partial Y^*/\partial \alpha$. Define $F(p, \alpha) = Y^S(p, \alpha) - X^D(p, \alpha)$, with $w$, $r$, $M$, and other prices fixed. The equilibrium solves $F(p^*, \alpha) = 0$. If $F_p = \partial Y^S/\partial p - \partial X^D/\partial p \ne 0$, the [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] gives $\partial p^*/\partial \alpha = -F_\alpha/F_p$, which is the formula in the callout. For the quantity, $Y^* = Y^S(p^*(\alpha), \alpha)$, so by the chain rule
$$\frac{\partial Y^*}{\partial \alpha} = \frac{\partial Y^S}{\partial p}\frac{\partial p^*}{\partial \alpha} + \frac{\partial Y^S}{\partial \alpha} = \frac{-Y^S_p\,(Y^S_\alpha - X^D_\alpha) + Y^S_\alpha\,(Y^S_p - X^D_p)}{Y^S_p - X^D_p}.$$
The terms $Y^S_p Y^S_\alpha$ cancel, which leaves the callout formula. Using $X^D(p^*(\alpha), \alpha)$ instead gives the same answer.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Five Firms Meet Ten Consumers
>Five firms each supply $5p$ and ten consumers each demand $10 - 5p$, so $AY = 25p$ and $AD = 100 - 50p$.
>
>>[!example]- Find $p^*$, total quantity, and each agent's quantity. If each firm's supply comes from $c(y) = y^2/10$, find each firm's profit.
>>Setting $25p = 100 - 50p$ gives $75p = 100$, so $p^* = 100/75 = 4/3$. Total quantity is $25 \cdot 4/3 = 100/3 \approx 33.3$. Each firm sells $5 \cdot 4/3 = 20/3$, and five firms sell $100/3$. Each consumer buys $10 - 20/3 = 10/3$, and ten consumers buy $100/3$. The price is below $2$, so every consumer buys a positive amount. With $c(y) = y^2/10$, $MC = y/5$, and $p = MC$ gives $y = 5p$ as stated. Profit is $\frac43 \cdot \frac{20}{3} - \frac{(20/3)^2}{10} = \frac{80}{9} - \frac{40}{9} = \frac{40}{9} \approx 4.44$. This profit is positive, and with $J$ fixed nothing removes it.

>[!example] (Medium) A Wage Rise for Coffee Carts
>Forty coffee carts each have cost $c(y) = w\,y^2/100$, where $w$ is the barista wage. Market demand is $X^D = 12000 - 400p$, and $w = 10$.
>
>>[!example]- Find $p^*$ and $Y^*$. Use the implicit function theorem to find $\partial p^*/\partial w$ and $\partial Y^*/\partial w$. Compare with the exact effect of a rise to $w = 11$.
>>Each cart sets $p = MC = wy/50$, so $y = 50p/w$ and $Y^S = 2000p/w$. At $w = 10$, $Y^S = 200p$. Then $200p = 12000 - 400p$ gives $p^* = 20$ and $Y^* = 4000$, or $100$ cups per cart. The derivatives are $\partial Y^S/\partial w = -2000p/w^2 = -400$, $\partial Y^S/\partial p = 200$, and $\partial X^D/\partial p = -400$. So
>>$$\frac{\partial p^*}{\partial w} = -\frac{-400 - 0}{200 + 400} = \frac{2}{3}, \qquad \frac{\partial Y^*}{\partial w} = \frac{200 \cdot 0 - (-400)(-400)}{600} = -266.7.$$
>>The exact solution is $p^* = 30w/(5 + w)$. At $w = 11$ it gives $p^* = 20.625$ and $Y^* = 3750$, changes of $0.625$ and $-250$, close to the linear estimates. At $Y = 4000$ the wage rise lifts MC by $2$ per cup, yet the price rises by only $2/3$. Consumers pay one third of the cost increase. [[6.8.4 Tax Incidence]] explains the split.

>[!example] (Hard) Moving Income Between Groups
>A campus grocery has supply $Y^S = 50p$. One hundred students each spend $20\%$ of income $M^A = 100$ on groceries, and one hundred staff each spend $5\%$ of income $M^B = 400$. The university moves $\tau$ dollars from each staff member to each student.
>
>>[!example]- Find $\partial p^*/\partial \tau$ at $\tau = 0$ and compare with the exact effect of $\tau = 10$.
>>Cobb-Douglas demand gives $X^D = 100 \cdot 0.2\,(M^A + \tau)/p + 100 \cdot 0.05\,(M^B - \tau)/p = (4000 + 15\tau)/p$. At $\tau = 0$, $50p = 4000/p$ gives $p^* = \sqrt{80} = 8.944$ and $Y^* = 447.2$. Supply does not depend on $\tau$, and $\partial X^D/\partial \tau = 15/p = 1.677$. The denominator is $50 + 4000/p^2 = 50 + 50 = 100$. So
>>$$\frac{\partial p^*}{\partial \tau} = -\frac{0 - 1.677}{100} = 0.0168, \qquad \frac{\partial Y^*}{\partial \tau} = 50 \times 0.0168 = 0.84.$$
>>A transfer of $\tau = 10$ is predicted to raise the price by $0.168$. Exactly, $p^{*2} = 4150/50 = 83$, so $p^* = 9.110$, a rise of $0.166$. Total income is unchanged, yet the price rises. Each dollar moved raises student spending by $0.20$ and lowers staff spending by $0.05$. If both groups spent the same share, the transfer would leave $p^*$ unchanged.
