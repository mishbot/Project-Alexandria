>[!note] Thermal Conduction
>**Conduction** is the flow of heat through a material by molecular collisions, with no bulk motion of the material. For a slab of length $L$ and cross-sectional area $A$ whose two faces are held at temperatures $T_1$ and $T_2$, the rate of heat flow in the steady state is
>$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = k\, A\, \frac{T_1 - T_2}{L},$$
>where $k$ is the **thermal conductivity** of the material, in $\text{W}/(\text{m}\cdot\text{K})$. Heat flows from the hot face to the cold face, in proportion to the area, to the temperature difference, and to $1/L$. Doubling the length halves the flow, and doubling the area doubles it. The rate is a power, in watts. Where the temperature varies along the direction of flow, the same law in differential form reads
>$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = -k\, A\, \frac{dT}{dx},$$
>with the minus sign because heat flows down the temperature gradient. The relation is called Fourier's law.
>
>| Material | $k$ ($\text{W}/(\text{m}\cdot\text{K})$) | Material | $k$ ($\text{W}/(\text{m}\cdot\text{K})$) |
>|---|---|---|---|
>| Silver | $420$ | Water | $0.56$ |
>| Copper | $380$ | Human tissue | $0.2$ |
>| Aluminum | $200$ | Wood | $0.08$ to $0.16$ |
>| Steel | $40$ | Fiberglass | $0.048$ |
>| Ice | $2$ | Goose down | $0.025$ |
>| Glass, brick, concrete | $0.84$ | Air | $0.023$ |
>
>Metals conduct hundreds of times better than insulators, and the best insulators are those that trap still air. Two slabs in series carry the same flow, and the temperature at their interface is set by that condition. Insulation is sold by its **R-value** $R = L/k$, the thermal resistance of a unit area, and R-values in series add.

>[!warning] Name of the Law
>Lecture called this law Newton's law of heating in passing. The standard name is Fourier's law. Newton's name belongs to a different law, for cooling by contact with a moving fluid.

# Intuition

Press a hot plate against one end of a cold metal rod. The atoms in the plate vibrate hard, and when they collide with the atoms at the end of the rod they set those vibrating harder too. Those atoms pass the extra motion to the next layer, and the next, and the energy creeps down the rod. Nothing travels but energy. At first the temperature spikes at the hot end and is unchanged everywhere else. As time passes the profile spreads, and after a long wait it settles into a straight line from the hot temperature to the cold one. That straight-line steady state is what the law describes. The slope of the line is $\Delta T / L$, and the flow is proportional to the slope. The diagram shows the profile relaxing toward the line.

The demonstration with a paper cup of water over a flame shows the same idea. The paper next to the flame would burn, but water conducts the heat away and spreads it through the whole cup. No part of the paper gets past $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ until the water is gone. A rod that is half brass and half wood, wrapped in paper and held over a flame, chars only over the wood. The brass carries the heat away into its large heat capacity, and the wood does not.

# Properties

#### *(Steady State Means Constant Flow)* In the steady state the same power crosses every cross-section, because nothing in between stores energy.

Energy is conserved, and a slab that has stopped changing temperature cannot be accumulating energy anywhere inside. Whatever enters one face in a second leaves the other face in the same second. This is the condition that fixes the temperature at the interface of two materials in series: the flow through the first slab equals the flow through the second. It is also why the steady profile in a uniform slab is a straight line. A constant flow with a constant $kA$ needs a constant slope.

#### *(Series Slabs)* Slabs stacked along the flow add their thermal resistances, $L/(kA)$, and the temperature drops divide in proportion.

Write the flow through each slab as $\Delta T_i / R_i$ with $R_i = L_i / (k_i A)$. Equal flows and drops that add up to the total give $dQ/dt = (T_1 - T_2) / \sum R_i$, the same form as resistors in series. The slab with the largest $L/k$ takes most of the temperature drop. A thin layer of fiberglass drops more temperature than a thick brick wall, which is why insulation works. A single material that would carry the same flow with the same total thickness has $k_{\text{eq}} = \sum L_i / \sum (L_i / k_i)$.

#### *(Only the Surface Temperatures Count)* The $T_1$ and $T_2$ in the law are the temperatures of the two faces, not of the air on either side.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not discuss the still-air layers on the faces of a wall. This property follows Giancoli section 19.10.

The face of a window on a winter day is much colder than the room and much warmer than the outdoors. A thin layer of still air clings to each side and carries a large part of the temperature drop. Using the room and outdoor air temperatures in the formula overestimates the flow through glass by a factor of ten or more. The air layers act as extra slabs in series. Their thickness depends on wind and draft, which is one reason [[2.6.2 Convection|convection]] is hard to compute.

#### *(Conductors and Insulators)* The spread in $k$ is a factor of $10^{4}$, and the best insulators are trapped air.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture showed that brass conducts heat far better than wood but did not explain why. It did not discuss why trapped air insulates. This property follows Giancoli section 19.10.

Metals conduct heat through the same free electrons that carry electric current, which is why good electrical conductors are good thermal conductors. Insulators pass heat only by lattice vibrations. Air itself has a tiny $k$, but air that is free to move carries heat by convection, so a good insulator is a solid foam or fiber that holds the air still. Goose down, fiberglass, and foam plastics all work this way. Water has a modest $k$ but a large heat capacity, and a wet garment feels cold because it conducts heat from the skin far better than the trapped air in a dry one.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Heat Through a Window
>A window pane is $1.20\ \text{m}$ by $2.00\ \text{m}$ and $4.0\ \text{mm}$ thick. The inner surface of the glass is $1.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$ warmer than the outer surface.
>
>>[!example]- Find the rate of heat flow through the glass.
>>With $k = 0.84\ \text{W}/(\text{m}\cdot\text{K})$ and $A = 2.40\ \text{m}^2$,
>>$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = kA\,\frac{\Delta T}{L} = (0.84)(2.40)\frac{1.5}{0.0040} = 760\ \text{W}.$$
>>A temperature difference of only $1.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$ across the glass drives $760\ \text{W}$. The room and the outdoors may differ by $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$, and the rest of the drop occurs in the air layers on the two faces, which conduct far worse than glass.

>[!example] (Medium) Replacing Two Insulators
>The wall of an old house has two insulating layers side by side. The outer layer has thickness $L_1$ and conductivity $k_1$. The inner layer has thickness $L_2$ and conductivity $k_2$. The outside is at $T_{\text{out}}$, the inside at $T_{\text{in}}$, and the wall has area $A$. The owner wants to replace both layers with one material of the same total thickness, $L_1 + L_2$. The new wall must let through the same heat flow.
>

>\end{document}

>
>>[!example]- Find the interface temperature $T_i$ and the conductivity $k$ of the single replacement layer.
>>The wall stores no energy, so the heat flowing into the interface each second equals the heat flowing out. Taking the flow from outside to inside,
>>$$\frac{k_1 A}{L_1}\,(T_{\text{out}} - T_i) = \frac{k_2 A}{L_2}\,(T_i - T_{\text{in}}).$$
>>The area cancels. Collecting the $T_i$ terms gives
>>$$T_i = \frac{(k_1/L_1)\,T_{\text{out}} + (k_2/L_2)\,T_{\text{in}}}{k_1/L_1 + k_2/L_2}.$$
>>Substituting $T_i$ back into either side gives the flow through the wall,
>>$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = \frac{A\,(T_{\text{out}} - T_{\text{in}})}{L_1/k_1 + L_2/k_2}.$$
>>A single layer of thickness $L_1 + L_2$ carries $kA\,(T_{\text{out}} - T_{\text{in}})/(L_1 + L_2)$. Setting the two flows equal,
>>$$k = \frac{L_1 + L_2}{L_1/k_1 + L_2/k_2}.$$
>>The interface temperature is a weighted average of the two outer temperatures. It sits closer to the temperature on the side of the better conductor. The next example puts numbers into the same formulas.

>[!example] (Medium) A Composite Wall
>A wall of area $10.0\ \text{m}^2$ has $10.0\ \text{cm}$ of brick ($k = 0.84$) on the outside and $8.0\ \text{cm}$ of fiberglass ($k = 0.048$) on the inside. The inner face is at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and the outer face at $-5\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the rate of heat loss, the temperature at the brick-fiberglass interface, and the conductivity of a single $18\ \text{cm}$ slab that would lose heat at the same rate.
>>The thermal resistances per unit area are $L/k$: for the fiberglass $0.080/0.048 = 1.67\ \text{m}^2\cdot\text{K/W}$ and for the brick $0.100/0.84 = 0.119\ \text{m}^2\cdot\text{K/W}$. In series they add to $1.79$, so the flow per unit area is $25/1.79 = 14.0\ \text{W/m}^2$ and the wall loses
>>$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = (14.0\ \text{W/m}^2)(10.0\ \text{m}^2) = 140\ \text{W}.$$
>>The same flow crosses each layer. The drop across the fiberglass is $(14.0)(1.67) = 23.3\ ^\circ\text{C}$, so the interface is at $20 - 23.3 = -3.3\ ^\circ\text{C}$, and the brick carries the remaining $1.7\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Nearly the whole drop is in the $8\ \text{cm}$ of fiberglass. The equivalent conductivity is
>>$$k_{\text{eq}} = \frac{L_1 + L_2}{L_1/k_1 + L_2/k_2} = \frac{0.18}{1.79} = 0.10\ \text{W}/(\text{m}\cdot\text{K}),$$
>>which is set almost entirely by the insulation.

>>[!example]- How much ice melts per minute once the flow is steady?
>>The ends are at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$, and $k = 380\ \text{W}/(\text{m}\cdot\text{K})$,
>>$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = (380)(1.00 \times 10^{-4})\frac{100}{0.200} = 19.0\ \text{W}.$$
>>In one minute $1140\ \text{J}$ reaches the ice bath. Melting takes $L_F = 333\ \text{kJ/kg}$ of [[2.4.4 Latent Heat|latent heat]], so the mass melted is $1140 / 333\,000 = 3.4\ \text{g}$ per minute. The bath stays at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ while ice remains, so the flow stays steady until the ice is gone. A steel rod of the same size would melt one tenth as much.
