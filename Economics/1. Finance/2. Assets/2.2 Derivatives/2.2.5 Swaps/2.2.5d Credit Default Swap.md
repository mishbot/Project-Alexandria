>[!note] Credit Default Swap
>A **credit default swap** (CDS) moves the default risk of a borrower, the reference entity, from one party to another. The contract names a reference asset, often a [[Corporate Bonds|corporate bond]]. It also fixes the notional $L$, the face amount the protection covers. Other terms are the protection period, the spread, the payment frequency, the credit events, and the settlement method. The two legs are:
>- The **protection buyer** pays the **CDS spread** $s$, a yearly rate on the notional, until maturity or a credit event.
>- The **protection seller** receives these payments and pays the buyer's loss if a credit event occurs before maturity.
>
>A **credit event** is a default that the contract recognizes. Typical credit events are a bankruptcy filing and a missed coupon or principal payment. Acceleration also counts, where lenders demand early repayment of an obligation. So does a repudiation or moratorium, where the issuer rejects or suspends its debts. The spread is quoted in basis points, where one basis point is $0.01\%$. With payments every $\tau$ years, each premium payment is
>$$\text{premium} = sL\tau.$$
>The buyer pays this amount on each payment date, for example with $\tau = 0.25$ for quarterly payments. Let the **recovery rate** $R$ be the reference bond's market value right after default, per unit of face value. The seller's payment at a credit event is
>$$\text{payout} = L(1 - R).$$
>This payment is the part of the face value that the defaulted bond no longer covers. The premiums stop at that date. The contract settles in one of two ways:
>- **Cash settlement** has the seller pay $L(1 - R)$ in cash, with $R$ read from the bond's market price after default.
>- **Physical settlement** has the buyer deliver bonds with face value $L$ and receive $L$ in cash. The bonds are worth $RL$, so the buyer again gains $L(1 - R)$.
>
>A bondholder who buys protection on the same face holds $RL + L(1 - R) = L$ at default. A CDS can cover one issuer, a basket of issuers, or a standard index such as CDX or iTraxx. Today most CDS trades clear through a central clearinghouse. A [[2.2.5c Total Return Swap|total return swap]] passes on an asset's whole return, while a CDS passes on only its default loss.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules list cash and physical settlement but give no payout formula. The recovery rate $R$, the payout $L(1 - R)$, and the results built on them below are standard material beyond the modules.

# Intuition

A bank that lends to a firm carries the risk that the firm defaults. The bank may want to keep the loan and the client while shedding that risk. A CDS lets the bank pay another party a fixed fee each year to take the default loss. Heavy investment in AI data centers has pushed many banks to their regulatory limits on exposure to large tech clients. Buying CDS protection on those clients cuts the risk without cutting off the clients. Other traders buy or sell protection without holding any bonds. They bet on whether the issuer's credit improves or worsens.

# Properties

#### *(Hedging a Bond)* A bond hedged with a CDS behaves like a risk-free bond that may be repaid early at face value.

Take a bond bought at par with coupon rate $c$, and protection on the same face $L$. Before a credit event, the holder receives the coupons and pays the premiums. So it earns a net rate of about $c - s$ per year on $L$. At a credit event, the holder receives $L$ and loses the remaining coupons. The position carries little credit risk, so $c - s$ should be close to the risk-free rate for the same term. The hedge differs from a risk-free bond in three ways:
- The CDS restores the face value only. A coupon that the issuer misses at default is lost.
- The protection lasts only for the CDS's term, which can be shorter than the bond's life.
- The protection seller may itself fail to pay when the issuer defaults.

#### *(Reading the Spread)* The spread is the yearly price of protection per unit of notional, and it rises when the issuer looks riskier.

A spread of $100$ basis points costs $\$10{,}000$ a year per $\$1$ million of notional. Sellers demand a wider spread when default looks more likely or the expected recovery looks lower. So traders read a widening spread as a sign of more credit risk. CDS spreads and CDS index levels may move before stock prices do, so traders watch them as a leading indicator. A buyer's spread stays fixed for the life of its contract. If the market spread later rises, the buyer's contract gains value, and the seller's loses the same amount. The buyer can then sell protection at the new spread and lock in the difference for the remaining term. A trader can profit this way with no default at all.

#### *(Single Names, Baskets, and Indices)* The contracts differ in which defaults trigger a payment and how much of the loss they cover.

The main variants are:
- A **single-name CDS** pays on a credit event of one reference entity.
- A **first-to-default CDS** covers a basket of issuers. It pays on the first default in the basket and then ends. An **$n$th-to-default CDS** pays on the $n$th default instead.
- A **tranche** covers one slice of the basket's total loss. The equity tranche absorbs the first losses, the mezzanine tranche the next ones, and the senior tranche the last.
- An **index CDS** covers a standard list of issuers, such as CDX.NA.IG, CDX.NA.FIN, or the iTraxx series. Index contracts make up most CDS trading worldwide.

A seller of senior-tranche protection loses money only after many issuers in the basket default. A seller of equity-tranche protection pays from the first default. Some contracts also have non-standard payoffs.

#### *(Clearing and Counterparty Risk)* A central clearinghouse protects the buyer against the seller's own default.

Before the 2008 financial crisis, most CDS traded over the counter (OTC), directly between two parties. A buyer then relied on the seller being able to pay at the moment of a credit event. In a crisis many issuers can default together, and sellers may then be short of cash. Today regulators require most CDS trades to pass through a central clearinghouse, such as ICE Clear Credit. The clearinghouse becomes the counterparty to both sides and collects margin from each, as for [[2.2.3 Futures|futures]]. The International Swaps and Derivatives Association (ISDA) reports a record \$41.8 trillion of global CDS trading activity in 2025.

#### *(Default Loss on a Swap)* A defaulted swap costs the solvent party its positive value, and a CDS on the counterparty can cover that loss.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The default loss on a swap is covered only in the homework. Covering that loss with a CDS goes beyond both the lecture notes and the homework.

After a default, the solvent party must replace the swap with a new one at market terms. Its loss is the swap's value $V$ to it on the default date. Here nothing is recovered on that claim. Take a fixed receiver whose counterparty defaults on payment date $T_k$ and misses that day's payment. With the [[2.2.5a Interest Rate Swap|interest rate swap]] valuation, its value is
$$V = L\big(R_S - R_M^{k-1,k}\big)\tau_k + L\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}\big(R_S - R_F^{i-1,i}\big)\tau_i\,\text{DF}_i.$$
The first term is the missed payment, at the floating rate $R_M^{k-1,k}$ set at $T_{k-1}$. The sum values the later payments at the forward rates $R_F^{i-1,i}$. The OIS discount factors $\text{DF}_i$ run from $T_i$ back to $T_k$. A fixed payer uses the same formula with every sign reversed. If $V < 0$, the solvent party owes the defaulted firm and loses nothing. So the loss is
$$\text{loss} = \max(V, 0).$$
The loss is zero when rates have moved enough against the solvent party to make $V$ negative. A CDS on the counterparty with notional $M$ pays $M(1 - R)$, where $R$ is the recovery rate on the counterparty's bonds. A notional of $M = \text{loss}/(1 - R)$ covers the loss. The swap's value moves with rates, so a notional chosen in advance covers the loss only approximately.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Premiums and the Payout
>A pension fund buys 4-year CDS protection on a retailer. The notional is \$32 million and the spread is 165 basis points per year, paid quarterly. Each premium is the spread times the notional times $0.25$, with no compounding. The retailer misses a coupon at 2.5 years, which is a premium date. The fund pays the premium due that day. After the default, the retailer's bonds trade at $38\%$ of face.
>
>>[!example]- Find the quarterly premium, the total premium paid, and the seller's payment under each settlement method.
>>Each premium is
>>$$sL\tau = 0.0165 \times 32{,}000{,}000 \times 0.25 = \$132{,}000.$$
>>Quarterly payments run from 0.25 to 2.5 years, which makes $10$ payments. Ignoring interest, the fund pays $10 \times 132{,}000 = \$1{,}320{,}000$ in total. The recovery rate is $R = 0.38$. Under cash settlement the seller pays
>>$$L(1 - R) = 32{,}000{,}000 \times 0.62 = \$19{,}840{,}000.$$
>>Under physical settlement the fund delivers bonds with face \$32 million and receives \$32 million in cash. If the fund does not own the bonds, it first buys them for $0.38 \times 32{,}000{,}000 = \$12{,}160{,}000$. Either way the fund gains $32{,}000{,}000 - 12{,}160{,}000 = \$19{,}840{,}000$. Net of premiums, it is ahead by $19{,}840{,}000 - 1{,}320{,}000 = \$18{,}520{,}000$.

>[!example] (Medium) Hedging a Bond
>An insurer holds \$18 million face of a 5-year bond that it bought at par. The bond pays a $5.8\%$ coupon semiannually. The insurer buys 5-year protection on the issuer with notional \$18 million at 190 basis points per year. The premiums are paid semiannually on the coupon dates. The 5-year risk-free rate is $3.8\%$. All rates use semiannual compounding. The issuer defaults at 3 years, on a coupon date, and misses that coupon. The insurer pays the premium due that day. The bonds then trade at $25\%$ of face.
>
>>[!example]- Find the insurer's net cash flow before and at the default, and compare its hedged rate with the risk-free rate.
>>Each coupon is $18{,}000{,}000 \times 0.058/2 = \$522{,}000$. Each premium is $18{,}000{,}000 \times 0.019/2 = \$171{,}000$. So the insurer nets $522{,}000 - 171{,}000 = \$351{,}000$ on each of the five dates from 0.5 to 2.5 years. That equals $18{,}000{,}000 \times 0.039/2$, a net rate of $3.9\%$ with semiannual compounding.
>>
>>At 3 years the bonds are worth $0.25 \times 18{,}000{,}000 = \$4{,}500{,}000$. The CDS pays $18{,}000{,}000 \times 0.75 = \$13{,}500{,}000$. Together they give $\$18{,}000{,}000$, the full face. The insurer also pays the premium of \$171,000 and receives no coupon. Counting the bonds at market value, it has $18{,}000{,}000 - 171{,}000 = \$17{,}829{,}000$ at 3 years.
>>
>>The hedged position earns $3.9\%$ and returns the face in every case. The risk-free rate is $3.8\%$, only 10 basis points lower. The two rates are close, as expected for a position with little credit risk. The hedge still lost the \$522,000 coupon due at default.

>[!example] (Hard) A Swap Default Covered by a CDS
>A bank pays a fixed $3.1\%$ and receives 6-month floating on a notional of \$50 million. Payments are semiannual, and both legs use semiannual compounding. The counterparty defaults on a payment date and misses that day's payment. Three later payments are scheduled, at 0.5, 1, and 1.5 years after the default. The floating rate set six months before the default was $3.6\%$. At the default, forward rates for all later periods are $3.8\%$. OIS rates are $3.5\%$ for all maturities, with continuous compounding. The bank recovers nothing on its swap claim. The counterparty's bonds recover $45\%$ of face.
>
>>[!example]- Find the bank's loss, the CDS notional that covers it, and the loss if all forwards had been $2.6\%$.
>>The bank pays fixed, so its net flow on each date is $L(R_M - R_S)\tau$. The missed payment uses the rate set six months earlier,
>>$$50{,}000{,}000 \times (0.036 - 0.031) \times 0.5 = \$125{,}000.$$
>>Each later payment uses the $3.8\%$ forward rate, so it equals $50{,}000{,}000 \times (0.038 - 0.031) \times 0.5 = \$175{,}000$. Discounted with OIS, the three later payments are worth $175{,}000e^{-0.0175} = 171{,}964.14$, $175{,}000e^{-0.035} = 168{,}980.95$, and $175{,}000e^{-0.0525} = 166{,}049.51$. Their sum is $506{,}994.60$. The swap's value to the bank at the default is
>>$$V = 125{,}000 + 506{,}994.60 = \$631{,}994.60.$$
>>The value is positive, so the bank loses all of it.
>>
>>A CDS with notional $M$ pays $M(1 - 0.45) = 0.55M$ at the default. Covering the loss needs $M = 631{,}994.60/0.55 = \$1{,}149{,}081.09$.
>>
>>With forward rates of $2.6\%$, each later net flow is $50{,}000{,}000 \times (0.026 - 0.031) \times 0.5 = -\$125{,}000$. Discounted the same way, the three flows sum to $-362{,}139.00$. Then
>>$$V = 125{,}000 - 362{,}139.00 = -\$237{,}139.00.$$
>>The swap has negative value to the bank, so the bank owes this amount to the defaulted firm. Its loss is $\max(V, 0) = 0$. A CDS notional of \$1.15 million bought in advance would then pay out with no swap loss to cover.
