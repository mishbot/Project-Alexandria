>[!note] Slutsky Equation
>The **Slutsky equation** splits the effect of a price change on demand into a substitution effect and an income effect. Let $x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)$ be the [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|Marshallian demand]] for good $i$ at prices $\mathbf{p} = (p_1, p_2)$ and income $M$. Let $h_i(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$ be the [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization|Hicksian demand]], the cheapest bundle that reaches utility $\bar u$. For a change in the good's own price $p_i$,
>$$\frac{\partial x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)}{\partial p_i} = \frac{\partial h_i(\mathbf{p}, v(\mathbf{p}, M))}{\partial p_i} - x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)\,\frac{\partial x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)}{\partial M}.$$
>The left side is the total effect on the demand the consumer chooses. The first term on the right is the **substitution effect**. It is the change in demand when the price changes and income is adjusted so that the consumer stays on the same indifference curve. The second term is the **income effect**. It is the change in demand caused by the loss of purchasing power. The Hicksian demand is evaluated at $\bar u = v(\mathbf{p}, M)$, the [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function|indirect utility]], so both demands start from the same bundle.
>
>The same decomposition holds for the price $p_j$ of another good,
>$$\frac{\partial x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)}{\partial p_j} = \frac{\partial h_i(\mathbf{p}, v(\mathbf{p}, M))}{\partial p_j} - x_j^*(\mathbf{p}, M)\,\frac{\partial x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)}{\partial M}.$$
>The income term now carries $x_j^*$, the quantity of the good whose price changed. The signs of each term are fixed or depend only on whether good $i$ is [[6.3.3 Income Changes|normal or inferior]]:
>
>| Term | Own price $p_i$ | Other price $p_j$, two goods |
>|---|---|---|
>| Substitution effect | $\le 0$, and $< 0$ at a smooth interior optimum | $\ge 0$, and $> 0$ at a smooth interior optimum |
>| Income effect, $i$ normal | $< 0$ | $< 0$ |
>| Income effect, $i$ inferior | $> 0$ | $> 0$ |
>| Total effect | $< 0$ if $i$ is normal, ambiguous if inferior | ambiguous |
>
>A substitution effect is zero when a small price change does not move the cheapest point on the indifference curve, as at the kink of [[6.4c Perfect Complements|perfect complements]]. A normal good therefore always has a downward-sloping demand curve. An inferior good whose total own-price effect is positive is a [[6.3.4 Price Changes|Giffen good]]. With more than two goods, the cross substitution effect $\partial h_i / \partial p_j$ can take either sign. [[6.3.6 Complements and Substitutes]] uses the signs of the cross effects to classify pairs of goods.

# Intuition

A price cut changes two things at once. The good becomes cheaper relative to other goods, so the consumer trades toward it. The consumer can also afford more of everything, as if income had risen. The Slutsky equation measures these two changes separately. Hicksian demand captures the first one, because it holds utility fixed and so removes the gain in purchasing power. The rest of the Marshallian response is the income effect.

The diagram shows a fall in $p_2$. The consumer starts at $A$. The budget line pivots outward around its $x_1$ intercept, and the consumer moves to $C$ on a higher indifference curve. The dashed line has the new price ratio and exactly enough income to reach the old utility $\bar u$. It touches the old indifference curve at $B$. The move from $A$ to $B$ is the substitution effect, a slide along one indifference curve. The move from $B$ to $C$ is the income effect, a parallel shift of the budget line.

# Properties

#### *(Purchasing Power)* The income term is the quantity bought times the response to income.

A consumer who buys $x_i^*$ units loses $x_i^*\,dp_i$ of purchasing power when $p_i$ rises by $dp_i$. That loss changes demand by $-x_i^*\,dp_i\,\partial x_i^*/\partial M$. Dividing by $dp_i$ gives the income term. The factor $x_i^*$ is the reason income effects are large for goods with a large budget share, such as rent, and small for goods like salt.

#### *(Drawing the Decomposition)* The compensated budget line has the new slope and touches the old indifference curve.

A graph question follows fixed steps. Draw the old budget line and its tangency point $A$. Draw the new budget line and its tangency point $C$. Draw a third line with the new slope $-p_1/p_2'$ and shift it until it touches the old indifference curve at $B$. For good $1$, the horizontal distance from $A$ to $B$ is the substitution effect and from $B$ to $C$ is the income effect. For good $2$, read the vertical distances. The income behind the third line is $e(\mathbf{p}', \bar u)$, the [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization|expenditure function]] at the new prices.

#### *(Giffen Condition)* A Giffen good must be inferior, and its income effect must outweigh its substitution effect.

The total own-price effect is positive only when
$$-x_i^*\,\frac{\partial x_i^*}{\partial M} > \left|\frac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_i}\right|.$$
The left side is positive only for an inferior good. It is large only when $x_i^*$ is large, so the good must also take a big share of the budget. The standard example is potatoes in nineteenth-century Ireland, a cheap staple that took most of a poor household's income. Most inferior goods fail the second condition and still have downward-sloping demand.

#### *(Curvature and Substitution)* The more sharply an indifference curve bends, the smaller the substitution effect.

The substitution effect is a slide along one indifference curve. For [[6.4c Perfect Complements|perfect complements]] the curve is L-shaped, and its corner is the cheapest point at any prices. Hicksian demand then does not respond to prices, so the substitution effect is zero and the whole price effect is an income effect. For [[6.4b Perfect Substitutes|perfect substitutes]] the curve is a straight line, and a small price change can move the consumer from one corner to the other. [[6.4a Cobb-Douglas]] sits between these two cases.

# Derivation

We want to write $\partial x_i^*/\partial p_i$ in terms of Hicksian demand. The starting point is the duality identity from [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]]. The cheapest bundle that reaches $\bar u$ is the bundle a utility maximizer buys with income $e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$:
$$h_i(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)).$$
Differentiate both sides with respect to $p_i$. The right side depends on $p_i$ directly and through income, so by the chain rule
$$\frac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_i} = \frac{\partial x_i^*}{\partial p_i} + \frac{\partial x_i^*}{\partial M}\,\frac{\partial e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)}{\partial p_i}.$$
The last factor comes from the [[7.3 Envelope Theorem|envelope theorem]]. Apply it to the expenditure-minimization Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 - \lambda\,(u(x_1, x_2) - \bar u)$. Only the direct effect of $p_i$ counts, so
$$\frac{\partial e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)}{\partial p_i} = h_i(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)).$$
This step is also known as Shephard's lemma. Substitute it, and set $\bar u = v(\mathbf{p}, M)$ so that $e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = M$:
$$\frac{\partial h_i(\mathbf{p}, v(\mathbf{p}, M))}{\partial p_i} = \frac{\partial x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)}{\partial p_i} + x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)\,\frac{\partial x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)}{\partial M}.$$
Moving the income term to the left side gives the Slutsky equation in the callout. The cross-price version follows the same steps with $p_j$ in place of $p_i$. The envelope theorem then gives $\partial e / \partial p_j = h_j = x_j^*$, which is why $x_j^*$ appears in the income term.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Signs Without Numbers
>For a student on a tight budget, instant noodles are an inferior good. The price of noodles rises.
>
>>[!example]- Give the sign of the substitution effect, the income effect, and the total effect on noodle demand.
>>The substitution effect is negative. Noodles are now more expensive relative to other food, so at fixed utility the student buys fewer. The income effect is $-x^*\,\partial x^*/\partial M$. Noodles are inferior, so $\partial x^*/\partial M < 0$ and the income effect is positive. The student is poorer and falls back on cheap food. The total effect is ambiguous. It is negative unless noodles take a large enough share of the budget for the income effect to be the larger of the two. In that case noodles would be a Giffen good for this student.

>[!example] (Medium) Decomposing a Price Cut
>A consumer has utility $u(x_1, x_2) = x_1^{1/2} x_2^{1/2}$ and income $M = 8$. The price of good $1$ is $p_1 = 1$. The price of good $2$ falls from $p_2 = 2$ to $p_2' = 1$. This is the situation in the diagram above. For this utility the Marshallian demands are $x_1^* = M/(2p_1)$ and $x_2^* = M/(2p_2)$, and the Hicksian demands are $h_1 = \bar u\,(p_2/p_1)^{1/2}$ and $h_2 = \bar u\,(p_1/p_2)^{1/2}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the points $A$, $B$, and $C$, and split the change in each good into substitution and income effects.
>>At the old prices the consumer buys $A = (4, 2)$, with utility $\bar u = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.83$. At the new prices the consumer buys $C = (4, 4)$.
>>
>>The point $B$ is the Hicksian demand at the new prices and the old utility. With $p_1 = p_2' = 1$, both formulas give $h_1 = h_2 = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.83$, so $B = (2.83, 2.83)$. The compensated income is $p_1 h_1 + p_2' h_2 = 5.66$, which is $2.34$ less than $M$.
>>
>>For good $2$, the substitution effect is $2.83 - 2 = +0.83$ and the income effect is $4 - 2.83 = +1.17$. The total is $+2$. For good $1$, the substitution effect is $2.83 - 4 = -1.17$ and the income effect is $4 - 2.83 = +1.17$. The two cancel, and the demand for good $1$ does not change. This matches $x_1^* = M/(2p_1)$, which does not contain $p_2$.

>[!example] (Hard) Checking Slutsky for Cobb-Douglas
>Take $u(x_1, x_2) = x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{1-\alpha}$ with $0 < \alpha < 1$. The Marshallian demands are $x_1^* = \alpha M/p_1$ and $x_2^* = (1-\alpha) M/p_2$. The Hicksian demand for good $1$ is
>$$h_1(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = \bar u \left(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{1-\alpha}.$$
>
>>[!example]- Verify the own-price and cross-price Slutsky equations for good 1.
>>Hicksian demand is proportional to $p_1^{-(1-\alpha)}$ and to $p_2^{1-\alpha}$. Its derivatives are therefore
>>$$\frac{\partial h_1}{\partial p_1} = -(1-\alpha)\,\frac{h_1}{p_1}, \qquad \frac{\partial h_1}{\partial p_2} = (1-\alpha)\,\frac{h_1}{p_2}.$$
>>At $\bar u = v(\mathbf{p}, M)$ the Hicksian and Marshallian demands coincide, so $h_1 = \alpha M/p_1$.
>>
>>For the own price, the substitution effect is $-(1-\alpha)\alpha M/p_1^2$. The income effect is $-x_1^*\,\partial x_1^*/\partial M = -(\alpha M/p_1)(\alpha/p_1) = -\alpha^2 M/p_1^2$. Their sum is
>>$$-\frac{\alpha M}{p_1^2}\,\big[(1-\alpha) + \alpha\big] = -\frac{\alpha M}{p_1^2},$$
>>which equals $\partial x_1^*/\partial p_1$ computed directly from $x_1^* = \alpha M/p_1$.
>>
>>For the cross price, the substitution effect is $(1-\alpha)\,\alpha M/(p_1 p_2) > 0$. The income effect is $-x_2^*\,\partial x_1^*/\partial M = -\big((1-\alpha)M/p_2\big)(\alpha/p_1)$. The two terms are equal in size and opposite in sign, so their sum is $0$. This matches $\partial x_1^*/\partial p_2 = 0$. [[6.3.6 Complements and Substitutes]] reads this result as goods that are net substitutes but neither gross substitutes nor gross complements.
