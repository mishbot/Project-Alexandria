>[!note] Resistivity
>The **resistivity** $\rho$ of a material is the reciprocal of its conductivity:
>$$\rho = \frac{1}{\sigma}.$$
>Here $\sigma$ is the conductivity from [[3.6.2. Current Density]]. In the notes on current and circuits, $\rho$ means resistivity, not volume charge density. Resistivity measures how strongly a material opposes a current. With it, $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ becomes
>$$\vec E = \rho\vec J.$$
>This form gives the field $\vec E$ needed to drive a current density $\vec J$ through the material. Like $\sigma$, $\rho$ depends on the material and its temperature. It does not depend on the size or shape of the object.
>
>The **resistance** $R$ of an object is the potential difference across it divided by the current through it, $R = V/I$. Its unit is the **ohm**, $1\ \Omega = 1\ \text{V/A}$. The EE note [[2.1f.1 Resistor]] treats resistance as a property of a circuit element. A uniform wire of length $L$ and cross-sectional area $A$, with current along its length, has
>$$R = \frac{\rho L}{A}.$$
>Here $\rho$ is the resistivity, $L$ is the length, and $A$ is the area of the cross-section. Resistivity has the unit $\Omega\cdot\text{m}$, and conductivity has the unit $(\Omega\cdot\text{m})^{-1}$.

# Intuition

To resist means to oppose. The resistance of an object measures how much it opposes a current through it. The ending *-ivity* marks a property of a material, as in conductivity. Two copper wires of different lengths have different resistances, but they share one resistivity.

Resistivity comes from collisions. A carrier loses the speed it gained from the field each time it hits an atom. A material with more frequent collisions needs a stronger field to drive the same current density, so its resistivity is larger. A longer wire makes the carriers cross more material, so it has more resistance. A thicker wire puts more carriers side by side, so it has less.

The copper wire of the Easy example doubles in length, then in area, and the bars track $R = \rho L/A$. Last, the wire is drawn out to three times its length, and $R$ rises nine times.

# Derivation

Take a uniform cylinder of material with length $L$ and cross-sectional area $A$. Its ends press against two ideal metal plates, as in [[3.6.1. Electric Current]]. The plates hold a potential difference $V$, and a current $I$ flows along the cylinder. The current spreads evenly over the cross-section, so the current density has size
$$J = \frac{I}{A}.$$
Here $I$ is the current and $A$ is the area it crosses. The resistivity is the same everywhere, so $E = \rho J$ is uniform along the cylinder too. For a uniform field, [[3.4.3. Electric Potential from Electric Field]] gives $V = EL$, so
$$E = \frac{V}{L}.$$
Here $V$ is the potential difference between the ends and $L$ is the length. Substituting both into $E = \rho J$ gives
$$\frac{V}{L} = \rho\,\frac{I}{A}, \qquad V = \frac{\rho L}{A}\,I.$$
The left equation restates $E = \rho J$ with measurable quantities, and the right one solves it for $V$. The potential difference is proportional to the current. The constant $\rho L/A$ is the resistance $R$. It depends on the material through $\rho$ and on the shape through $L$ and $A$. It does not depend on $V$ or $I$.

The unit of $\rho$ follows from $\rho = RA/L$. It is $\Omega\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{m} = \Omega\cdot\text{m}$.

# Properties

#### *(Material Versus Object)* Resistivity belongs to a material, while resistance belongs to one object made of it.

Doubling a wire's length doubles its resistance, because the carriers cross twice as much material. Doubling its cross-sectional area halves the resistance, because twice as many carriers share the current. Neither change affects $\rho$. A problem that reshapes a wire keeps $\rho$ fixed and recomputes $R$. Drawing a wire out to a greater length also shrinks its area, since the volume of metal stays the same. Both changes then raise the resistance. A wire drawn to three times its length has a third of the area and nine times the resistance.

#### *(Hot Metals Resist More)* A metal's resistivity rises with temperature, because hotter atoms hit the carriers more often and harder.

Temperature measures the average energy per particle, as in [[2.3.1 Molecular Interpretation of Temperature]]. In a hot metal the atoms vibrate more. An electron is then more likely to hit one, and it bounces back faster after a hit. Both effects slow the drift, so $\rho$ rises. In a cold metal the atoms vibrate less, and $\rho$ falls. In a classroom demonstration, a wire was connected to a $12\ \text{V}$ battery and a meter that read the current. Dipping the wire in liquid nitrogen raised the current. Heating it in a Bunsen burner flame lowered the current.

#### *(Typical Values)* Resistivities at room temperature span more than twenty powers of ten, from metals to insulators.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture gave no numerical resistivities. The values below come from Giancoli section 25.4, Table 25-1.

Silver and copper have the lowest resistivities, and most wires are made of copper or aluminum. Nichrome has a high resistivity for a metal and is used in heating elements. Glass and hard rubber have resistivities about $10^{17}$ to $10^{23}$ times that of copper. The table lists values at $20^\circ\text{C}$.

| Material | $\rho$ ($\Omega\cdot\text{m}$) |
|---|---|
| Silver | $1.59 \times 10^{-8}$ |
| Copper | $1.68 \times 10^{-8}$ |
| Aluminum | $2.65 \times 10^{-8}$ |
| Tungsten | $5.6 \times 10^{-8}$ |
| Iron | $9.71 \times 10^{-8}$ |
| Nichrome | $1.00 \times 10^{-6}$ |
| Carbon (graphite) | $3 \times 10^{-5}$ to $6 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| Glass | $10^{9}$ to $10^{12}$ |
| Hard rubber | $10^{13}$ to $10^{15}$ |

#### *(A Linear Temperature Model)* Over a modest range of temperature, a metal's resistivity rises in proportion to the temperature change.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture described the temperature dependence only in words. Giancoli section 25.4 gives the linear model and the temperature coefficients.

The model is
$$\rho_T = \rho_0\left[1 + \alpha\,(T - T_0)\right].$$
Here $\rho_0$ is the resistivity at a reference temperature $T_0$, usually $20^\circ\text{C}$, and $\rho_T$ is the resistivity at temperature $T$. The constant $\alpha$ is the temperature coefficient of resistivity, in $\text{K}^{-1}$. A wire's length and area change very little with temperature. Its resistance therefore follows the same rule, $R_T = R_0[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)]$. Platinum has $\alpha = 0.00393\ \text{K}^{-1}$, so its resistance rises about $0.4\%$ per kelvin. This repeatable change makes platinum wire useful in [[2.1.2 Temperature Scales|thermometers]]. Nichrome has $\alpha = 0.0004\ \text{K}^{-1}$, so its resistance changes little as a heater warms up. Carbon has a negative $\alpha$, and its resistivity falls as it warms.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Resistance of an Extension Cord
>One conductor of an extension cord is a copper wire $25\ \text{m}$ long with diameter $1.3\ \text{mm}$. Copper has $\rho = 1.68 \times 10^{-8}\ \Omega\cdot\text{m}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the resistance of the wire and the potential difference along it when it carries $10\ \text{A}$.
>>The area is $A = \pi(0.65 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m})^2 = 1.33 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2$. The resistance is
>>$$R = \frac{\rho L}{A} = \frac{(1.68 \times 10^{-8})(25)}{1.33 \times 10^{-6}} = 0.316\ \Omega.$$
>>Here $L = 25\ \text{m}$ is the length of the conductor. From $R = V/I$, the potential difference is $V = IR = (10\ \text{A})(0.316\ \Omega) = 3.16\ \text{V}$. The cord has two such conductors, out and back. Together they take about $6.3\ \text{V}$ of a $120\ \text{V}$ outlet.

>[!example] (Medium) Aluminum or Copper
>A power line can be made of aluminum or of copper. The two choices must have the same length and the same resistance. Aluminum has $\rho = 2.65 \times 10^{-8}\ \Omega\cdot\text{m}$ and a mass density of $2700\ \text{kg/m}^3$. Copper has $\rho = 1.68 \times 10^{-8}\ \Omega\cdot\text{m}$ and a mass density of $8960\ \text{kg/m}^3$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the ratio of the diameters and the ratio of the masses, aluminum to copper.
>>Equal $R = \rho L/A$ and equal $L$ require equal values of $\rho/A$. So the areas scale with the resistivities:
>>$$\frac{A_{\text{Al}}}{A_{\text{Cu}}} = \frac{\rho_{\text{Al}}}{\rho_{\text{Cu}}} = \frac{2.65}{1.68} = 1.577.$$
>>Here each $A$ is a cross-sectional area and each $\rho$ a resistivity. The area goes as the diameter squared, so the diameter ratio is $\sqrt{1.577} = 1.26$.
>>
>>The mass is the mass density times the volume $AL$. With equal lengths,
>>$$\frac{m_{\text{Al}}}{m_{\text{Cu}}} = \frac{2700}{8960} \times 1.577 = 0.475.$$
>>Here the first factor is the ratio of the mass densities and the second is the ratio of the areas. The aluminum line is $26\%$ thicker but has less than half the mass. Long overhead lines therefore use aluminum.

>[!example] (Hard) Copper Joined to Iron
>A copper wire $2.0\ \text{m}$ long is joined end to end with an iron wire $0.50\ \text{m}$ long. Both have diameter $0.80\ \text{mm}$. A steady current of $1.5\ \text{A}$ flows through both. Copper has $\rho = 1.68 \times 10^{-8}\ \Omega\cdot\text{m}$, and iron has $\rho = 9.71 \times 10^{-8}\ \Omega\cdot\text{m}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field in each wire, the potential difference across each, and the resistance of the whole wire.
>>The same current crosses every cross-section. Both wires have the area $A = \pi(0.40 \times 10^{-3})^2 = 5.03 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^2$, so both have the same current density:
>>$$J = \frac{I}{A} = \frac{1.5}{5.03 \times 10^{-7}} = 2.98 \times 10^{6}\ \text{A/m}^2.$$
>>Here $I$ is the current and $A$ the common area. The fields differ, because $E = \rho J$ and the resistivities differ. In copper, $E_{\text{Cu}} = (1.68 \times 10^{-8})(2.98 \times 10^{6}) = 0.0501\ \text{V/m}$. In iron, $E_{\text{Fe}} = (9.71 \times 10^{-8})(2.98 \times 10^{6}) = 0.290\ \text{V/m}$.
>>
>>Each field is uniform along its wire, so each potential difference is the field times the length. Copper has $V_{\text{Cu}} = (0.0501)(2.0) = 0.100\ \text{V}$, and iron has $V_{\text{Fe}} = (0.290)(0.50) = 0.145\ \text{V}$. The integral of $\vec E$ along the whole wire adds the two parts, so the total is $0.245\ \text{V}$. The resistance of the whole wire is
>>$$R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{0.245\ \text{V}}{1.5\ \text{A}} = 0.163\ \Omega.$$
>>Here $V$ is the total potential difference and $I$ is the current. The iron is a fifth of the length but takes $59\%$ of the potential difference. Adding $\rho L/A$ for each wire gives the same total, $0.0668\ \Omega + 0.0966\ \Omega = 0.163\ \Omega$.
