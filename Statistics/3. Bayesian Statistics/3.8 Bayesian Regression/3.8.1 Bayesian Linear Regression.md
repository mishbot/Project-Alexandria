>[!note] Bayesian Linear Regression
>The **Bayesian linear regression** model is
>$$\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol\beta, \sigma^2 \;\sim\; N(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta,\ \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}_n), \qquad \boldsymbol\beta \mid \sigma^2 \sim N(\boldsymbol\beta_0,\ \sigma^2 \boldsymbol\Lambda_0^{-1}), \qquad \sigma^2 \sim \text{InvGamma}(a_0, b_0),$$
>where $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, and the prior is the **Normal-Inverse-Gamma** (NIG) family scaled by $\sigma^2$. The conjugate posterior is again NIG with
>$$\boldsymbol\Lambda_n = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} + \boldsymbol\Lambda_0, \qquad \boldsymbol\beta_n = \boldsymbol\Lambda_n^{-1}\!\left(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y} + \boldsymbol\Lambda_0 \boldsymbol\beta_0\right),$$
>$$a_n = a_0 + \tfrac{n}{2}, \qquad b_n = b_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\mathbf{y}^\top \mathbf{y} + \boldsymbol\beta_0^\top \boldsymbol\Lambda_0 \boldsymbol\beta_0 - \boldsymbol\beta_n^\top \boldsymbol\Lambda_n \boldsymbol\beta_n\right).$$
>**Marginal posterior for $\boldsymbol\beta$** (integrating out $\sigma^2$):
>$$\boldsymbol\beta \mid \mathbf{y} \;\sim\; t_{2a_n}\!\left(\boldsymbol\beta_n,\ \tfrac{b_n}{a_n}\boldsymbol\Lambda_n^{-1}\right),$$
>a **multivariate Student-$t$** centered at $\boldsymbol\beta_n$ with $2a_n$ degrees of freedom. The scale matrix $b_n \boldsymbol\Lambda_n^{-1}/a_n$ inherits both the design-matrix geometry (through $\boldsymbol\Lambda_n$) and the residual scale $\hat\sigma^2 = b_n/a_n$.
>
>**Posterior predictive** for new inputs $\mathbf{X}_\star$: again multivariate Student-$t$,
>$$\mathbf{y}_\star \mid \mathbf{y} \;\sim\; t_{2a_n}\!\left(\mathbf{X}_\star \boldsymbol\beta_n,\ \tfrac{b_n}{a_n}(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{X}_\star \boldsymbol\Lambda_n^{-1} \mathbf{X}_\star^\top)\right).$$
>The predictive covariance splits into noise variance ($\mathbf{I}$) and parameter-uncertainty variance ($\mathbf{X}_\star \boldsymbol\Lambda_n^{-1} \mathbf{X}_\star^\top$) — the latter grows with distance from the training design, producing natural uncertainty inflation for extrapolation.
>
>**Ridge-regression correspondence.** With prior $\boldsymbol\beta \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 \lambda^{-1} \mathbf{I})$, the posterior mean is
>$$\boldsymbol\beta_n = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y},$$
>exactly the **ridge regression** estimator with penalty $\lambda$. Ridge is the Bayesian MAP under a Gaussian prior; the Bayesian interpretation adds a principled way to choose $\lambda$ (via marginal likelihood) and a credible interval on each coefficient.
>
>**Reference prior limit.** With $\boldsymbol\Lambda_0 \to 0$ and $a_0, b_0 \to 0$ (improper prior $p(\boldsymbol\beta, \sigma^2) \propto 1/\sigma^2$), the posterior mean equals the OLS estimate $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\text{OLS}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$, and the marginal posterior is $\boldsymbol\beta \mid \mathbf{y} \sim t_{n-d}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\text{OLS}},\ \hat\sigma^2 (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1})$ with $\hat\sigma^2 = \mathrm{RSS}/(n-d)$ — numerically identical to the frequentist sampling distribution.

# Intuition

The conjugate analysis works because everything in the model is Gaussian. Writing the log-joint as a function of $\boldsymbol\beta$, both the prior and the likelihood contribute quadratic forms; their sum is another quadratic, completed to identify a Gaussian posterior for $\boldsymbol\beta$ conditional on $\sigma^2$. The variance, $\sigma^2$, has its own Inverse-Gamma conjugate prior, and the two pieces combine into the NIG hierarchy. Integrating $\sigma^2$ out of the joint posterior yields a Student-$t$ marginal for $\boldsymbol\beta$, mirroring the univariate Normal-Inverse-Gamma story in [[3.5.2 Normal-Normal Model]].

The matrix $\boldsymbol\Lambda_n = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} + \boldsymbol\Lambda_0$ is the **posterior precision**: the data contribute their Gram matrix $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$ and the prior contributes $\boldsymbol\Lambda_0$. For ridge, $\boldsymbol\Lambda_0 = \lambda \mathbf{I}$ regularizes directions where the data are information-poor (small eigenvalues of $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$). The prior effectively adds phantom observations that pull $\boldsymbol\beta$ toward $\boldsymbol\beta_0$, and the ratio of prior-precision eigenvalues to data-precision eigenvalues controls how much each coordinate of $\boldsymbol\beta$ is shrunk.

The predictive variance is the crucial Bayesian payoff. Plug-in prediction $\mathbf{X}_\star \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ forgets that $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ itself was estimated; the Bayesian predictive distribution correctly inflates variance by $\mathbf{X}_\star \boldsymbol\Lambda_n^{-1} \mathbf{X}_\star^\top$, which grows for test inputs far from the training design. A regression model is therefore *automatically* more uncertain about extrapolation — no post-hoc confidence-inflation tricks needed.

# Properties

#### *(Ridge as MAP Under Gaussian Prior)* The ridge estimator is the posterior mode under $\boldsymbol\beta \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2/\lambda)$.

Setting $\boldsymbol\beta_0 = \mathbf{0}$ and $\boldsymbol\Lambda_0 = \lambda \mathbf{I}$ yields posterior mean $\boldsymbol\beta_n = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$, the ridge estimator. The Bayesian view explains *why* ridge shrinks: the Gaussian prior puts mass near zero, so coefficients without strong data support are pulled toward zero. The choice of $\lambda$ corresponds to choosing the prior's concentration; cross-validation and Type-II MLE (evidence maximization, see [[3.7 Marginal Likelihood]]) give two principled ways to set it.

#### *(Lasso as MAP Under Laplace Prior)* A Laplace (double-exponential) prior on $\boldsymbol\beta$ yields the LASSO posterior mode.

Taking $\boldsymbol\beta_j \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Laplace}(0, 1/\lambda)$ with density $\propto \exp(-\lambda |\beta_j|)$, the log-posterior is $\mathbf{y}$-fit plus $-\lambda \|\boldsymbol\beta\|_1$; maximizing it recovers the LASSO. The Laplace prior is *not* conjugate to the Gaussian likelihood — the posterior has no closed form, but it admits a data-augmented Gibbs sampler via a scale mixture representation. Practical Bayesian LASSO fits typically use the Park-Casella Gibbs sampler.

#### *(Multivariate Student-t Marginal for $\boldsymbol\beta$)* Integrating $\sigma^2$ out of the NIG posterior yields a Student-$t$ marginal for the coefficient vector.

$$\boldsymbol\beta \mid \mathbf{y} \sim t_{2a_n}\!\left(\boldsymbol\beta_n,\ \tfrac{b_n}{a_n}\boldsymbol\Lambda_n^{-1}\right).$$
Degrees of freedom $2a_n$ grow linearly with $n$; the tails thicken for small $n$ and approach Gaussian as $n \to \infty$. The $j$-th marginal is univariate Student-$t$:
$$\beta_j \mid \mathbf{y} \sim t_{2a_n}\!\left(\beta_{n,j},\ \tfrac{b_n}{a_n}[\boldsymbol\Lambda_n^{-1}]_{jj}\right),$$
whose $(1-\alpha)$ credible interval is $\beta_{n,j} \pm t_{2a_n, 1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{b_n [\boldsymbol\Lambda_n^{-1}]_{jj}/a_n}$. Under the reference prior limit this collapses to the classical $t$-interval.

#### *(Posterior Predictive is Multivariate Student-t)* Predictions at new inputs inherit heavy tails and design-dependent uncertainty.

$$\mathbf{y}_\star \mid \mathbf{y} \sim t_{2a_n}\!\left(\mathbf{X}_\star \boldsymbol\beta_n,\ \tfrac{b_n}{a_n}(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{X}_\star \boldsymbol\Lambda_n^{-1} \mathbf{X}_\star^\top)\right).$$
The predictive variance decomposes as noise variance $\mathbf{I}$ plus parameter-uncertainty variance $\mathbf{X}_\star \boldsymbol\Lambda_n^{-1} \mathbf{X}_\star^\top$. For a test input far from training, $\mathbf{X}_\star \boldsymbol\Lambda_n^{-1} \mathbf{X}_\star^\top$ is large; predictive intervals widen automatically. This is the discrete-grid analog of the increasing posterior variance in [[3.10 Gaussian Processes|GP regression]] away from data.

#### *(Reference-Prior Posterior Equals OLS Sampling Distribution)* The Jeffreys prior limit recovers frequentist inference exactly.

With $\boldsymbol\Lambda_0 = 0$ and $a_0, b_0 \to 0$,
$$\boldsymbol\beta_n = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y} = \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\text{OLS}}, \qquad \boldsymbol\beta \mid \mathbf{y} \sim t_{n-d}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\text{OLS}},\ \hat\sigma^2 (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}),$$
with $\hat\sigma^2 = \mathrm{RSS}/(n-d)$. The Bayesian $(1-\alpha)$ credible interval coincides numerically with the frequentist $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval, though the interpretations differ (see [[3.2 Credible Intervals]]).

#### *(Conjugate Gibbs Sampler)* In non-conjugate extensions, the conditional for $\boldsymbol\beta$ given $\sigma^2$ stays Gaussian, enabling Gibbs.

If $\sigma^2$ has any prior (not necessarily Inverse-Gamma), the conditional $\boldsymbol\beta \mid \sigma^2, \mathbf{y} \sim N(\boldsymbol\beta_n, \sigma^2 \boldsymbol\Lambda_n^{-1})$ remains Gaussian — a full conditional ready for Gibbs. This **conditional conjugacy** is why Bayesian regression is a canonical Gibbs target even when the full joint is not conjugate. See [[3.11.2 Gibbs Sampling]].

#### *(Evidence and Model Comparison)* The marginal likelihood has closed form, enabling Bayesian model selection.

$$p(\mathbf{y}) \;=\; \frac{|\boldsymbol\Lambda_0|^{1/2}}{|\boldsymbol\Lambda_n|^{1/2}} \cdot \frac{b_0^{a_0}}{b_n^{a_n}} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(a_n)}{\Gamma(a_0)} \cdot (2\pi)^{-n/2}.$$
This lets Bayes factors compare nested linear models (whether to include a covariate, polynomial order, etc.) without resorting to BIC. The closed form also enables Type-II MLE tuning of $\boldsymbol\Lambda_0$ (evidence-based choice of ridge penalty). See [[3.7 Marginal Likelihood]].

#### *(Sensitivity to Prior Scale)* Regression coefficient priors must be calibrated to covariate scale.

A prior $\beta_j \sim N(0, 1)$ has very different regularizing force when the covariate $x_{ij}$ ranges over $[-1, 1]$ than when it ranges over $[-1000, 1000]$. Standard practice is to standardize covariates before regression, making a unit-scale prior the default. Alternately, use a scale-invariant hierarchical prior that learns an appropriate per-coefficient scale.

# Examples

>[!example]- Example 1: Closed-Form Posterior for a Single-Predictor Regression
>Data $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ with $x_i$ and $y_i$ centered (so intercept drops out) and $y_i = \beta x_i + \varepsilon_i$, $\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$. Prior $\beta \sim N(0, \sigma^2/\lambda)$ and improper $p(\sigma^2) \propto 1/\sigma^2$.
>
>*Design quantities:* $S_{xx} = \sum x_i^2$, $S_{xy} = \sum x_i y_i$, $S_{yy} = \sum y_i^2$.
>*Posterior for $\beta \mid \sigma^2$:* $N(\beta_n, \sigma^2/(S_{xx} + \lambda))$ with $\beta_n = S_{xy}/(S_{xx} + \lambda)$ — the ridge estimator.
>*Posterior for $\sigma^2$:* $\text{InvGamma}(n/2,\ (S_{yy} - \beta_n S_{xy})/2 + \lambda \beta_n^2/2)$ under the improper prior.
>*Marginal posterior for $\beta$:* $t_n(\beta_n,\ \hat\sigma^2/(S_{xx} + \lambda))$ with $\hat\sigma^2 = b_n/a_n$.
>
>Setting $\lambda = 0$ recovers the frequentist OLS. Increasing $\lambda$ shrinks $\beta_n$ toward zero and narrows the credible interval around the shrunk value — a variance-bias tradeoff made explicit.

>[!example]- Example 2: Predictive Uncertainty for Extrapolation
>Suppose a simple linear regression is fit with $n = 50$ training points $x_i \in [-1, 1]$. Consider predicting at $x_\star = 2$ (outside the training range).
>
>*Plug-in variance:* $\hat\sigma^2 = $ residual variance, giving predictive standard deviation $\sqrt{\hat\sigma^2}$ regardless of $x_\star$ — *wrong*.
>*Bayesian predictive variance:* $\hat\sigma^2(1 + \mathbf{x}_\star^\top \boldsymbol\Lambda_n^{-1} \mathbf{x}_\star)$. For centered $x$ and $\lambda = 0$:
>$$\text{Var}[\tilde y_\star \mid \mathbf{y}] = \hat\sigma^2\!\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{n} + \tfrac{x_\star^2}{\sum x_i^2}\right).$$
>With $n = 50$, $\sum x_i^2 = 16$ (say), and $x_\star = 2$, the variance inflates by a factor $1 + 1/50 + 4/16 = 1.27$ over the plug-in. At $x_\star = 10$, the factor is $1 + 0.02 + 6.25 = 7.27$ — a 2.7-fold increase in predictive standard deviation. Extrapolation uncertainty is automatic.

>[!example]- Example 3: Ridge via Marginal-Likelihood Tuning
>With prior $\boldsymbol\beta \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 \lambda^{-1}\mathbf{I})$ and $\sigma^2 \sim \text{InvGamma}(a_0, b_0)$, the marginal likelihood $p(\mathbf{y} \mid \lambda)$ is a smooth function of $\lambda$. Maximizing it gives an **empirical-Bayes** ridge penalty.
>
>*Recipe:* for each candidate $\lambda$ on a log-grid, compute the closed-form evidence using the formula above and pick the maximizer. This works out to a GCV-like criterion but is principled (the evidence already penalizes complexity — see [[3.7 Marginal Likelihood]]'s BIC property). For a design with $n = 100, d = 10$ and well-conditioned $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$, the evidence-optimal $\hat\lambda$ typically lands between cross-validation's choice and the LOO-CV choice, with similar predictive performance.

# Derivation

Goal: derive the NIG posterior for $(\boldsymbol\beta, \sigma^2)$.

The likelihood times prior is
$$p(\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol\beta, \sigma^2)\, p(\boldsymbol\beta \mid \sigma^2)\, p(\sigma^2) \;\propto\; (\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\|^2\right)$$
$$\times\; (\sigma^2)^{-d/2}|\boldsymbol\Lambda_0|^{1/2}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}(\boldsymbol\beta - \boldsymbol\beta_0)^\top \boldsymbol\Lambda_0(\boldsymbol\beta - \boldsymbol\beta_0)\right) \;\times\; (\sigma^2)^{-a_0 - 1}\exp(-b_0/\sigma^2).$$
Collecting terms quadratic in $\boldsymbol\beta$ inside the exponent:
$$\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\|^2 + (\boldsymbol\beta - \boldsymbol\beta_0)^\top \boldsymbol\Lambda_0(\boldsymbol\beta - \boldsymbol\beta_0) = (\boldsymbol\beta - \boldsymbol\beta_n)^\top \boldsymbol\Lambda_n (\boldsymbol\beta - \boldsymbol\beta_n) + \text{const}(\boldsymbol\beta),$$
by completing the square, with $\boldsymbol\Lambda_n = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} + \boldsymbol\Lambda_0$ and $\boldsymbol\beta_n = \boldsymbol\Lambda_n^{-1}(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y} + \boldsymbol\Lambda_0 \boldsymbol\beta_0)$. The constant-in-$\boldsymbol\beta$ piece is
$$\text{const}(\boldsymbol\beta) = \mathbf{y}^\top \mathbf{y} + \boldsymbol\beta_0^\top \boldsymbol\Lambda_0 \boldsymbol\beta_0 - \boldsymbol\beta_n^\top \boldsymbol\Lambda_n \boldsymbol\beta_n.$$
Substituting:
$$p(\boldsymbol\beta, \sigma^2 \mid \mathbf{y}) \;\propto\; (\sigma^2)^{-(n + d)/2 - a_0 - 1} \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}(\boldsymbol\beta - \boldsymbol\beta_n)^\top \boldsymbol\Lambda_n (\boldsymbol\beta - \boldsymbol\beta_n)\right) \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{b_n}{\sigma^2}\right),$$
which is NIG$(\boldsymbol\beta_n, \boldsymbol\Lambda_n, a_n, b_n)$ with $a_n, b_n$ as stated.

*Marginal posterior for $\boldsymbol\beta$:* integrating out $\sigma^2$ against the Inverse-Gamma marginal of the NIG. The resulting density is $\propto \left[1 + (\boldsymbol\beta - \boldsymbol\beta_n)^\top (b_n \boldsymbol\Lambda_n^{-1}/a_n)^{-1}(\boldsymbol\beta - \boldsymbol\beta_n)/(2 a_n)\right]^{-(2a_n + d)/2}$, the multivariate-$t$ kernel.

*Posterior predictive:* $\mathbf{y}_\star = \mathbf{X}_\star \boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon_\star$ with $\boldsymbol\beta \mid \mathbf{y} \sim t_{2a_n}(\boldsymbol\beta_n, \cdot)$ and $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\star \mid \sigma^2 \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})$. Summing two Student-$t$ dependence structures preserves degrees of freedom and adds scales.
