>[!note] Volatility Trading
>**Volatility trading** takes option positions whose profit depends on the size of the stock's moves. A delta hedge removes the exposure to their direction. Consider a delta-hedged long option, the portfolio $\Pi = f - \Delta S$ with $\Delta = \partial f/\partial S$. The option is priced, and its Greeks are computed, at the implied volatility $\sigma_I$. Let $\sigma_R$ be the realized volatility, the volatility the stock shows over a short interval $(t, t + \delta t)$. The portfolio's profit over that interval, after dividends paid on the short shares and financing at the rate $r$, is
>$$\Pi_{P\&L}(t, t + \delta t) = \frac{1}{2}\Gamma S^2\big(\sigma_R^2 - \sigma_I^2\big)\,\delta t + \mathcal{V}\,\delta\sigma_I + \ldots$$
>Here $\Gamma$ is the option's gamma, $\mathcal{V}$ its vega, and $\delta\sigma_I$ the change in implied volatility over the interval. The dots stand for smaller terms. The profit has two parts.
>- **Gamma P&L** is the first term. It is positive when the stock moves more than the option price assumed, so when $\sigma_R > \sigma_I$.
>- **Vega P&L** is the second term. It is positive for a long option when the market raises the implied volatility.
>
>A delta-hedged long option is a long volatility position. A delta-hedged written option is short volatility and has the opposite profit. A trader makes money by buying volatility cheap, at a $\sigma_I$ below the $\sigma_R$ that follows. Volatility is also traded as an asset class of its own. Traders compare past realized volatility with the implied volatility quoted beforehand, to judge whether options were cheap or expensive.
>- A [[2.3.1a Option Contract|covered call]], long stock plus a short call, is a short volatility position. Call buyers who are not price sensitive can push premiums above their fair level, and the writer keeps the excess.
>- The **VIX** is the Chicago Board Options Exchange (CBOE) Volatility Index. Traders take positions on it through VIX futures and VIX options.
>- A **variance swap** pays the realized variance over a set period minus a fixed strike variance. The realized variance is usually computed from daily log returns.
>
>With a variance notional $N_{\text{var}}$ and a strike variance $K_{\text{var}}$, the variance swap pays at maturity
>$$N_{\text{var}}\big(\sigma_R^2 - K_{\text{var}}\big).$$
>Here $\sigma_R^2$ is the annualized realized variance over the swap's life. [[2.3.5a Implied Volatility]] explains how $\sigma_I$ is read from prices. [[2.3.5b Volatility Surface]] shows how it varies with strike and maturity. This note takes $\sigma_I$ and the [[2.3.4 Greeks]] as inputs and explains how a hedged option gains or loses money.

# Intuition

An option's price contains a forecast of volatility, the implied volatility $\sigma_I$. A trader who expects a different volatility can bet against that forecast. Buying the option alone also bets on the direction of the stock. Shorting $\Delta$ shares per option removes the directional bet over each short interval, as in [[2.3.3d Delta Hedging]]. The hedged position then gains or loses only through the size of the moves. It gains on days with large moves and loses a little on quiet days. Over many days, the gains exceed the losses when $\sigma_R > \sigma_I$.

# Properties

#### *(Breakeven Move)* Each day, the hedged long option gains when the stock moves more than $\sigma_I S\sqrt{\delta t}$ in either direction.

Over one rebalancing interval, $\sigma_R^2 S^2\,\delta t \approx \delta S^2$. So the gamma P&L for one interval can be written in terms of the move,
$$\frac{1}{2}\Gamma\big(\delta S^2 - \sigma_I^2 S^2\,\delta t\big).$$
This is a parabola in $\delta S$ that crosses zero at $\delta S = \pm\sigma_I S\sqrt{\delta t}$, the **breakeven move**. With $\delta t = 1/250$ for one trading day, an implied volatility of $20\%$ gives a breakeven of $20\%/\sqrt{250} = 1.26\%$ of the stock price. On a day with no move, the position loses $\frac{1}{2}\Gamma\sigma_I^2 S^2\,\delta t$. That loss is the time decay net of financing and dividends, because the Black-Scholes-Merton relation gives $\Theta + (r - q)S\Delta - rf = -\frac{1}{2}\sigma_I^2 S^2\Gamma$.

#### *(Path Dependence)* The total gamma P&L weights each day's variance gap by $\frac{1}{2}\Gamma S^2$, which is largest near the strike and near expiry.

Adding the daily terms over the life of the option gives
$$\sum_i \frac{1}{2}\Gamma_i S_i^2\big(\sigma_{R,i}^2 - \sigma_I^2\big)\,\delta t.$$
Here $i$ counts the days. $\Gamma_i$, $S_i$, and $\sigma_{R,i}$ are the gamma, stock price, and realized volatility on day $i$. This note calls the weight $\frac{1}{2}\Gamma S^2$ the dollar gamma. It changes with the stock price and the time left, as the figure shows.

#### *(Vega P&L)* A change in implied volatility changes the option's marked value at once, before any realized volatility accrues.

Vega is the same for a call and a put, $\mathcal{V} = S\sqrt{\tau}\,e^{-q\tau}n(d_1) > 0$, so every long option gains when $\sigma_I$ rises. The gamma P&L builds up day by day. The vega P&L appears as soon as the market changes its quote. A trader can be right about realized volatility and still lose. Take an at-the-money three-month call at $S = \$100$, bought at $\sigma_I = 25\%$, with $r = q = 0$. Its vega is $19.91$, so a cut in implied volatility to $22\%$ costs $\$0.60$ per call. With realized volatility at $27\%$, the gamma P&L at the money earns about $\$0.0066$ per call per day. The cut equals about $90$ trading days of that gain, more than the option's remaining life of about $63$ days. Vega contains $\sqrt{\tau}$, so it shrinks to zero at expiry. An option held to expiry ends at its payoff, whatever $\sigma_I$ is then. Its final profit is the payoff minus the premium plus the hedge's gains. Later quotes of $\sigma_I$ affect that total only through the hedge ratios the trader uses. So the vega P&L matters most to a trader who closes or marks the position before expiry.

#### *(Covered Call)* A covered call carries the gamma and vega of a written call, so its owner is short volatility.

The position is long one share and short one call. The share has a delta of $1$ and no gamma or vega. So the position's Greeks are $\Delta = 1 - e^{-q\tau}N(d_1)$, $-\Gamma$, and $-\mathcal{V}$, where $\Gamma$ and $\mathcal{V}$ belong to the call. It gains when the stock moves less than the call price assumed and when implied volatility falls. Its delta is positive, so it still loses when the stock falls. Call buyers include speculators and hedgers who need protection against a rise. Such buyers may not be price sensitive. Their demand can push the call premium above the level that the later realized volatility justifies. The covered call writer collects that excess premium.

#### *(VIX)* The VIX measures the volatility that S&P 500 index options price over the next 30 days.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules name the VIX, VIX futures, and VIX options. They do not describe how the index is computed or how its futures settle.

The CBOE computes the VIX from the prices of many out-of-the-money S&P 500 puts and calls across strikes. It does not invert the Black-Scholes-Merton formula for one option. The CBOE built the formula so that the squared VIX approximates the fair strike of a 30-day variance swap. A VIX of $20$ means option prices imply a volatility of about $20\%$ per year over the next 30 days. The index is a formula applied to option prices, so no one can buy it directly. Traders use VIX futures, which settle to a value of the index computed on their expiry date, and VIX options. The VIX tends to rise when stock prices fall. This matches the negative correlation between prices and volatility in [[2.3.5b Volatility Surface]].

#### *(Variance Swap)* A variance swap pays for realized variance with equal weight on every day, with no hedge to rebalance.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules give the payoff and say that realized variance usually uses log returns. The exact formula, the vega notional, and the convexity argument are market conventions beyond the modules.

The contract fixes the closing prices $S_0, S_1, \ldots, S_n$ that enter the realized variance,
$$\sigma_R^2 = \frac{252}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\ln\frac{S_i}{S_{i-1}}\right)^2.$$
Here $n$ is the number of daily returns in the swap's life. Term sheets usually use $252$ trading days per year. The formula does not subtract the mean return, unlike the sample estimate in [[3.1f Historical Volatility]]. The strike is quoted as a volatility $\sigma_K$, with $K_{\text{var}} = \sigma_K^2$. It is set so that the swap costs nothing at the start, as for other [[2.2.5 Swaps|swaps]]. Size is often quoted as a vega notional $N_{\text{vega}} = 2\sigma_K N_{\text{var}}$. Near the strike, a one-point rise in $\sigma_R$ then pays about $N_{\text{vega}}$.

# Derivation

>[!warning] Notation
>This note writes $\delta t$, $\delta S$, and $\delta\sigma$ for small changes over one interval, which keeps $\Delta$ for delta. It writes $\mathcal{V}$ for vega, where the lecture writes $v$ in this derivation.

We want the profit of the delta-hedged long option $\Pi = f - \Delta S$ over $(t, t + \delta t)$, with $\Delta$ held fixed during the interval.

The profit sums four parts: the option's change, the short shares' change, the dividends owed, and the financing cost,
$$\Pi_{P\&L}(t, t + \delta t) = \delta f - \Delta\,\delta S - \Delta S q\,\delta t - (f - \Delta S)r\,\delta t.$$
The short seller pays the lender the dividends $qS\,\delta t$ per share. The position ties up the net cash $f - \Delta S$, which costs interest at $r$. For a call, $f < \Delta S$. So the short sale raises more cash than the call costs, and the last term is a gain.

Expand the option's value in a Taylor series in $t$, $S$, and $\sigma$,
$$\delta f = \Theta\,\delta t + \frac{1}{2}\Gamma\,\delta S^2 + \Delta\,\delta S + \mathcal{V}\,\delta\sigma + \ldots$$
The dots hold terms of higher order, such as those in $\delta S\,\delta\sigma$, $\delta S^3$, and $\delta\sigma^2$. The term $\frac{1}{2}\Gamma\,\delta S^2$ stays because $\delta S^2$ is of order $\delta t$ for a stock price.

Substitute the expansion. The terms $\Delta\,\delta S$ cancel, and the terms in $\delta t$ collect into one bracket,
$$\Pi_{P\&L}(t, t + \delta t) = \big(\Theta + (r - q)S\Delta - rf\big)\delta t + \frac{1}{2}\Gamma\,\delta S^2 + \mathcal{V}\,\delta\sigma + \ldots$$

The option is priced at $\sigma = \sigma_I$, so $f$ satisfies the [[2.3.3e Black-Scholes-Merton Equation|Black-Scholes-Merton equation]] with that volatility. In terms of the Greeks it reads $\Theta + (r - q)S\Delta + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_I^2S^2\Gamma = rf$. So the bracket equals $-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_I^2 S^2\Gamma$, and
$$\Pi_{P\&L}(t, t + \delta t) = \frac{1}{2}\Gamma\,\delta S^2 - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_I^2 S^2\Gamma\,\delta t + \mathcal{V}\,\delta\sigma + \ldots$$

Over a short interval the squared move is $\delta S^2 \approx \sigma_R^2 S^2\,\delta t$, where $\sigma_R$ is the realized volatility over $(t, t + \delta t)$. Substituting this, and writing $\delta\sigma_I$ for the change in implied volatility, gives the formula in the callout. The derivation uses only the Taylor expansion and the equation at $\sigma_I$. It does not assume that the stock moves with volatility $\sigma_I$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) One Day of Gamma P&L
>A trader holds $400$ calls on a stock at $\$72$ and keeps them delta-hedged. Each call has $\Gamma = 0.035$, and the calls are priced at an implied volatility of $30\%$. One trading day is $\delta t = 1/250$ of a year. The gamma P&L formula already accounts for interest on the hedge, so no rate is needed.
>
>>[!example]- Find the breakeven daily move and the gamma P&L for a rise of $\$2.10$ and for a move of $\$0.30$.
>>The breakeven move is
>>$$\sigma_I S\sqrt{\delta t} = 0.30 \times 72 \times \sqrt{1/250} = 21.6/15.811 = \$1.3661.$$
>>That is $1.90\%$ of the stock price. For $400$ calls, the gamma P&L is $400 \times \frac{1}{2}(0.035)(\delta S^2 - 1.3661^2) = 7.00\,(\delta S^2 - 1.8662)$.
>>
>>On the $\$2.10$ day, the gamma P&L is $7.00 \times (4.41 - 1.8662) = +\$17.81$. A move of $\$2.10$ in one day matches a realized volatility of $2.10/(72\sqrt{1/250}) = 46.1\%$, well above the implied $30\%$.
>>
>>On the $\$0.30$ day, the gamma P&L is $7.00 \times (0.09 - 1.8662) = -\$12.43$. A day with no move at all would cost $\$13.06$. A fall of $\$2.10$ would earn the same $\$17.81$ as the rise, because only $\delta S^2$ enters.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The factor $252$ in the realized variance and the vega notional below are market conventions beyond the modules.

>[!example] (Medium) Settling a Variance Swap
>A fund buys a one-month variance swap on a stock index. The strike is $\sigma_K = 19\%$, and the vega notional is $\$50{,}000$. The swap covers $21$ trading days. Over those days the sum of the squared daily log returns is $0.004536$. The swap pays no interest before maturity and settles in cash at maturity.
>
>>[!example]- Find the realized volatility and the fund's payoff. Then find the payoff if realized volatility had been equally far below the strike.
>>Annualize the sum with the factor $252/n$,
>>$$\sigma_R^2 = \frac{252}{21} \times 0.004536 = 0.054432, \qquad \sigma_R = 23.33\%.$$
>>In variance points (squared volatility points), $\sigma_R^2 = 544.32$ and $K_{\text{var}} = 19^2 = 361$. The variance notional is $N_{\text{var}} = 50{,}000/(2 \times 19) = \$1{,}315.79$ per variance point. The fund receives
>>$$1{,}315.79 \times (544.32 - 361) = \$241{,}210.53.$$
>>A linear estimate gives $N_{\text{vega}}(\sigma_R - \sigma_K) = 50{,}000 \times (23.3307 - 19) = \$216{,}533$. The swap pays more because its payoff is convex in $\sigma_R$.
>>
>>Now suppose realized volatility had been $19 - 4.3307 = 14.6693\%$. Then $\sigma_R^2 = 215.19$ variance points, and the payoff is $1{,}315.79 \times (215.19 - 361) = -\$191{,}856$. The fund loses less on the way down than it gains on the way up. Its largest possible loss, at zero realized volatility, is $1{,}315.79 \times 361 = \$475{,}000$.

>[!example] (Hard) Splitting One Day of P&L
>A trader buys $1{,}000$ six-month European calls with strike $\$52$ on a stock at $\$50$. The stock has a dividend yield of $1\%$, and the risk-free rate is $4\%$, both per year with continuous compounding. The calls are priced at an implied volatility of $28\%$, and the trader shorts $\Delta$ shares per call. One trading day later, with $\delta t = 1/250$, the stock is at $\$51.40$ and the implied volatility is $29.5\%$.
>
>>[!example]- Split the day's profit into gamma P&L and vega P&L, and compare their sum with the exact profit from repricing.
>>Start with the Greeks at $\tau = 0.5$. With $\sigma_I\sqrt{\tau} = 0.28\sqrt{0.5} = 0.19799$ and $\sigma_I^2/2 = 0.0392$,
>>$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(50/52) + (0.04 - 0.01 + 0.0392)(0.5)}{0.19799} = \frac{-0.03922 + 0.03460}{0.19799} = -0.02334.$$
>>So $N(d_1) = 0.49069$ and $n(d_1) = 0.39883$. Also $d_2 = -0.22133$ and $N(d_2) = 0.41242$. The formulas with a dividend yield give $c = \$3.3910$, $\Delta = e^{-0.005}(0.49069) = 0.48824$, $\Gamma = e^{-0.005}(0.39883)/(50 \times 0.19799) = 0.040087$, and $\mathcal{V} = 50\sqrt{0.5}\,e^{-0.005}(0.39883) = 14.031$.
>>
>>The stock moved $\delta S = 1.40$. The breakeven move is $0.28 \times 50 \times \sqrt{1/250} = 0.885$, so the day beat it. Since $\sigma_I^2S^2\,\delta t = 0.0784 \times 2{,}500/250 = 0.784$, the gamma P&L per call is
>>$$\frac{1}{2}\Gamma\big(\delta S^2 - \sigma_I^2 S^2\,\delta t\big) = \frac{1}{2}(0.040087)(1.96 - 0.784) = 0.02357.$$
>>The vega P&L per call is $14.031 \times 0.015 = 0.21046$. For $1{,}000$ calls these are $\$23.57$ and $\$210.46$, a total of $\$234.03$.
>>
>>Now reprice exactly. At $S = 51.40$, $\tau = 0.496$, and $\sigma = 29.5\%$, the formula gives $c = \$4.3088$. The four parts of the day's profit per call are below.
>>
>>| Part | Formula | Per call |
>>|---|---|---|
>>| Option | $\delta f$ | $+0.91774$ |
>>| Short shares | $-\Delta\,\delta S$ | $-0.68354$ |
>>| Dividends owed | $-\Delta S q\,\delta t$ | $-0.00098$ |
>>| Financing | $-(f - \Delta S)r\,\delta t$ | $+0.00336$ |
>>| Total | | $+0.23658$ |
>>
>>For $1{,}000$ calls the exact profit is $\$236.58$. The decomposition misses by $\$2.55$, which the higher-order terms cover. On this day the vega P&L is about nine times the gamma P&L. A rise of $1.5$ points in implied volatility mattered more than a move of $1.6$ times the breakeven.
