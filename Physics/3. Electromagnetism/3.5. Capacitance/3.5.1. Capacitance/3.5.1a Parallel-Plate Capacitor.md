>[!note] Parallel-Plate Capacitor
>A **parallel-plate capacitor** is two flat conducting plates, each of area $A$, held a distance $d$ apart. With charges $+Q$ and $-Q$, the charge sits on the facing surfaces with density $\sigma = Q/A$. The field between the plates is uniform and points from the positive plate to the negative plate. Its size is
>$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A}.$$
>The potential difference between the plates is
>$$V = Ed = \frac{Qd}{\varepsilon_0 A},$$
>with the positive plate at the higher potential. Dividing $Q$ by $V$ gives the capacitance
>$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}.$$
>Here $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{C}^2/(\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2)$, which is the same as $\text{F/m}$. A larger area or a smaller gap gives a larger capacitance.
>
>The result holds when the plates are much wider than the gap, $\sqrt A \gg d$. Near the edges the field bends outward, as described in [[3.2.2. Electric Field Lines]]. Wide plates make that edge region a small part of the whole.

# Intuition

Hold the potential difference fixed at $V$. The field in the gap is then $V/d$, and the charge density on each plate is $\sigma = \varepsilon_0 E = \varepsilon_0 V/d$. Halving the gap doubles the field and so doubles the charge density. Doubling the area doubles the metal that carries that density. Either change doubles the stored charge at the same voltage.

The plates are the simplest case of the recipe in [[3.5.1. Capacitance]]. The field is uniform, so the integral for the potential difference becomes a product.

The first frames follow the derivation, from Gauss's law to $V = Ed$ to $C = \varepsilon_0 A/d$. Then the Easy example's plates sit on a $9.0\ \text{V}$ supply. Halving the gap or doubling the area doubles the charge.

# Derivation

Put $+Q$ on the upper plate and $-Q$ on the lower plate. The charges spread over the facing surfaces with density $\sigma = Q/A$. [[3.3.3b Parallel Conducting Plates]] found the field between such plates with Gauss's law:
$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A}.$$
The field is uniform and points from the positive plate to the negative plate. Put $x = 0$ on the positive plate and $x = d$ on the negative plate, so the field points along $+x$. The potential difference between them is
$$V(d) - V(0) = -\int_0^d E\,dx = -Ed.$$
The negative plate is lower by $Ed$, so the size of the potential difference is
$$V = Ed = \frac{Qd}{\varepsilon_0 A}.$$
Dividing $Q$ by $V$ gives $C = \varepsilon_0 A/d$. The charge cancels.

# Properties

#### *(Moving Plates)* At fixed charge $V$ grows in proportion to $d$, and at fixed $V$ the charge falls as $1/d$.

An isolated capacitor keeps its charge when its plates move. [[3.3.3b Parallel Conducting Plates]] shows that its field then does not depend on the gap. Pulling the plates twice as far apart halves $C$ and doubles $V = Ed$. With a supply connected, $V$ stays fixed instead. Then $E = V/d$ halves, and the charge $Q = CV$ halves. [[3.5.3. Energy Stored in an Electric Field]] follows the energy in both cases.

#### *(Only the Gap Counts)* The thickness of the plates and the kind of metal do not enter the capacitance.

The charge sits on the facing surfaces, and the field inside the metal is zero. Making a plate thicker adds metal with no field in it. The potential difference comes only from the field in the gap. For the same reason, a metal sheet placed in the gap removes its own thickness from $d$. The Hard example below works this out.

#### *(Capacitance Grows With Size)* Enlarging every length of a capacitor by the same factor multiplies its capacitance by that factor.

Scaling every length by $s$ multiplies $A$ by $s^2$ and $d$ by $s$. The ratio $A/d$, and so $C$, grows by $s$. Every vacuum capacitor has this form, $\varepsilon_0$ times a length built from its dimensions. For one farad with air between the plates, that length is $A/d = C/\varepsilon_0 = 1.1 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m}$. The farad is therefore a very large unit. The Medium example below turns this into a plate size.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Small Air Capacitor
>Two square plates, $12\ \text{cm}$ on a side, sit $0.40\ \text{mm}$ apart in air. They connect to a $9.0\ \text{V}$ supply.
>
>>[!example]- Find the capacitance, the stored charge, and the field between the plates.
>>The area is $A = (0.12)^2 = 0.0144\ \text{m}^2$. The capacitance is
>>$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d} = \frac{(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(0.0144)}{0.40 \times 10^{-3}} = 3.19 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{F} = 319\ \text{pF}.$$
>>The stored charge is $Q = CV = (3.19 \times 10^{-10})(9.0) = 2.87 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{C}$. The field between the plates is
>>$$E = \frac{V}{d} = \frac{9.0}{0.40 \times 10^{-3}} = 2.25 \times 10^{4}\ \text{V/m}.$$

>[!example] (Medium) How Large Is a Farad?
>An air capacitor is to have $C = 1.0\ \text{F}$ with its plates $0.10\ \text{mm}$ apart.
>
>>[!example]- Find the plate area and the side of a square plate.
>>Solving $C = \varepsilon_0 A/d$ for the area gives
>>$$A = \frac{Cd}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{(1.0)(0.10 \times 10^{-3})}{8.85 \times 10^{-12}} = 1.13 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m}^2.$$
>>A square plate would be $\sqrt{1.13 \times 10^{7}} = 3.36 \times 10^{3}\ \text{m}$ on a side, about $3.4\ \text{km}$.

>[!example] (Hard) A Metal Sheet in the Gap
>Plates of area $0.020\ \text{m}^2$ sit $2.0\ \text{mm}$ apart and connect to a $300\ \text{V}$ supply. A flat metal slab $0.50\ \text{mm}$ thick slides into the gap. It stays parallel to the plates and touches neither.
>
>>[!example]- Find the capacitance and the charge before and after. Does the slab's position in the gap matter?
>>Before the slab enters, the capacitance is
>>$$C_0 = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d} = \frac{(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(0.020)}{2.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 88.5\ \text{pF},$$
>>and the charge is $Q_0 = C_0V = 2.66 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{C}$.
>>
>>With the slab in place, follow the recipe with $\pm Q$ on the plates. The slab is a conductor, so the field inside it is zero. The face toward the positive plate takes an induced charge $-Q$, and the face toward the negative plate takes $+Q$. Each air gap then has the field $E = Q/(\varepsilon_0 A)$. Call the two air gaps $d_1$ and $d_2$. Their sum is $d_1 + d_2 = d - t$, with $t$ the slab thickness. Integrating across both gaps gives
>>$$V = E d_1 + E d_2 = \frac{Q(d - t)}{\varepsilon_0 A}.$$
>>Only the sum $d_1 + d_2$ appears, so the slab's position does not matter. The capacitance is
>>$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d - t} = \frac{(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(0.020)}{1.5 \times 10^{-3}} = 118\ \text{pF}.$$
>>The supply now holds $Q = CV = 3.54 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{C}$ on the plates. The field in the air rises from $V/d = 1.5 \times 10^{5}$ to $V/(d-t) = 2.0 \times 10^{5}\ \text{V/m}$.
