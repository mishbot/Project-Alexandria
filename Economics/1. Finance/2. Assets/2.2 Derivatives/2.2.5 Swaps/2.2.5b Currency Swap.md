>[!note] Currency Swap
>A **currency swap** is a contract that exchanges principal and interest in one currency for principal and interest in another. Call the two currencies domestic and foreign, and quote exchange rates in domestic currency per unit of foreign currency. Let $L_D$ and $L_F$ be the two principals and $c_D$ and $c_F$ their coupon rates. Payments fall on dates $T_1, \dots, T_n$, and $\tau_i = T_i - T_{i-1}$ is the accrual fraction (the length of period $i$ in years). Seen by the party that receives domestic interest, the contract has three parts:
>- **The initial exchange** at the start date $T_0$ has the party pay $L_D$ and receive $L_F$.
>- **The coupon exchanges** at each $T_i$ have the party receive $c_D\tau_iL_D$ and pay $c_F\tau_iL_F$.
>- **The final exchange** at $T_n$ reverses the initial one at the same amounts. The party gets $L_D$ back and repays $L_F$.
>
>The principals usually have equal value on the start date, so the initial exchange is worth zero. With $S_{T_0}$ the spot rate (the exchange rate for immediate delivery) on that date, they satisfy
>$$L_D = S_{T_0}L_F.$$
>After the start, $S_{T_0}$ matters only through the two principals. Each leg can pay a fixed or a floating rate, so a swap can be fixed-for-fixed, fixed-for-floating, or floating-for-floating.
>
>Let $t_i$ be a remaining payment date, in years from today. On that date the party receives $C_{D,i}$ in domestic currency and pays $C_{F,i}$ in foreign currency. The last date includes the principals. Let $S_0$ be today's spot rate, and $r$ and $r_f$ the domestic and foreign risk-free rates, continuously compounded. There are two ways to value the swap today:
>- **As two bonds**, the party is long a domestic bond and short a foreign bond. Each bond is valued in its own currency at its own rate. The foreign value is then converted at today's spot rate.
>- **As a strip of FX forwards**, each remaining date is a foreign exchange (FX) forward contract. Under it the party sells $C_{F,i}$ units of foreign currency for $C_{D,i}$. The forward rate $F_i$ for delivery at $t_i$ comes from [[2.2.2d Covered Interest Parity|covered interest parity]].
>
>The bond method gives the value $V$ to the party as
>$$B_D = \sum_{i} C_{D,i}e^{-rt_i}, \qquad B_F = \sum_{i} C_{F,i}e^{-r_ft_i}, \qquad V = B_D - S_0B_F.$$
>Here $B_D$ is in domestic currency and $B_F$ in foreign currency. The forward method gives
>$$V = \sum_{i}\big(C_{D,i} - F_iC_{F,i}\big)e^{-rt_i}, \qquad F_i = S_0e^{(r - r_f)t_i}.$$
>Each term is the domestic value of one exchange at $t_i$, discounted at the domestic rate. The two methods always give the same $V$, and the other party holds $-V$. With annual compounding, $e^{-rt_i}$ becomes $(1 + r)^{-t_i}$ and $F_i = S_0\big[(1 + r)/(1 + r_f)\big]^{t_i}$. When the yield curves are not flat, each date uses its own zero rate. A zero rate is the rate for a single payment on that date. The formulas ignore the risk that either party defaults. An [[2.2.5a Interest Rate Swap|interest rate swap]] exchanges two interest streams in one currency, so its principals cancel and never change hands. A currency swap must exchange them, and the final principal exchange makes its value sensitive to the exchange rate. Like forwards and other swaps, it can be hedged with positions set up once at the start. Options need hedges that are adjusted over time.

>[!warning] Notation for the Principals
>The lecture writes $FX_0$ for the exchange rate on the start date. It prints the principal relation as $N_{\text{€}} = FX_0N_\$$. That form holds only if $FX_0$ is quoted in euros per dollar. These notes quote rates in domestic currency per foreign unit (dollars per euro), so the relation is $L_D = S_{T_0}L_F$, or $N_\$ = FX_0N_{\text{€}}$. The lecture also writes $r_d$ and $r_f$ for the two coupon rates. Here the coupons are $c_D$ and $c_F$, and $r$ and $r_f$ are the discount rates.

# Intuition

Firms often borrow in one currency and earn in another. A US company may find euro lenders who offer it better terms, while its revenue is in dollars. If it keeps the euro loan, a stronger euro raises the dollar cost of every coupon and the repayment. A currency swap removes that risk. The firm passes the borrowed euros to a dealer and receives dollars of equal value. The dealer then pays the firm's euro coupons and euro repayment. In return the firm pays dollar interest and, at the end, the dollar principal.

# Properties

#### *(Converting a Loan)* A firm with a foreign loan can turn it into a domestic loan with the swap.

Let the firm borrow $L_F$ at coupon rate $c_F$. It enters a swap whose foreign leg has the loan's dates and coupon. At $T_0$ it hands $L_F$ to the dealer and receives $L_D = S_{T_0}L_F$. At each $T_i$ the dealer pays the firm $c_F\tau_iL_F$, which the firm passes to its lenders. The firm pays the dealer $c_D\tau_iL_D$. At $T_n$ the dealer returns $L_F$, which repays the loan, and the firm pays $L_D$. Every foreign flow cancels. The firm is left with a domestic loan of $L_D$ at rate $c_D$. Exchange-rate moves no longer change its costs. A firm with foreign revenue can run the same steps in reverse to turn domestic debt into foreign debt. Firms may also borrow abroad because they get better terms there. That comparative-advantage argument works as it does for [[2.2.5a Interest Rate Swap|interest rate swaps]].

#### *(Exchange-Rate Exposure)* The party that pays foreign currency loses when that currency strengthens, mostly through the final exchange.

With interest rates fixed, $V = B_D - S_0B_F$ is a straight line in $S_0$ with slope $-B_F$. A 1% rise in the spot rate changes $V$ by $-0.01\,S_0B_F$. When the foreign bond trades near its principal, that is about 1% of $S_0L_F$. The final exchange makes up most of $B_F$, because it includes the whole principal. In the Hard example below it is 95% of $B_F$. An interest rate swap has no such term, since its principals cancel. Interest rates move the value too. A higher domestic rate lowers $B_D$, and a higher foreign rate lowers $B_F$.

#### *(Today's Spot Rate)* Converting the foreign bond at the start-date rate gives a wrong value and can even flip its sign.

The rate $S_{T_0}$ sets $L_D$ and $L_F$ and plays no other role in the value. Converting $B_F$ at $S_{T_0}$ values the swap as if the currency had not moved since the start. In the Medium example below, the correct value is negative and the start-date rate gives a positive one. The forward method builds each $F_i$ from $S_0$, so this mistake cannot occur there. The discount rates need the same care. The bond method discounts foreign flows at $r_f$. The forward method converts each foreign flow first and then discounts it at $r$.

#### *(Floating Legs)* A currency swap with floating legs equals a fixed-for-fixed currency swap plus one interest rate swap per floating leg.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules list fixed-for-floating and floating-for-floating currency swaps but value only the fixed-for-fixed case. The steps below combine that case with the interest rate swap results.

A floating leg pays its currency's reference rate, set at the start of each period and paid at its end. Suppose that rate and the discount rate come from one curve. Then the leg plus its principal is a floating-rate note (FRN). As in an [[2.2.5a Interest Rate Swap|interest rate swap]], an FRN is worth its principal right after each reset date. Today it is worth the principal plus the next coupon, which is already set, discounted from the next payment date. That value replaces $B_D$ or $B_F$ in the bond method. Take a swap that receives fixed domestic interest and pays floating foreign interest. It equals two contracts. The first is a fixed-for-fixed currency swap that pays the foreign coupon $c_F$. The second is a foreign interest rate swap that receives $c_F$ and pays floating. The interest rate swap exchanges no principal, so the principal flows stay those of the currency swap.

# Derivation

We want to show that both methods give the value of the swap and that they agree. The table lists the flows of the party that receives domestic currency.

| Leg | $T_0$ | $T_i$, $0 < i < n$ | $T_n$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Domestic | $-L_D$ | $+c_D\tau_iL_D$ | $+L_D(1 + c_D\tau_n)$ |
| Foreign | $+L_F$ | $-c_F\tau_iL_F$ | $-L_F(1 + c_F\tau_n)$ |

Start with the bond method. From today on, the domestic row pays coupons at rate $c_D$ and then the face value $L_D$. These are the flows of a domestic bond, so their value today is the present value $B_D$. The foreign row is a short foreign bond, worth $B_F$ units of foreign currency today. A foreign amount held today converts to domestic currency at the spot rate $S_0$. Hence $V = B_D - S_0B_F$. At inception the $T_0$ exchange happens today. It is worth $-L_D + S_{T_0}L_F = 0$, so it does not change $V$.

Now take the forward method. At $t_i$ the party pays $C_{F,i}$ foreign units and receives $C_{D,i}$. This is a short forward on $C_{F,i}$ units with delivery price $K_i = C_{D,i}/C_{F,i}$ per unit. By [[2.2.2c Value of a Forward Contract]], a short forward is worth $(K_i - F_i)e^{-rt_i}$ per unit. For $C_{F,i}$ units the value is
$$C_{F,i}(K_i - F_i)e^{-rt_i} = \big(C_{D,i} - F_iC_{F,i}\big)e^{-rt_i}.$$
The right side is the domestic payment minus the foreign payment valued at the forward rate. Summing over the remaining dates gives the forward formula.

To see that the methods agree, substitute the covered-interest-parity forward rate into the foreign part of one term,
$$F_iC_{F,i}e^{-rt_i} = S_0e^{(r - r_f)t_i}C_{F,i}e^{-rt_i} = S_0C_{F,i}e^{-r_ft_i}.$$
The domestic rate cancels, and the right side is $S_0$ times the present value of $C_{F,i}$ at the foreign rate. Summing over $i$ gives $S_0B_F$, so the forward method returns $B_D - S_0B_F$. With annual compounding the same step reads $S_0\big[(1 + r)/(1 + r_f)\big]^{t_i}(1 + r)^{-t_i} = S_0(1 + r_f)^{-t_i}$. The equality holds date by date, so each exchange has the same value under both methods.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Turning a Euro Loan Into Dollar Debt
>A US manufacturer earns its revenue in dollars. It borrows €60 million for three years at a fixed coupon of 2.8% per year, paid annually. On the same day it enters a three-year currency swap with a dealer, with annual exchanges. The spot rate is 1.12 dollars per euro. In the swap the firm receives 2.8% a year on €60 million. It pays 4.6% a year on the dollar principal. Each coupon is the annual rate times the principal (annual compounding).
>
>>[!example]- Find the dollar principal, list the firm's cash flows in each currency, and state the firm's resulting loan.
>>The principals have equal value at the start, so $L_D = 1.12 \times 60{,}000{,}000 = \$67{,}200{,}000$. The euro coupon is €1,680,000 a year. The dollar coupon is $0.046 \times 67{,}200{,}000 = \$3{,}091{,}200$ a year. The table lists the firm's flows, in euros in the first three rows and in dollars in the last.
>>
>>| Flow | Year 0 | Years 1 and 2 | Year 3 |
>>|---|---|---|---|
>>| Euro loan (€) | $+60{,}000{,}000$ | $-1{,}680{,}000$ | $-61{,}680{,}000$ |
>>| Swap, euro leg (€) | $-60{,}000{,}000$ | $+1{,}680{,}000$ | $+61{,}680{,}000$ |
>>| Net euros (€) | $0$ | $0$ | $0$ |
>>| Swap, dollar leg (\$) | $+67{,}200{,}000$ | $-3{,}091{,}200$ | $-70{,}291{,}200$ |
>>
>>The euro flows cancel on every date. The firm has a three-year dollar loan of $\$67{,}200{,}000$ at 4.6% per year. Its costs no longer depend on the exchange rate. Without the swap, a euro at 1.25 dollars in year 3 would make the final euro payment cost $61{,}680{,}000 \times 1.25 = \$77{,}100{,}000$.

>[!example] (Medium) Valuing an Old Swiss Franc Swap
>A dealer entered a five-year currency swap 3.6 years ago. It receives 5.0% per year on \$36 million and pays 1.5% per year on CHF 32 million, with annual coupons. The principals are swapped back at the end, and two exchanges remain, in 0.4 and 1.4 years. The spot rate is now 1.18 dollars per Swiss franc. Risk-free rates are flat at 4.2% in the US and 0.8% in Switzerland, both with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Value the swap to the dealer and its counterparty, and show the error from converting at the start-date rate.
>>The start-date rate was $36/32 = 1.125$ dollars per franc. The dealer receives $\$1{,}800{,}000$ in 0.4 years and $\$37{,}800{,}000$ in 1.4 years. It pays CHF 480,000 and CHF 32,480,000 on the same dates. The dollar bond is worth
>>$$B_D = 1{,}800{,}000e^{-0.042 \times 0.4} + 37{,}800{,}000e^{-0.042 \times 1.4} = 1{,}770{,}012.60 + 35{,}641{,}443.45 = \$37{,}411{,}456.05.$$
>>The franc bond, discounted at the Swiss rate, is worth
>>$$B_F = 480{,}000e^{-0.008 \times 0.4} + 32{,}480{,}000e^{-0.008 \times 1.4} = 478{,}466.45 + 32{,}118{,}253.56,$$
>>which is CHF 32,596,720.02. Converting at today's spot rate gives the value to the dealer,
>>$$V = 37{,}411{,}456.05 - 1.18 \times 32{,}596{,}720.02 = 37{,}411{,}456.05 - 38{,}464{,}129.62 = -\$1{,}052{,}673.57.$$
>>The counterparty holds $+\$1{,}052{,}673.57$. The franc has risen from 1.125 to 1.18 dollars, and the dealer owes francs, so it has lost value.
>>
>>Converting at the start-date rate instead gives $37{,}411{,}456.05 - 1.125 \times 32{,}596{,}720.02 = +\$740{,}146.03$. That number ignores the franc's rise and has the wrong sign.

>[!example] (Hard) A Forward Strip and a Stronger Euro
>A dealer holds a currency swap with 2.25 years left. It receives 4.0% per year on \$54 million and pays 2.5% per year on €50 million, with annual coupons. Exchanges remain in 0.25, 1.25, and 2.25 years, and the principals return at the last one. The spot rate is 1.16 dollars per euro. Risk-free rates are flat at 4.5% in the US and 2.5% in the euro area, both with annual compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Value the swap as FX forwards and check it with the bond method. Find the change if the euro rises 3%.
>>The dealer receives \$2,160,000 on the first two dates and \$56,160,000 on the last. It pays €1,250,000, €1,250,000, and €51,250,000. Covered interest parity with annual compounding gives the forward rates
>>$$F_i = 1.16\Big(\frac{1.045}{1.025}\Big)^{t_i}.$$
>>They are 1.165618, 1.188361, and 1.211549 dollars per euro. Each euro payment converts to dollars at its forward rate, and the net dollar flow is discounted at $1.045^{-t_i}$. The table uses unrounded forward rates.
>>
>>| $t_i$ | Dollars received | Euros paid $\times F_i$ | Net flow | $1.045^{-t_i}$ | Present value |
>>|---|---|---|---|---|---|
>>| 0.25 | $2{,}160{,}000.00$ | $1{,}457{,}022.00$ | $702{,}978.00$ | $0.989056$ | $695{,}284.68$ |
>>| 1.25 | $2{,}160{,}000.00$ | $1{,}485{,}451.69$ | $674{,}548.31$ | $0.946465$ | $638{,}436.48$ |
>>| 2.25 | $56{,}160{,}000.00$ | $62{,}091{,}880.84$ | $-5{,}931{,}880.84$ | $0.905708$ | $-5{,}372{,}553.69$ |
>>
>>The present values sum to $V = -\$4{,}038{,}832.53$. The two coupon forwards have positive value, because the dollar coupon exceeds the dollar cost of the euro coupon. The final forward has a large negative value. Its forward rate of 1.211549 is well above the start-date rate of $54/50 = 1.08$, which set the principals.
>>
>>For the bond check, $B_D = \$55{,}045{,}303.94$ at 4.5% and $B_F$ = €50,934,600.41 at 2.5%. Then $V = 55{,}045{,}303.94 - 1.16 \times 50{,}934{,}600.41 = 55{,}045{,}303.94 - 59{,}084{,}136.47 = -\$4{,}038{,}832.53$, the same value.
>>
>>A 3% rise takes the spot rate to $1.16 \times 1.03 = 1.1948$. With rates unchanged, $B_D$ and $B_F$ stay the same, so the change in value is
>>$$\Delta V = -0.03 \times 1.16 \times 50{,}934{,}600.41 = -\$1{,}772{,}524.09.$$
>>The new value is $-\$5{,}811{,}356.62$. The final exchange makes up €48,480,285.93 of $B_F$, or 95%. So almost all of the loss comes from the principal the dealer must repay at 2.25 years.
