>[!note] Paden–Kahan Subproblems
>The Paden–Kahan subproblems provide geometric building blocks for analytic inverse kinematics of revolute joints:
>- **Subproblem 1 (PK1): Rotation about a single axis.** Given a unit twist $\xi$ (zero pitch) and points $p,q\in\mathbb{R}^3$, find $\theta$ such that
>$$e^{\hat{\xi}\theta} p = q.$$
>A solution exists if and only if $p$ and $q$ lie in the same plane perpendicular to the rotation axis and are equidistant from that axis. The solution, when it exists, is unique.
>‎ 
>- **Subproblem 2 (PK2): Rotation about two intersecting axes.** Given two intersecting revolute axes $\xi_1,\xi_2$, find $\theta_1,\theta_2$, such that
>$$e^{\hat{\xi}_1 \theta_1} e^{\hat{\xi}_2 \theta_2} p = q.$$
>Geometrically, the problem reduces to the intersection of two circles on a sphere centered at the intersection point of the axes. The number of solutions is 0, 1, or 2.
>‎ 
>- **Subproblem 3 (PK3): Rotation to a specified distance.** Given a revolute axis $\xi$, points $p,q$, and a distance $\delta$, find $\theta$ such that
>$$\| e^{\hat{\xi}\theta} p - q \| = \delta.$$
>This reduces to planar geometry using projections onto a plane perpendicular to the axis. The number of solutions is 0, 1, or 2.
>
>These subproblems can be strategically combined to isolate and solve joint variables sequentially. A common strategy is to apply both sides of the kinematic equation to carefully chosen points on or near joint axes, reducing the unknowns to one of the canonical subproblems.

# Intuition
The Paden–Kahan subproblems work by converting algebraic equations involving matrix exponentials into elementary geometric problems (intersections of circles, arcs on spheres, distance constraints). The key technique is to choose a "witness point"—a specific point in 3D space—and apply both sides of the kinematic equation to it. This collapses the $4\times4$ matrix equation into a $3\times1$ vector equation that has a geometric interpretation (rotation of a point around an axis to a target).

For example, to solve the first joint in $e^{\hat{\xi}_1\theta_1}e^{\hat{\xi}_2\theta_2}\cdots e^{\hat{\xi}_n\theta_n}g_{st}(0) = g_D$, you might choose a point $q$ on the axis of $\xi_2$. Since $e^{\hat{\xi}_2\theta_2}$ fixes points on its own axis, applying both sides to $q$ eliminates $\theta_2$ (and all subsequent variables), reducing to $e^{\hat{\xi}_1\theta_1}q = g_D(\cdots)^{-1}q$, which is PK1.

# Properties

#### (*PK1 Solution*) Project $p$ and $q$ onto the plane perpendicular to the axis $\omega$ through the axis point $r$. The angle $\theta$ is the signed angle between the projected vectors $p' = p - r - \omega\omega^T(p-r)$ and $q' = q - r - \omega\omega^T(q-r)$ in that plane. Explicitly: $\theta = \mathrm{atan2}(\omega \cdot (p' \times q'),\, p' \cdot q')$.

#### (*PK2 Solution*) The two rotations compose as $e^{\hat{\xi}_1\theta_1}e^{\hat{\xi}_2\theta_2}p = q$. By applying $e^{-\hat{\xi}_1\theta_1}$ to both sides: $e^{\hat{\xi}_2\theta_2}p = e^{-\hat{\xi}_1\theta_1}q$. Both sides are constrained to circles on a sphere centered at the intersection point, and the problem reduces to finding their intersection. The number of solutions (0, 1, or 2) depends on whether the circles intersect.

#### (*PK3 Solution*) The distance constraint $\|e^{\hat{\xi}\theta}p - q\| = \delta$ reduces to a single equation in $\theta$ after projecting onto the plane perpendicular to $\omega$. The problem becomes: find where a circle (the orbit of $p$ under rotation) intersects a sphere of radius $\delta$ centered at $q$. This yields 0, 1, or 2 solutions.

#### (*Strategy for Full IK*) For a 6R arm with intersecting wrist axes: (1) use the wrist intersection point to decouple position from orientation, (2) solve for the first 3 joints using PK2 + PK1 (or PK3) to place the wrist point correctly, (3) solve for the last 3 wrist joints using PK1 or PK2 to achieve the desired orientation.
