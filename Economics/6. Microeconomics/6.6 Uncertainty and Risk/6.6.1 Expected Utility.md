>[!note] Expected Utility
>A **lottery** is a random amount of money $M$ that equals $M_i$ with probability $p_i$, for $i = 1, \dots, n$, where $0 \le p_i \le 1$ and $p_1 + \dots + p_n = 1$. The person chooses before she learns which outcome occurs. Under **expected utility** she values the lottery by the probability-weighted average of the utilities of its outcomes:
>$$E(U) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i\,U(M_i).$$
>- $U$ is the utility of money (income or final wealth), with $U' > 0$. The other notes in this section write it as $u$.
>- The **expected value** is $E(M) = \sum_i p_i M_i$ and the **variance** is $V(M) = \sum_i p_i\,(M_i - E(M))^2$.
>- $E(U)$ is not $U(E(M))$. The first averages utilities. The second is the utility of a sure payment equal to the average outcome. The person prefers the sure amount $E(M)$ to the lottery when $U(E(M)) > E(U)$.
>- For a lottery with at least two different outcomes of positive probability, the sign of $U''$ decides the comparison:
>    - $U'' = 0$, so $U(M) = a + bM$: $E(U) = U(E(M))$. The person is indifferent to uncertainty, or **risk neutral**, and ranks lotteries by $E(M)$ alone.
>    - $U'' < 0$ (concave): $E(U) < U(E(M))$. The person dislikes uncertainty, or is **risk averse**.
>    - $U'' > 0$ (convex): $E(U) > U(E(M))$. The person likes uncertainty, or is **risk loving**.
>- Utility over sure bundles is ordinal ([[6.1.3 Utility Functions]]). The utility of money in expected utility is not. $U$ and $a + bU$ with $b > 0$ rank all lotteries the same way, but other increasing transformations can reverse the ranking.
>- Standard example: next year's income is $20$, $25$, or $30$ (recession, slow growth, boom), each with probability $1/3$. Then $E(M) = 25$, $V(M) = 50/3$, and $E(U) = \tfrac13 U(20) + \tfrac13 U(25) + \tfrac13 U(30)$. The person prefers a sure $25$ exactly when $\tfrac12 U(20) + \tfrac12 U(30) < U(25)$.
>
>[[6.6.2 Risk Aversion]] measures how strongly a person dislikes risk. [[6.6.3 Insurance]] and [[6.6.4 Investment in a Risky Asset]] use expected utility to choose coverage and a portfolio.

# Intuition

The consumer model of the earlier sections treats every number as known: income $M$, prices, and the interest rate $r$. Many choices are made before some of these numbers are known. Next year's income depends on whether the economy is in recession, slow growth, or a boom. The person needs a rule that ranks uncertain prospects. Expected utility is that rule. It computes the utility the person would get in each state of the world and averages these utilities with the state probabilities.

The rule extends the [[6.5.3 Intertemporal Choice|two-period model]]. With no savings, the person consumes her income in period 1, so her utility in state $i$ is $U(M_i)$. At period 0 she does not know the state. She weights each $U(M_i)$ by $p_i$, the same way the [[2.5a Expectation|expectation]] of a [[2.1. Random Variables|random variable]] weights its values. When $U$ is curved, the average of the utilities differs from the utility of the average outcome. A person with diminishing marginal utility gains less from a boom than she loses in a recession of the same size, so for her $E(U) < U(E(M))$.

# Properties

#### *(Chord Below the Curve)* Comparing $E(U)$ with $U(E(M))$ compares a point on a chord with a point on the graph of $U$.

Take a lottery that pays $M_1$ or $M_2$ with probability $1/2$ each. The point $(E(M), E(U))$ is the midpoint of the chord joining $(M_1, U(M_1))$ and $(M_2, U(M_2))$. The point $(E(M), U(E(M)))$ lies on the graph directly above or below it. A [[2.2 Convex Functions|concave]] function lies above its chords, so $U(E(M)) > E(U)$. A convex function lies below its chords, and a linear function is its own chord. With probabilities $p$ and $1 - p$, the point $(E(M), E(U))$ moves along the chord to the weighted average, and the same comparison holds. For more than two outcomes the general statement is Jensen's inequality, covered in [[6.6.2 Risk Aversion]].

#### *(Linear Utility Case)* A risk-neutral person maximizes expected money.

With $U(M) = a + bM$ and $b > 0$, linearity of expectation gives $E(U) = a + b\,E(M)$. Ranking lotteries by $E(U)$ is then the same as ranking them by $E(M)$, and the variance plays no role. For a curved $U$, $E(U)$ depends on every outcome and probability. Two lotteries with the same mean can then have different expected utilities, as the Easy example shows. A person who ranks lotteries by $U(E(M))$ also ranks them by the mean alone, so that rule treats every person as risk neutral.

#### *(Dropping the Sure Part)* An outcome equal to the sure alternative cancels from the comparison.

In the income example the sure income $25$ is also one of the three outcomes. Subtracting $\tfrac13 U(25)$ from both sides of $\tfrac13 U(20) + \tfrac13 U(25) + \tfrac13 U(30) < U(25)$ gives $\tfrac13 U(20) + \tfrac13 U(30) < \tfrac23 U(25)$. Multiplying by $3/2$ gives $\tfrac12 U(20) + \tfrac12 U(30) < U(25)$. So preferring a sure $25$ to the three-outcome lottery is the same as preferring it to a fifty-fifty lottery over $20$ and $30$. This reduction lets the chord picture above settle the three-outcome case.

#### *(Utility in Each State)* With savings, the outcome in state $i$ is income plus savings with interest.

In period 1 the person maximizes $U(c^1)$ subject to $c_i^1 \le M_i^1 + (1+r)(M^0 - c^0)$, where $M_i^1$ is her income in state $i$ and $M^0 - c^0$ her savings from period 0. Her expected utility over period-1 consumption is therefore $\sum_i p_i\,U\big(M_i^1 + (1+r)(M^0 - c^0)\big)$. Setting $M^0 - c^0 = 0$ isolates the risk and leaves $\sum_i p_i\,U(M_i^1)$. A choice made before the state is known enters every outcome. Coverage in [[6.6.3 Insurance]] and the stock share in [[6.6.4 Investment in a Risky Asset]] work the same way.

#### *(Only Affine Transformations)* $\tilde U = a + bU$ with $b > 0$ ranks lotteries exactly as $U$ does, and a nonlinear increasing transformation may not.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture did not cover which transformations of $U$ preserve the ranking of lotteries.

Linearity of expectation gives $E(\tilde U) = a + b\,E(U)$, an increasing function of $E(U)$, so the ranking is unchanged. Now take $\tilde U = f(U)$ with $f$ increasing and curved. $\tilde U$ and $U$ rank sure amounts the same way, since both increase with money. They can rank lotteries differently, because $E(f(U)) \ne f(E(U))$ when $f$ is curved. The curvature of $U$ describes the person's attitude to risk, so it cannot be changed freely. For sure bundles curvature carries no meaning, as *(Concavity Is Not Ordinal)* in [[6.1.3 Utility Functions]] states. The Hard example shows a reversal.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Same Mean, Less Spread
>Next year's income is $20$, $25$, or $30$. Under forecast A the probabilities are $(1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$. Under forecast B they are $(1/4, 1/2, 1/4)$. A person with $U(M) = \ln M$ can pick the job that matches either forecast.
>
>>[!example]- Find the mean and variance of income under each forecast. Which does she pick, and what does a risk-neutral person do?
>>Forecast A has $E(M) = 25$ and $V(M) = \tfrac13 \cdot 25 + 0 + \tfrac13 \cdot 25 = 50/3 \approx 16.67$. Forecast B has $E(M) = 0.25 \cdot 20 + 0.5 \cdot 25 + 0.25 \cdot 30 = 25$ and $V(M) = 0.25 \cdot 25 + 0 + 0.25 \cdot 25 = 12.5$. The means are equal and B has the smaller variance.
>>
>>With $U = \ln M$, forecast A gives $E(U) = \tfrac13(\ln 20 + \ln 25 + \ln 30) = 3.2053$. Forecast B gives $E(U) = 0.25 \ln 20 + 0.5 \ln 25 + 0.25 \ln 30 = 3.2087$. She picks B, since it moves probability from the extremes to the mean. A risk-neutral person compares only $E(M) = 25$ and is indifferent.

>[!example] (Medium) Expected Utility or Utility of the Expected Value
>A lottery pays $10$ with probability $1/3$ and $0$ with probability $2/3$. A classmate writes its expected utility as $U(\tfrac13 \cdot 10 + \tfrac23 \cdot 0)$.
>
>>[!example]- Give the correct expression. Then compare it with the classmate's for $U(M) = \sqrt M$, $U(M) = M$, and $U(M) = M^2$.
>>The expected utility is $\tfrac13 U(10) + \tfrac23 U(0)$. The classmate's expression is $U(E(M)) = U(10/3)$, the utility of a sure $3.33$.
>>
>>| $U(M)$ | $E(U) = \tfrac13 U(10) + \tfrac23 U(0)$ | $U(E(M)) = U(10/3)$ | Comparison |
>>|---|---|---|---|
>>| $\sqrt M$ (concave) | $\sqrt{10}/3 = 1.054$ | $1.826$ | $E(U) < U(E(M))$ |
>>| $M$ (linear) | $3.333$ | $3.333$ | equal |
>>| $M^2$ (convex) | $100/3 = 33.33$ | $100/9 = 11.11$ | $E(U) > U(E(M))$ |
>>
>>The pattern matches the sign of $U''$. The classmate's formula depends only on the mean, so it would make every person risk neutral.

>[!example] (Hard) An Increasing Transformation That Changes the Choice
>Ana has $U_A(M) = \sqrt M$. Ben has $U_B(M) = M$, which equals $U_A^2$, an increasing transformation of Ana's utility for $M \ge 0$. Chris has $U_C(M) = 5 + 2\sqrt M$. Each chooses between a sure $3$ and the lottery of the Medium example.
>
>>[!example]- Who picks what, and what does this say about transformations of $U$?
>>Ana compares $\sqrt 3 = 1.732$ with $1.054$ and takes the sure $3$. Chris compares $5 + 2(1.732) = 8.464$ with $5 + 2(1.054) = 7.108$ and also takes the sure $3$. His utility is $5 + 2U_A$, an affine transformation of Ana's, so he ranks every lottery as she does. Ben compares $3$ with $E(M) = 3.333$ and takes the lottery.
>>
>>All three agree on sure amounts, since each utility increases with money. Ben's transformation $U_B = U_A^2$ is convex in $U_A$. It removes Ana's risk aversion and makes Ben risk neutral. Over lotteries, the utility of money is fixed up to $a + bU$ with $b > 0$, and no further.
