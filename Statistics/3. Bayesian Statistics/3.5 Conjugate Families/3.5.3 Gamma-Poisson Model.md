>[!note] Gamma-Poisson Conjugacy
>Let $\lambda > 0$ be an unknown Poisson rate and let $y_1, \dots, y_n$ be i.i.d. Poisson counts. The **Gamma-Poisson conjugate pair** is
>$$\lambda \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta), \qquad y_i \mid \lambda \;\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\; \text{Poisson}(\lambda),$$
>which produces the closed-form posterior
>$$\lambda \mid y \;\sim\; \text{Gamma}\!\left(\alpha + \sum_{i=1}^n y_i,\ \beta + n\right).$$
>The prior hyperparameters update by adding the total event count $\sum y_i$ to the shape $\alpha$ and the total exposure $n$ to the rate $\beta$. The prior is interpretable as having previously observed $\alpha$ events over $\beta$ units of exposure (shape/rate parametrization: mean $\alpha/\beta$, variance $\alpha/\beta^2$).
>
>**Exposure-weighted form.** When observation $i$ has exposure $t_i$ (time, area, population) and $y_i \mid \lambda \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda t_i)$, the posterior becomes $\text{Gamma}(\alpha + \sum y_i,\ \beta + \sum t_i)$. Total events over total exposure.
>
>**Posterior summaries:**
>- **Posterior mean**: $\mathbb{E}[\lambda \mid y] = \dfrac{\alpha + \sum y_i}{\beta + n}$ — a weighted average of the prior mean $\alpha/\beta$ and the MLE $\bar y$ with weights $\beta/(\beta + n)$ and $n/(\beta + n)$.
>- **Posterior mode (MAP)**: $\dfrac{\alpha + \sum y_i - 1}{\beta + n}$ when $\alpha + \sum y_i \ge 1$; equals the MLE $\bar y$ under the improper prior $\text{Gamma}(1, 0)$.
>- **Posterior variance**: $\dfrac{\alpha + \sum y_i}{(\beta + n)^2}$; shrinks like $O(1/n)$.
>
>**Common prior choices:**
>- $\text{Gamma}(1, 0)$ (improper, flat on $\lambda$): posterior is $\text{Gamma}(1 + \sum y_i, n)$; MAP equals MLE.
>- $\text{Gamma}(0.5, 0)$ (improper Jeffreys prior): $p(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{-1/2}$ from Fisher information.
>- $\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ with small $\alpha, \beta$: weakly informative; mean $\alpha/\beta$, effective exposure $\beta$.
>
>**Posterior predictive** for a new count $\tilde y$ over unit exposure:
>$$\tilde y \mid y \;\sim\; \text{NegBin}\!\left(\alpha + \sum y_i,\ \tfrac{\beta + n}{\beta + n + 1}\right),$$
>a Negative Binomial (also called Gamma-Poisson mixture) with variance strictly larger than the plug-in Poisson prediction. The extra variance is *exactly* the amount required to account for remaining uncertainty about $\lambda$, and is the Bayesian account of why raw Poisson models overstate confidence.

# Intuition

Count data from a Poisson process carries information about its rate only through the sum of counts and the total exposure. The Poisson likelihood, written as a function of $\lambda$, is proportional to $\lambda^{\sum y_i} e^{-n\lambda}$ — a $\lambda^a e^{-b\lambda}$ kernel. The Gamma density has exactly that kernel ($\lambda^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta \lambda}$), so multiplying prior by likelihood adds exponents and stays Gamma. Mechanically, the prior contributes $\alpha$ pseudo-events over $\beta$ pseudo-exposure; the data contribute $\sum y_i$ real events over $n$ real units of exposure; the posterior just accumulates both.

The Negative Binomial posterior predictive reveals why Poisson regression models so often underfit dispersion. A frequentist plug-in predicts $\tilde y \sim \text{Poisson}(\hat\lambda)$, which has variance equal to its mean. The Bayesian account, marginalizing over posterior uncertainty about $\lambda$, predicts $\tilde y$ with variance strictly greater than its mean — exactly the **overdispersion** that a practicing analyst sees in real count data (insurance claims, goals per soccer match, radioactive decay counts observed under rate uncertainty). Negative-Binomial regression is, at bottom, Bayesian Gamma-Poisson with the rate-uncertainty baked in.

# Properties

#### *(Posterior Mean as Shrinkage)* The posterior mean is a convex combination of the prior mean and the MLE, weighted by effective sample sizes.

$$\mathbb{E}[\lambda \mid y] = w\, \frac{\alpha}{\beta} + (1-w)\, \bar y, \qquad w = \frac{\beta}{\beta + n}.$$
The prior's effective exposure is $\beta$, the data's exposure is $n$. When $n \ll \beta$, the posterior sits near the prior mean; when $n \gg \beta$, it sits at the MLE. This is the count-data analog of [[3.5.2 Normal-Normal Model]]'s precision-weighted averaging.

#### *(Posterior Predictive is Negative Binomial, Not Poisson)* Predictive distribution inflates variance to account for rate uncertainty.

$$\tilde y \mid y \sim \text{NegBin}\!\left(r,\ p\right), \quad r = \alpha + \sum y_i, \quad p = \tfrac{\beta + n}{\beta + n + 1},$$
with mean $r(1-p)/p = (\alpha + \sum y_i)/(\beta + n) = \mathbb{E}[\lambda \mid y]$ and variance $r(1-p)/p^2$, which exceeds the mean. As $n \to \infty$ and the posterior concentrates at $\hat\lambda$, the Negative Binomial converges to $\text{Poisson}(\hat\lambda)$ and variance inflation vanishes.

#### *(Sequential Updating)* Gamma-Poisson updates are associative in the $(\alpha, \beta)$ parametrization.

After seeing $y^{(1)}$ of size $n_1$ and then $y^{(2)}$ of size $n_2$, the posterior is $\text{Gamma}(\alpha + \sum y^{(1)} + \sum y^{(2)},\ \beta + n_1 + n_2)$, identical to processing all $n_1 + n_2$ observations at once. The same collapse holds for exposure-weighted counts. See [[3.1 Bayesian Framework]]'s *(Sequential Updating)* property.

#### *(Bernstein-von Mises)* As $n \to \infty$, the posterior converges to the Gaussian around the MLE with variance equal to the inverse Fisher information.

The Gamma posterior $\text{Gamma}(\alpha_n, \beta_n)$ has mean $\alpha_n/\beta_n \to \bar y$ and variance $\alpha_n/\beta_n^2 \to \bar y / n$. The Fisher information for a Poisson rate is $I(\lambda) = 1/\lambda$, so the inverse-information variance is $\lambda/n \approx \bar y/n$, matching the posterior variance. The Gamma shape grows linearly with $n$, so the distribution tightens to Gaussian by the central-limit argument applied inside the Gamma family.

#### *(Jeffreys Prior is Gamma$(1/2, 0)$)* The improper prior $p(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{-1/2}$ is the Jeffreys (Fisher-information-induced) prior.

For a Poisson likelihood, $I(\lambda) = 1/\lambda$, so the Jeffreys prior $p(\lambda) \propto \sqrt{I(\lambda)} = \lambda^{-1/2}$ is improper Gamma with $\alpha = 1/2, \beta = 0$. The posterior $\text{Gamma}(1/2 + \sum y_i, n)$ is proper whenever at least one observation exceeds zero. This prior is reparametrization-invariant: the Jeffreys prior for $\lambda$ yields the same inference as the Jeffreys prior for $\sqrt\lambda$.

#### *(Exposure Scales Linearly)* When observation $i$ has known exposure $t_i$ and $y_i \mid \lambda \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda t_i)$, posteriors accumulate both events and exposure.

$$\lambda \mid y \sim \text{Gamma}\!\left(\alpha + \sum_{i=1}^n y_i,\ \beta + \sum_{i=1}^n t_i\right).$$
This is the workhorse form in epidemiology (incidence rates per person-year), quality control (defects per item-hour), and particle-physics counting experiments (events per unit luminosity). The posterior mean is "total events over total exposure," with priors as pseudo-events over pseudo-exposure.

#### *(Sum of Counts is Sufficient)* Only $\sum y_i$ and $n$ (or $\sum t_i$) matter for inference.

By the factorization criterion, $T(y) = (\sum y_i, n)$ is a sufficient statistic for $\lambda$. Two datasets with the same sum and same exposure yield identical posteriors regardless of the individual counts. This is the Poisson-family version of the sufficiency that made the [[3.5.1 Beta-Binomial Model|Beta-Binomial]] collapse to $(y, n)$.

# Examples

>[!example]- Example 1: Storefront Traffic Rate
>A retailer wants to estimate the hourly customer arrival rate $\lambda$ at a new store. A prior elicited from an experienced operations manager is $\lambda \sim \text{Gamma}(10, 2)$, which has prior mean $10/2 = 5$ customers per hour and prior standard deviation $\sqrt{10}/2 \approx 1.58$. Over one full day (8 hours), the store logs $y = 48$ arrivals.
>
>*Posterior:* $\lambda \mid y \sim \text{Gamma}(10 + 48,\ 2 + 8) = \text{Gamma}(58, 10)$.
>*Posterior mean:* $58/10 = 5.8$ customers/hour.
>*Posterior std:* $\sqrt{58}/10 \approx 0.762$.
>*95% equal-tail credible interval* (Gamma quantiles): $[4.40, 7.42]$.
>
>The prior's effective exposure is $\beta = 2$ hours. After 8 hours of data, the posterior mean has shifted from the prior mean of 5.0 toward the observed rate of 48/8 = 6.0, landing at 5.8.
>
>*Posterior predictive for next hour's count:* $\tilde y \mid y \sim \text{NegBin}(58,\ 10/11)$, with mean $58/10 = 5.8$ and variance $58 \cdot 1/11 \cdot 11/10 = 5.8 \cdot 1.1 = 6.38$ — larger than $5.8$ by exactly the factor $1 + 1/(\beta_n) = 11/10$.

>[!example]- Example 2: Defects per Unit (Exposure-Weighted)
>A manufacturing line records defects per batch, where batches differ in size. Batches have sizes $t_1, \dots, t_5 = (100, 120, 95, 140, 110)$ units and observed defect counts $y_1, \dots, y_5 = (3, 4, 2, 5, 2)$. Using a weakly informative prior $\text{Gamma}(1, 50)$ (prior mean $0.02$ defects per unit with low confidence):
>
>*Total events:* $\sum y_i = 16$.
>*Total exposure:* $\sum t_i = 565$.
>*Posterior:* $\lambda \mid y \sim \text{Gamma}(1 + 16,\ 50 + 565) = \text{Gamma}(17, 615)$.
>*Posterior mean:* $17/615 \approx 0.0276$ defects per unit.
>*MLE by comparison:* $16/565 \approx 0.0283$.
>
>The prior's effective exposure of 50 units nudges the posterior mean a hair toward the prior mean of 0.02, but the 565 units of real data dominate. A 95% credible interval on $\lambda$ is approximately $[0.0161,\ 0.0421]$.

>[!example]- Example 3: Conjugate Prior as Pseudo-Data
>A Bayesian analyst asserts prior $\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ without justification. Challenged to explain what this prior "means," the analyst re-expresses it as: *"Before seeing data, I behave as though I had previously seen $\alpha$ events in $\beta$ units of exposure."*
>
>*Verification:* If an improper flat prior $\text{Gamma}(0, 0)$ were updated with imaginary data of $\alpha$ events and $\beta$ exposure, the posterior would be $\text{Gamma}(0 + \alpha,\ 0 + \beta) = \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$ — precisely the stated prior.
>
>*Hyperparameter elicitation consequence:* if an expert is willing to say "my prior rate is around 3 events per hour, and I am about as confident as if I had watched 5 hours of data," then $\alpha/\beta = 3$ and $\beta = 5$, giving $\alpha = 15$ and $\beta = 5$. This pseudo-data framing is often easier to elicit than a raw $(\alpha, \beta)$ pair.

# Derivation

Goal: derive the $\text{Gamma}(\alpha + \sum y_i, \beta + n)$ posterior.

The Gamma prior density is
$$p(\lambda) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \lambda^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta \lambda} \;\propto\; \lambda^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta\lambda}.$$
The Poisson likelihood for $n$ i.i.d. observations is
$$p(y \mid \lambda) = \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^{y_i}}{y_i!} \;\propto\; \lambda^{\sum y_i} e^{-n\lambda}.$$
Multiplying prior and likelihood:
$$p(\lambda \mid y) \;\propto\; \lambda^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta\lambda} \cdot \lambda^{\sum y_i} e^{-n\lambda} \;=\; \lambda^{(\alpha + \sum y_i) - 1} e^{-(\beta + n)\lambda},$$
the kernel of $\text{Gamma}(\alpha + \sum y_i,\ \beta + n)$. The normalizing constant follows automatically. For exposure-weighted observations, the likelihood becomes $\prod_i (\lambda t_i)^{y_i} e^{-\lambda t_i}/y_i! \propto \lambda^{\sum y_i} e^{-\lambda \sum t_i}$, and the posterior hyperparameters become $(\alpha + \sum y_i,\ \beta + \sum t_i)$.

*Posterior predictive:* for new $\tilde y$ with unit exposure,
$$p(\tilde y \mid y) = \int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^{\tilde y}}{\tilde y!}\, p(\lambda \mid y)\, d\lambda.$$
With $p(\lambda \mid y) = \text{Gamma}(\alpha_n, \beta_n)$, the integral is a Gamma-Poisson mixture, closed-form as $\text{NegBin}(\alpha_n,\ \beta_n/(\beta_n + 1))$ in the standard stopping-time parametrization.
