>[!note] Perfect Substitutes
>Two goods are **perfect substitutes** when the consumer trades them at a fixed rate, whatever amounts the consumer already holds. Their utility function is linear,
>$$u(x_1, x_2) = \alpha x_1 + \beta x_2, \qquad \alpha, \beta > 0,$$
>where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the utility from one unit of good 1 and one unit of good 2. One unit of good 1 replaces $\alpha/\beta$ units of good 2. The [[6.1.4 Indifference Curves and MRS|MRS]] is the constant $-\alpha/\beta$, so indifference curves are parallel straight lines. At prices $\mathbf{p} = (p_1, p_2)$ and income $M$, the consumer spends all income on the good with the higher marginal utility per dollar. The [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|Marshallian demand]] for good 1 is
>$$x_1^*(\mathbf{p}, M) = \begin{cases} M/p_1 & \text{if } p_1/p_2 < \alpha/\beta \quad (\alpha/p_1 > \beta/p_2), \\ \text{any value in } [0, M/p_1] & \text{if } p_1/p_2 = \alpha/\beta, \\ 0 & \text{if } p_1/p_2 > \alpha/\beta, \end{cases}$$
>and $x_2^* = (M - p_1x_1^*)/p_2$ uses the rest of the budget. The optimum is a [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions|corner solution]] except at the price ratio $p_1/p_2 = \alpha/\beta$, where every bundle on the budget line is optimal. The Lagrangian tangency condition cannot locate the optimum. The [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function|indirect utility]] and the [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization|expenditure function]] follow from the same comparison,
>$$v(\mathbf{p}, M) = M\max\left(\frac{\alpha}{p_1}, \frac{\beta}{p_2}\right), \qquad e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = \bar u\min\left(\frac{p_1}{\alpha}, \frac{p_2}{\beta}\right),$$
>where $p_1/\alpha$ is the cost of one unit of utility bought through good 1. The Hicksian demand is $h_1(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = \bar u/\alpha$ if $p_1/\alpha < p_2/\beta$ and $h_1 = 0$ if $p_1/\alpha > p_2/\beta$, with any split at equality. Perfect substitutes are the $\rho = 1$ case of [[6.4d CES Utility|CES utility]] and the opposite extreme from [[6.4c Perfect Complements|perfect complements]]. They model the same product sold by different sellers.

# Intuition

Some goods do exactly the same job. A driver who needs gasoline does not care which station's pump it comes from. A carpenter does not care which store sold an identical box of nails. Each unit of a good always adds the same amount of utility, so mixing the two brings no benefit. Every dollar goes to the good that delivers more utility per dollar. Price sensitivity is at its most extreme. A tiny difference in price moves all spending from one good to the other.

The diagram uses $u = 2x_1 + x_2$, $p_1 = 3$, $p_2 = 1$ and $M = 6$. A dollar spent on good 1 buys $2/3$ unit of utility. A dollar spent on good 2 buys $1$. The budget line is steeper than the indifference lines. Moving along the budget line from $A$ toward $B$ therefore crosses lower and lower indifference lines. The consumer chooses the corner $A = (0, 6)$ with $u = 6$. The other corner $B = (2, 0)$ gives only $u = 4$.

# Properties

#### *(Tangency Fails)* The Lagrangian first-order conditions have no solution except at one price ratio.

The conditions $u'_{x_i} = \lambda p_i$ read $\alpha = \lambda p_1$ and $\beta = \lambda p_2$. Together they require $\alpha/\beta = p_1/p_2$, a condition that contains no quantities. At any other price ratio no bundle satisfies them, and the maximum sits where a constraint $x_i \ge 0$ binds. At that one ratio every bundle on the budget line satisfies them. On an exam, compute utility at the two corners $(M/p_1, 0)$ and $(0, M/p_2)$ and pick the larger. This compares $\alpha M/p_1$ with $\beta M/p_2$, which is the marginal-utility-per-dollar rule. The method of [[7.2 Lagrange Multipliers]] ignores the constraints $x_i \ge 0$, so it cannot find a corner.

#### *(Jumping Demand)* Demand for good 1 is discontinuous in its own price at $\bar p_1 = \alpha p_2/\beta$.

Plot the [[6.3.4 Price Changes|demand curve]] with $p_1$ on the vertical axis. Above $\bar p_1$, demand is zero. Below $\bar p_1$, demand follows the curve $x_1^* = M/p_1$. At $\bar p_1$ itself, demand is the whole interval $[0, M/\bar p_1]$, a horizontal segment. A one-cent cut from slightly above $\bar p_1$ to slightly below it moves demand from $0$ to about $M/\bar p_1$. Real buyers of near-identical products often split their purchases. They do so for reasons outside this model, such as travel distance or habit.

#### *(Units Set the Weights)* The ratio $\alpha/\beta$ converts units of good 1 into units of good 2.

Identical goods sold in the same unit have $\alpha = \beta$, and the rule reduces to buying the cheaper one. Identical goods sold in different units do not. Take the same water in 1-liter and 0.5-liter bottles. Utility in liters is $u = x_1 + 0.5x_2$, and the consumer compares prices per liter, $p_1$ against $2p_2$. The bottle with the lower sticker price may lose this comparison. Before applying the rule, write both goods in a common unit of the service they provide.

#### *(All-or-Nothing Substitution)* The substitution effect is zero for price changes that do not cross the switch point and total for those that do.

Hicksian demand is $\bar u/\alpha$ or $0$, and it does not move while $p_1/\alpha$ and $p_2/\beta$ keep their order. A consumer who buys only good 1 and faces a small rise in $p_1$ therefore has a zero substitution effect. The whole fall in $x_1^* = M/p_1$ is an income effect in the [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]]. When the price ratio crosses $\alpha/\beta$, the cheapest bundle jumps from one axis to the other. Perfect substitutes allow more substitution than any other family, but all of it happens at a single price ratio.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Oat Milk From Two Stores
>A student has $M = 18$ to spend on 1-liter cartons of the same oat milk. Store 1 sells a carton for $p_1 = 3$ and store 2 for $p_2 = 2.25$. Utility is $u = 4x_1 + 4x_2$, where $x_i$ is cartons from store $i$.
>
>>[!example]- Which bundle does the student buy: $(6, 0)$, $(0, 6)$, or $(0, 8)$?
>>The weights are equal, so the student buys only from the cheaper store. Store 2 gives $4/2.25 \approx 1.78$ units of utility per dollar, against $4/3 \approx 1.33$ at store 1. The student buys $x_2^* = 18/2.25 = 8$ cartons, so the bundle is $(0, 8)$ with $u = 32$.
>>
>>The bundle $(6, 0)$ buys from the expensive store. The bundle $(0, 6)$ divides income by the wrong price. The weight $4$ plays no role, since any common weight $\alpha = \beta$ gives the same choice.

>[!example] (Medium) Large Eggs or Small Eggs
>A baker cares only about the total weight of egg. A large egg holds $60$ g and costs $p_1 = 0.33$. A small egg holds $40$ g and costs $p_2 = 0.20$. The baker has $M = 12$, and utility in grams is $u = 60x_1 + 40x_2$.
>
>>[!example]- What does the baker buy? Below what price of large eggs does the choice switch, and what happens at that price?
>>Large eggs give $60/0.33 \approx 181.8$ g per dollar. Small eggs give $40/0.20 = 200$ g per dollar. The baker buys only small eggs, $x_2^* = 12/0.20 = 60$, for $2400$ g. A large egg is cheaper per gram only if $60/p_1 > 200$, that is, if $p_1 < 0.30$.
>>
>>At $p_1 = 0.30$ both eggs give $200$ g per dollar. Every bundle with $0.30x_1 + 0.20x_2 = 12$ gives $2400$ g, for example $(20, 30)$ or $(40, 0)$. At $p_1 = 0.29$ the baker buys only large eggs, $x_1^* = 12/0.29 \approx 41.4$. A large egg still costs more than a small one, yet it gives more egg per dollar. The comparison must use price per gram. A two-cent change in $p_1$, from $0.31$ to $0.29$, moves demand for large eggs from $0$ to about $41$.

>[!example] (Hard) The Wedding Cake
>The same baker must deliver a cake that needs $3000$ g of egg. Prices start at $p_1 = 0.33$ and $p_2 = 0.20$. Then the price of small eggs rises to $p_2' = 0.25$.
>
>>[!example]- What is the cheapest way to get $3000$ g before and after the rise? How much does the switch save?
>>This is an expenditure-minimization problem with $\bar u = 3000$. The cost of one gram is $0.33/60 = 0.0055$ through large eggs and $0.20/40 = 0.005$ through small eggs. Before the rise, $e = 3000 \times 0.005 = 15$, and the baker buys $75$ small eggs.
>>
>>After the rise, a gram through small eggs costs $0.25/40 = 0.00625$, more than through large eggs. So $e = 3000 \times 0.0055 = 16.50$, and the baker buys $50$ large eggs. The old bundle of $75$ small eggs would now cost $75 \times 0.25 = 18.75$, so the switch saves $2.25$. Hicksian demand for small eggs falls from $75$ to $0$ after a $25\%$ price rise. Suppose instead $p_2$ had risen only to $0.21$, below the switch price $0.22$. The baker would then still buy $75$ small eggs, for $15.75$, and there would be no substitution.
