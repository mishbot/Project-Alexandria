>[!note] Finite (Galois) Fields
>A **Galois field**, also known as a **finite field**, is a set of elements equipped with arithmetic operations, such that the result of any operation is always an element of the set. 
>
>More particularly, a finite field with $p$ elements is denoted $\mathbb{F}_p$ or $\mathrm{GF}(p)$, where $p$ is a **prime number** or a **power of a prime**. In the case where $p$ is prime, the field consists of the integers
>$$\{0, 1, 2, \dots, m - 1\},$$
>with all arithmetic performed [[4.1 Modular Arithmetic|modulo]] $m$.
>
>The number of polynomials of degree at most $d$ over a finite field $\mathbb{F}_m$ is $m^{d+1}$, since there are m choices for each of the $d+1$ coefficients. When specifying a polynomial by values at $d+1$ distinct points, there are also $m^{d+1}$ possibilities.

# Intuition
The defining feature of a Galois field is that it contains only a finite number of elements, yet all standard arithmetic operations are still valid. This structure allows for consistent algebraic manipulation in settings where only a limited number of values are available, such as polynomial arithmetic modulo a prime.

# Properties
#### (*Counting Theorem*) The number of polynomials of degree at most $d$ over a finite field $\mathbb{F}_p$ is $p^{d+1}$.


# Examples
>[!example]  (Medium) Polynomial Arithmetic Over a Finite Field
>Let $f(x)=3x^2+5x+2$ and $g(x)=x+4$, such that both polynomials are in $\mathbb{F}_{7}$.
>>[!example]- Evaluate $f(0), f(1), f(2), f(3)$.
>>We have $f(0)=2$, $f(1)=10 \equiv 3 \pmod{7}$, $f(2)=24 \equiv 3 \pmod{7}$, $f(3)=44 \equiv 2 \pmod{7}$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute $h(x)=(f \cdot g)(x)$. What is $h(6)$?
>>We have that $(f \cdot g)(x)=3x^3+17x^2+22x+8 \equiv 3x^3+3x^2+x+1$. 
>>
>>Then, $h(6)=3 \cdot 6 \cdot 36 + 3 \cdot 36 + 7 \equiv 4 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 1 + 0 \equiv 7 \equiv 0 \pmod{7}$. Therefore, $h(6)=0$ in $\mathbb{F}_{7}$.
>
>Now, let $f$ and $g$ be some polynomials in $\mathbb{F}_{p}$.
>>[!example]- We say a polynomial $f = 0$ if $\forall x, f(x) = 0$. Show that if $f \cdot g = 0$, it is not always true that either $f = 0$ or $g = 0$.
>>Let's work over $F_{2}$. Then, let $f(x)=x$ and $g(x)=x+1$. Then, $(f \cdot g)(x)=x^2+x$, which evaluates to $0$ for all values in $\mathbb{F}_{2}$.
>
>>[!example]- How many $f$ of degree exactly $d < p$ are there such that $f(0) = a$ for some fixed $a \in \{ 0, 1, \ldots, p−1 \}$?
>>To have a polynomial of degree $d$, it must hold that $c_{d} > 0$. Since all coefficients must be in the set of residuals $\{ 0, 1, \ldots, p-1 \}$, then there are $p$ values for the first $d$ coefficients (including the constant), and exactly $p-1$ (excluding the zero) values for the leading coefficient. Therefore, the number of functions of degree $d$ must be $(p-1)p^d$.
>
>>[!example]- Consider $\operatorname{GF}(5)$. How many polynomials of degree at most $4$ are there that fit through $3$ points?
>>Polynomials of degree $\le 4$ can be made using $5$ points maximum. As such, there are $p^2=5^2=25$ possibilities for the other two unspecified coefficients. This includes polynomials of degrees $2$ and $3$.
>>
>>For a more intuitive explanation, consider the polynomial,
>>$$p(x) = c_{4}x^4 + c_{3}x^3 + \ldots + a.$$
>>
>>Then, if $c_{4}=0$ and $c_{3}=0$, $p(x)$ is a degree-2 polynomial. There is one unique degree-2 polynomial that passes through $3$ points.
>>
>>If $c_{3} \ne 0$ (and $c_{4} = 0$), then there are $p-1$ options for the leading coefficient for a degree-3 polynomial. This leaves us with $4$ choices.
>>
>>If $c_{4} \ne 0$ (and $c_{3} \in \{ 0, \ldots,  \}$), then there are $p-1$ options for $c_{4}$ and $p$ options for $c_{3}$, which is $(p-1)p$ polynomials in total. In this case, $20$.
>>
>>Therefore, there are exactly $1+4+20=25$ polynomials of degree at most $4$ that fit through $3$ points in $\operatorname{GF}(5)$

>
>First, assume we have polynomials $p_{-1}(x)$, $p_{0}(x)$, and $p_{1}(x)$ satisfying:
>$$\begin{align}
>p_{-1}(0) &\equiv p_{-1}(1) \equiv 0 \pmod{5}; & p_{-1}(-1) &\equiv 1 \pmod{5} \\
>p_{0}(-1) &\equiv p_{0}(1) \equiv 0 \pmod{5}; & p_{0}(0) &\equiv 1 \pmod{5} \\
>p_{1}(-1) &\equiv p_{1}(0) \equiv 0 \pmod{5}; & p_{1}(1) &\equiv 1 \pmod{5}
>\end{align}$$
>

>>It holds that $p(x)=3p_{-1}(x) + p_{0}(x) + 2p_{1}(x)$.
>
>>[!example]- Find $p_{-1}(x)$. In other words, find a degree 2 polynomial that has roots at x = 0 and x = 1 and
