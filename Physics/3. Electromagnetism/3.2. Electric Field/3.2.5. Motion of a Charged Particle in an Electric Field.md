>[!note] Motion of a Charged Particle in an Electric Field
>A particle of charge $q$ and mass $m$ in an electric field $\vec E$ feels the force $\vec F = q\vec E$. If no other force matters, [[1.2.1. Newton's Laws|Newton's second law]] gives its acceleration:
>$$\vec a = \frac{q\vec E}{m}.$$
>A positive charge accelerates along the field and a negative charge against it. The motion then follows from mechanics:
>- In a **uniform field**, the acceleration is constant. The particle moves like a projectile under gravity, with $q\vec E/m$ in place of $\vec g$.
>- In a **nonuniform field**, the acceleration changes from place to place, and Newton's second law becomes a differential equation.
>
>A standard nonuniform case has two fixed charges $+Q$ a distance $D$ apart. A charge $q < 0$ of mass $m$ sits on their perpendicular bisector. At distance $x$ from the midpoint, the force points back toward the midpoint, with
>$$m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -\frac{2kQ\,|q|\,x}{\left(x^2 + (D/2)^2\right)^{3/2}}.$$
>For small displacements, $|x| \ll D/2$, this is [[1.6.1. Simple Harmonic Motion|simple harmonic motion]] with angular frequency
>$$\omega^2 = \frac{2kQ\,|q|}{m\,(D/2)^3} = \frac{16kQ\,|q|}{mD^3}.$$

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not treat motion in a uniform field, such as an electron deflected between two plates. The first bullet above, the first Intuition paragraph, and the Easy and Hard examples follow Giancoli section 21.10.

# Intuition

The field tells the particle how hard to accelerate, per unit charge. The particle's own charge and mass decide the rest. Electrons have a tiny mass, so modest fields give them enormous accelerations. A field of $500\ \text{N/C}$ accelerates an electron at about $10^{14}\ \text{m/s}^2$. The electric force on an electron in a field of $100\ \text{N/C}$ is about $10^{12}$ times its weight. Gravity can be ignored for electrons and protons in almost every problem.

The oscillating charge on the bisector works like a mass on a spring. Displace it to one side and both fixed charges pull it back toward the middle. It overshoots, gets pulled back from the other side, and keeps going. Near the middle the restoring force is proportional to the displacement, which defines simple harmonic motion. Farther out the force stops growing in proportion, so the motion is still periodic but no longer simple harmonic.

The animation shows a negative bead between two fixed positive charges. The green force always points toward the middle. The graph of $x$ against $t$ traces a sine curve.

# Derivation

Put the fixed charges $+Q$ at $(0, \pm D/2)$ and the moving charge $q < 0$ at $(x, 0)$. The Hard example in [[3.1.5. Superposition Principle]] found the net force. The $y$-components cancel by symmetry, so the particle stays on the $x$-axis. Newton's second law along $x$ reads
$$m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -\frac{2kQ\,|q|\,x}{\left(x^2 + (D/2)^2\right)^{3/2}}.$$
For $|x| \ll D/2$, drop $x^2$ next to $(D/2)^2$ in the denominator, as in the small-angle approximation for a pendulum:
$$\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} \approx -\frac{2kQ\,|q|}{m\,(D/2)^3}\,x.$$
This has the form $d^2x/dt^2 = -\omega^2 x$. The coefficient of $x$ is $\omega^2$, and $(D/2)^3 = D^3/8$ gives the second form, $16kQ|q|/(mD^3)$.

# Properties

#### *(Absolute Values Keep $\omega$ Real)* Writing $|q|$ in the force keeps $\omega^2$ positive.

The moving charge is negative. Suppose $q$ itself went into the magnitude $kQ|q|/(x^2 + (D/2)^2)$ of each attraction. The coefficient of $x$ would flip sign, and $\omega^2$ would come out negative. Its square root would then be imaginary, which signals a sign error. The free-body diagram already set the direction of each force toward a fixed charge, so the magnitude must stay positive. [[3.1.4. Coulomb's Law]] states the same rule for any force diagram.

#### *(Small Oscillations Only)* The frequency is independent of the amplitude only while the displacement stays small.

The approximation dropped $x^2$ next to $(D/2)^2$. At $x = 0.1\,(D/2)$ the error in the force is about $1.5\%$. At larger amplitudes the restoring force grows more slowly than $x$. The particle takes longer to return, and the period grows. The same thing happens to a pendulum swinging through a large angle.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) An Electron in a Uniform Field
>An electron starts at rest in a uniform field of $500\ \text{N/C}$ pointing along $+x$.
>
>>[!example]- Find its acceleration and the time it takes to reach $1.0 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m/s}$.
>>The electron is negative, so it accelerates along $-x$ with
>>$$a = \frac{eE}{m_e} = \frac{(1.602 \times 10^{-19})(500)}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}} = 8.8 \times 10^{13}\ \text{m/s}^2.$$
>>Starting from rest, it reaches $v = at$ after
>>$$t = \frac{v}{a} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{6}}{8.8 \times 10^{13}} = 1.1 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{s}.$$

>[!example] (Medium) Oscillation Frequency
>Two charges of $+2.0\ \mu\text{C}$ are fixed $10\ \text{cm}$ apart on a frictionless table. A small bead of mass $1.0\ \text{g}$ and charge $-0.50\ \mu\text{C}$ is released near the midpoint, slightly off it along the bisector.
>
>>[!example]- Find the frequency and period of its small oscillations.
>>With $D = 0.10\ \text{m}$,
>>$$\omega^2 = \frac{16kQ\,|q|}{mD^3} = \frac{16(8.99 \times 10^{9})(2.0 \times 10^{-6})(0.50 \times 10^{-6})}{(1.0 \times 10^{-3})(0.10)^3} = 1.44 \times 10^{5}\ \text{s}^{-2}.$$
>>So $\omega = 379\ \text{rad/s}$, the frequency is $f = \omega/2\pi = 60\ \text{Hz}$, and the period is $T = 1/f = 17\ \text{ms}$.

>[!example] (Hard) Deflecting an Electron Beam
>An electron enters the region between two parallel plates moving horizontally at $2.0 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m/s}$. Between the plates the field is uniform, $1.0 \times 10^{4}\ \text{N/C}$, pointing up. The plates are $5.0\ \text{cm}$ long.
>
>>[!example]- How far does the electron move vertically while between the plates, and at what angle does it leave?
>>The field has no horizontal component, so the horizontal speed stays constant. The electron spends
>>$$t = \frac{0.050\ \text{m}}{2.0 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m/s}} = 2.5 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{s}$$
>>between the plates. The electron is negative, so it accelerates downward, with
>>$$a = \frac{eE}{m_e} = \frac{(1.602 \times 10^{-19})(1.0 \times 10^{4})}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}} = 1.76 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m/s}^2.$$
>>Its downward displacement is $y = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 = 5.5 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}$, or $5.5\ \text{mm}$. It leaves with a downward speed of $at = 4.4 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m/s}$. Its direction is $\arctan(4.4/20) = 12^\circ$ below the horizontal.
