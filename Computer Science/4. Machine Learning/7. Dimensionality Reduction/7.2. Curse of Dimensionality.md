>[!note] Curse of Dimensionality
>The **curse of dimensionality** is the set of problems that appear as the number of features $d$ grows. Volume grows exponentially with $d$, so a fixed number of samples covers the space more and more thinly. Richard Bellman named the effect in 1957. Take data spread uniformly over the unit cube $[0, 1]^d$. A sub-cube that captures a fraction $r$ of the data needs edge length
>$$e_d(r) = r^{1/d},$$
>where $r \in (0, 1]$ (ESL section 2.5). With $d = 10$, capturing $1\%$ of the data takes $63\%$ of each feature's range, and capturing $10\%$ takes $80\%$. Distances also lose contrast. Beyer et al. (1999) studied the distance from a query to a random data point. If its relative spread shrinks to zero, then
>$$\frac{D_{\max} - D_{\min}}{D_{\min}} \to 0 \quad \text{in probability as } d \to \infty.$$
>Here $D_{\max}$ and $D_{\min}$ are the largest and smallest distances from the query to the $n$ data points. The relative spread does shrink when the coordinates are independent and identically distributed. The main effects of high dimension are:
>
>- **Volume near the boundary**: the inner cube $[\epsilon, 1 - \epsilon]^d$ holds a fraction $(1 - 2\epsilon)^d$ of the volume. For $\epsilon = 0.05$ and $d = 100$, that is $0.9^{100} \approx 2.7 \times 10^{-5}$.
>- **Volume in the corners**: the inscribed ball holds a fraction $\pi^{d/2}/\big(2^d\,\Gamma(d/2 + 1)\big)$ of the cube. This is $0.785$ for $d = 2$ and $0.0025$ for $d = 10$.
>- **Sparse samples**: keeping the spacing between samples fixed takes $N^d$ samples where $N$ suffice in one dimension. Matching in ten dimensions the density of $100$ points in one takes $100^{10} = 10^{20}$ points.
>- **Distant neighbors**: nearest-neighbor methods average over neighborhoods that span most of each feature's range, so their estimates become biased.
>- **Empty cells**: a histogram with $m$ bins per feature has $m^d$ cells, and almost all of them stay empty.
>
>Models can still learn in high dimensions when the data have structure. One common structure is a low **intrinsic dimension**, meaning the data lie near a surface of few dimensions. Others are a smooth target function and a target that depends on few features. [[7.1. Principal Component Analysis|PCA]], regularization and [[5.2. Convolutional Neural Networks|convolutional networks]] all exploit such structure.

# Intuition

The name comes from Bellman's work on dynamic programming, where a grid over $d$ state variables needs exponentially many cells. In machine learning, every method that learns from nearby examples faces the same exponential count. In one dimension, $100$ samples on $[0, 1]$ sit about $0.01$ apart. In ten dimensions, the same $100$ samples leave most of the cube far from every sample. A method that averages nearby examples then has none nearby. Its neighbors differ from the query in many features at once.

Geometry in many dimensions also differs from geometry in two or three. Almost all the volume of a cube sits in its corners and near its faces. Random points lie at nearly equal distances from each other. Methods that rely on a meaningful notion of "close" then work poorly. Examples are k-nearest neighbors and Gaussian kernels with a fixed width. Real data suffer less from these effects, because they do not fill the space. Photos of faces have millions of pixel values, but they vary in far fewer ways.

# Properties

#### *(Neighbors Are Far Away)* Among $500$ uniform points in a ten-dimensional ball, the point nearest the center typically lies past the halfway mark.

Draw $n$ points uniformly in the unit ball of $\mathbb{R}^d$. ESL section 2.5 gives the median distance from the origin to the closest one:
$$m(d, n) = \left(1 - 2^{-1/n}\right)^{1/d}.$$
With $n = 500$, it is $0.0014$ for $d = 1$, $0.11$ for $d = 3$ and $0.52$ for $d = 10$. ESL concludes that most data points are closer to the boundary than to any other data point. Predictions near the boundary extrapolate from neighbors that all lie on one side. A [[4.5. k-Nearest Neighbors|k-nearest-neighbor]] estimate in high dimensions therefore has large bias even with $k = 1$. Shrinking the neighborhood lowers the bias but leaves fewer points inside it, so the variance grows. Keeping both small takes exponentially more data (see [[2.3.1. Bias-Variance Decomposition]]).

#### *(Distances Concentrate)* For independent uniform coordinates, the spread of squared distances relative to their mean shrinks like $1/\sqrt{d}$.

The squared distance between two random points adds $d$ independent terms $(x_j - y_j)^2$. The Hard example computes their mean, $1/6$, and variance, $7/180$. The squared distance therefore has mean $d/6$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{7d/180}$, a ratio of $\sqrt{1.4/d} \approx 1.18/\sqrt{d}$. With $d = 100$, it is $16.7 \pm 2.0$, and with $d = 10{,}000$ it is $1667 \pm 20$. By the [[3.3 Central Limit Theorem|central limit theorem]], its distribution is close to a narrow Gaussian. Ranking points by distance then depends on small differences, which noise in a few features can reverse. Data with clusters or low intrinsic dimension fail the condition of Beyer et al., and their distances keep their contrast.

#### *(Gaussian Mass on a Shell)* A standard Gaussian in $d$ dimensions puts almost all of its mass near the sphere of radius $\sqrt{d}$.

If $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}_d)$, then $\|\mathbf{x}\|^2$ has a chi-squared distribution with mean $d$ and variance $2d$. The norm $\|\mathbf{x}\|$ has a standard deviation close to $1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.71$ for every large $d$. With $d = 100$, samples have norm $10.0 \pm 0.71$, although the density is highest at the origin. A sample near the mode is therefore very unlikely. The midpoint of two independent samples has norm near $\sqrt{d/2} \approx 7.1$, about four standard deviations inside the shell. A straight line between two samples passes through a region where samples rarely fall.

#### *(Slow Rates for Smooth Targets)* No method can learn every smooth function in $d$ dimensions faster than $n^{-2\beta/(2\beta + d)}$ in mean squared error.

Stone (1982) proved this optimal rate for nonparametric regression. Here $\beta$ counts the derivatives of the target, and $n$ is the sample size. For $\beta = 2$, the exponent is $4/5$ in one dimension and $4/14 \approx 0.29$ in ten. Halving the error then takes $2^{5/4} \approx 2.4$ times more data in one dimension. It takes $2^{14/4} \approx 11.3$ times more in ten, and $2^{26} \approx 6.7 \times 10^7$ times more in $100$. The rate is a lower bound over the whole class of smooth functions. Faster learning needs stronger assumptions about the target.

#### *(Structure Lowers the Cost)* A correct parametric model avoids the exponential cost, and data of low intrinsic dimension reduce it.

Suppose the true model is linear with noise variance $\sigma^2$. ESL section 2.5 shows that least squares then has expected test error about $\sigma^2 + \sigma^2 d/n$. The excess error grows only linearly in $d$. The price is bias whenever the linear model is wrong. Local methods can adapt to the data instead. Kpotufe (2011) studied the convergence rate of k-nearest-neighbor regression. The rate depends on the local intrinsic dimension of the data, which can be far below $d$. Images, audio and text have low intrinsic dimension relative to their raw size. Convolutional networks add the assumptions of locality and translation equivariance, which shrink the set of candidate functions.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) How Big Is a Neighborhood?
>A music app describes each song by $d$ features scaled to $[0, 1]$, and the songs are spread roughly uniformly. To recommend songs, it searches a cube around a song that holds $1\%$ of the catalog.
>
>>[!example]- How long is the cube's edge for $d = 1, 3, 10, 50$?
>>The edge is $e_d(0.01) = 0.01^{1/d}$. It equals $0.01$ for $d = 1$, $0.215$ for $d = 3$, $0.631$ for $d = 10$ and $0.912$ for $d = 50$. With $50$ features, the neighborhood spans $91\%$ of every feature's range. It barely restricts any single feature, so the recommended songs need not resemble the query in any particular respect.

>[!example] (Medium) Testing Every Combination
>A chemist studies a process with $d$ adjustable settings. She wants to test $10$ levels of each setting, and each run takes one minute.
>
>>[!example]- How long does a full grid take for $d = 3$ and $d = 6$? How does random sampling compare?
>>A full grid has $10^d$ runs. For $d = 3$, that is $1000$ minutes, about $16.7$ hours. For $d = 6$, it is $10^6$ minutes, about $694$ days or $1.9$ years. Suppose instead she samples settings uniformly at random. The chance that at least one of $60$ runs lands in the best $5\%$ of the setting space is $1 - 0.95^{60} \approx 0.954$. That chance does not depend on $d$. Random sampling cannot cover the space. It can still find a good region, as long as that region is not too small.

>[!example] (Hard) Distances in a Sensor Database
>A database stores $1{,}000$ readings, each a vector of $d$ features drawn independently and uniformly from $[0, 1]$. A query is a new reading drawn the same way.
>
>>[!example]- Find the mean and variance of one term $(x_j - y_j)^2$. What do they imply for nearest-neighbor search at $d = 10$ and $d = 1000$?
>>The difference $t = x_j - y_j$ has the triangular density $1 - |t|$ on $[-1, 1]$. Its even moments are
>>$$\mathbb{E}[t^2] = 2\int_0^1 t^2(1 - t)\,dt = \frac{1}{6}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[t^4] = 2\int_0^1 t^4(1 - t)\,dt = \frac{1}{15}.$$
>>The variance of $t^2$ is $1/15 - 1/36 = 7/180$. Over $d$ independent features, the squared distance has mean $d/6$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{7d/180}$. Its relative spread is $1.18/\sqrt{d}$, which is $0.37$ at $d = 10$ and $0.037$ at $d = 1000$. A simulation with $1{,}000$ points measures the ratio $D_{\max}/D_{\min}$ from a query. It is typically about $4$ at $d = 10$, $1.4$ at $d = 100$ and $1.1$ at $d = 1000$. At $d = 1000$ the nearest reading is only about $10\%$ closer than the farthest. Small errors in a few features can then change which reading is nearest. Suppose instead that the readings were uniform on a three-dimensional cube placed inside the $d$-dimensional space. Distances would then match those in three dimensions, and the median ratio would stay near $20$.
