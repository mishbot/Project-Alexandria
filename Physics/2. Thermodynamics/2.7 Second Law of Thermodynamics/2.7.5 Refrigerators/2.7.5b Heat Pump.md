>[!note] Heat Pump
>A **heat pump** is a [[2.7.5a Refrigerator|refrigerator]] used for its hot side. It takes heat $Q_L$ from the cold outdoors, adds work $W$, and delivers $Q_H = Q_L + W$ to the warm interior of a building. The machine is the same. Only the purpose changes, and with it the figure of merit. What is wanted is now $Q_H$, so
>$$\text{COP}_{\text{hp}} = \frac{Q_H}{W} = \frac{Q_L + W}{W} = \text{COP}_{\text{ref}} + 1.$$
>The heat pump's coefficient of performance is always at least one, because the work itself ends up as heat in the house. The ideal value, from the reversed [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot cycle]], is
>$$\text{COP}_{\text{hp, ideal}} = \frac{T_H}{T_H - T_L},$$
>which is large when indoors and outdoors are close in temperature and falls toward one as the outdoors gets colder. An electric resistance heater has $\text{COP} = 1$ exactly: each joule of electricity becomes one joule of heat. A heat pump with $\text{COP} = 3$ delivers three joules of heat per joule of electricity, because two of the three came from outside. This is why heat pumps are cheaper to run than resistance heaters in mild climates and lose their advantage in severe cold.
>
>The same machine cools the house in summer as an air conditioner and warms it in winter as a heat pump. In summer the house is the cold reservoir, and in winter it is the hot one. Only the side that counts as useful changes.

# Intuition

A heat pump is a refrigerator whose hot side is the room you want warm. In winter it takes heat $Q_L$ from the outdoor air, adds the work $W$, and delivers $Q_H = Q_L + W$ indoors. The house gets warmer and the outdoors gets slightly colder. Winter air at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ still holds a large amount of internal energy, so there is plenty of $Q_L$ to take. The work is the price of moving heat from the cold outdoors into the warm house. The [[2.7.2 Second Law of Thermodynamics|second law]] sets its minimum. The colder it is outside, the more work each joule of heat costs. The sketch shows one device in two seasons. In summer the flows reverse, and the machine cools the house as an [[2.7.5a Refrigerator|air conditioner]]. The house then plays the cold reservoir.

Inside the machine, a working fluid runs through an outdoor coil. The coil is colder than the outdoor air, so heat flows into the fluid. A compressor raises the fluid's temperature above room temperature. An indoor coil then lets the heat flow into the house. A reversing valve swaps the roles of the two coils for summer.

# Properties

#### *(One Plus the Refrigerator)* $\text{COP}_{\text{hp}} = \text{COP}_{\text{ref}} + 1$ for the same machine between the same reservoirs.

The two coefficients count the same cycle from opposite ends. The refrigerator counts the heat taken from the cold side, the heat pump counts the heat delivered to the hot side, and the two differ by the work. In the ideal case, $T_H / (T_H - T_L) = T_L / (T_H - T_L) + 1$. The identity holds for real machines too, because it follows from energy conservation alone.

#### *(Better Than Burning)* A heat pump delivers more heat than the energy it consumes, without violating any law.

The extra heat is not created. It is moved from outdoors, where it was already present as internal energy of the air or the ground, to indoors. Energy is conserved once the outdoor reservoir is counted. The second law is respected because work was spent. A furnace, by contrast, releases chemical energy and can never deliver more heat than the fuel contains.

#### *(Cold Weather Hurts)* The COP falls as the outdoor temperature drops, so a heat pump is least effective when the most heat is needed.

At $T_L = 10\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $T_H = 20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ the ideal COP is $29$; at $T_L = -20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ it is $7$. Real units fall from about $4$ to about $2$ over the same range and switch to a resistance backup in deep cold. Ground-source heat pumps draw from soil a few meters down, which stays near $10\ ^\circ\text{C}$ all year, and keep a high COP through the winter.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Ideal COP on a Mild Day
>A heat pump keeps a house at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ when it is $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ outside.
>
>>[!example]- Find the ideal coefficient of performance, and the heat delivered per joule of work.
>>With $T_H = 293\ \text{K}$ and $T_L = 273\ \text{K}$,
>>$$\text{COP}_{\text{hp, ideal}} = \frac{T_H}{T_H - T_L} = \frac{293}{20} = 15.$$
>>An ideal pump would deliver $15\ \text{J}$ of heat per joule of electricity, $14\ \text{J}$ of it drawn from the cold outdoor air. Real units deliver $3$ to $4\ \text{J}$, which is still three to four times what a resistance heater provides.

>[!example] (Medium) Running Cost
>A house needs $10\ \text{kW}$ of heat. Electricity costs the same per joule whichever heater is used.
>
>>[!example]- Compare the electrical power drawn by a resistance heater and by a heat pump with COP $3.5$.
>>The resistance heater draws the full $10\ \text{kW}$. The heat pump draws
>>$$P = \frac{Q_H / t}{\text{COP}} = \frac{10\ \text{kW}}{3.5} = 2.9\ \text{kW},$$
>>and takes the remaining $7.1\ \text{kW}$ from outdoors. The heat pump costs $29$ percent as much to run. On a colder day its COP might fall to $2$, and the cost would rise to half that of the resistance heater, still a saving.

>[!example] (Hard) The Same Machine in Summer
>The heat pump of the previous example is reversed in summer to cool the house from $35\ ^\circ\text{C}$ outside to $24\ ^\circ\text{C}$ inside. Its refrigerator COP in this mode is $3.0$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the heat delivered outdoors per joule of work, and the ideal COP for comparison.
>>As a refrigerator the machine removes $Q_L = 3.0\ \text{J}$ from the house per joule of work and delivers $Q_H = Q_L + W = 4.0\ \text{J}$ outdoors. Its heating COP in this configuration would be $4.0$, one more than its cooling COP, as the identity requires. The ideal cooling COP between $297\ \text{K}$ and $308\ \text{K}$ is $297 / 11 = 27$, so the real unit reaches about a ninth of the limit. The gap between real and ideal is set by how far the refrigerant's temperature must overshoot each reservoir to move heat at a useful rate.
