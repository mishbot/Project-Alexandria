>[!note] Superposition Principle
>The **superposition principle** states that the net electric force on a charge is the vector sum of the individual forces. Every other charge contributes one force. Each force is computed with [[3.1.4. Coulomb's Law|Coulomb's law]] as if the other charges were absent. For a charge $Q$ near charges $q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_n$,
>$$\vec F_{\text{net}} = \vec F_1 + \vec F_2 + \cdots + \vec F_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} k\,\frac{q_i Q}{r_i^2}\,\hat r_i.$$
>Here $r_i$ is the distance from $q_i$ to $Q$, and $\hat r_i$ is the unit vector pointing from $q_i$ toward $Q$. The presence of other charges does not change the force that any one pair exerts. The net force is found with a free-body diagram:
>- Draw one arrow for each other charge, along the line joining it to $Q$. Point it away from that charge if the two are alike, and toward it if they are opposite.
>- Label each arrow with its magnitude, $F_i = k\,|q_i|\,|Q| / r_i^2$.
>- Find each arrow's direction from the right triangle whose hypotenuse joins the two charges.
>- Before computing, use symmetry to set components to zero or to equate magnitudes.
>- Add the components, $F_{x} = \sum_i F_{i,x}$ and $F_{y} = \sum_i F_{i,y}$. Signs enter only here.
>
>The size of the net force is $\sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2}$, and its direction is the angle $\arctan(F_y / F_x)$, placed in the correct quadrant. If the charge is free to move, [[1.2.1. Newton's Laws|Newton's second law]] $\vec F_{\text{net}} = m\vec a$ gives its motion.

# Intuition

Electric forces combine exactly as the forces of mechanics do. A charge feels a separate push or pull from each neighbor. Each one has the size Coulomb's law gives for that pair alone. The net force is their [[3.1. Vector|vector]] sum, so the methods of mechanics apply without change. Draw the arrows, split them into components, and add.

The work in a problem is mostly geometry. The arrows point along lines between charges, and their angles come from the triangles those lines form. Symmetry often halves the work. Two equal charges placed as mirror images about an axis push with equal strength. Their components across the axis cancel, and their components along it add. Spotting this before any algebra keeps the calculation short and catches sign errors.

# Properties

#### *(Linear in Each Charge)* Doubling one charge doubles its own term in the sum and leaves the other terms unchanged.

Experiments show that the force between two charges does not depend on whether a third charge is nearby. The net force is therefore a sum of separate terms, one per charge, each proportional to that charge. The same rule extends from a few charges to a smooth spread of charge. The sum becomes an integral over small pieces $dq$, the method of [[3.2.4. Electric Field of a Continuous Charge Distribution]].

#### *(Exact Mirrors Only)* Symmetry sets a component to zero only when the positions and the charges are exact mirror images.

Place two equal charges $+Q$ at $(0, \pm D/2)$ and a charge $q$ on the $x$-axis. The top charge and the bottom charge are mirror images across the $x$-axis. Their forces on $q$ have equal sizes, $F_1 = F_2$, and their $y$-components cancel. Only the $x$-components need computing, and they are equal. If the bottom charge were $+2Q$, the $y$-components would not cancel. A wrong symmetry claim drops half of the problem. The note [[3.2.3. Symmetry Arguments]] develops the idea further.

The animation shows the cancellation for a negative charge on the axis. The vertical parts of the two attractions cancel, and the horizontal parts add to $2F_1\cos\theta$. On the other side of the middle the net force reverses. It always points back toward the middle.

#### *(Components Without Angles)* Each component equals the force times one leg of the triangle, divided by the distance between the charges.

The line from $q_i$ to $Q$ has horizontal and vertical legs $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$, and the distance is $r_i = \sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}$. The force points along that line, so its components are
$$F_{i,x} = \pm F_i\,\frac{|\Delta x|}{r_i}, \qquad F_{i,y} = \pm F_i\,\frac{|\Delta y|}{r_i}.$$
The sign of each component is read from the arrow on the diagram. These ratios are the cosine and sine of the angle, taken from the triangle already drawn. No angle needs to be measured or named.

#### *(Points of Zero Force)* On the line through two like charges, a third charge feels zero net force at one point between them.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not find points of zero force. This property follows the problems at the end of Giancoli chapter 21.

Place two like charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ a distance $d$ apart. Between them, their forces on a third charge point in opposite directions. They balance where $|q_1|/x^2 = |q_2|/(d - x)^2$, with $x$ measured from $q_1$. The balance point is closer to the smaller charge. It does not depend on the size or sign of the third charge, since that charge multiplies both forces. For opposite charges of unequal size, the balance point lies outside the pair, beyond the smaller charge.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Three Charges in a Line
>Charge $q_1 = +3.0\ \mu\text{C}$ sits at $x = 0$, charge $q_2 = -2.0\ \mu\text{C}$ at $x = 0.20\ \text{m}$, and $Q = +1.0\ \mu\text{C}$ at $x = 0.40\ \text{m}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the net force on $Q$.
>>Charge $q_1$ repels $Q$, so its force points in the $+x$ direction. Its size is
>>$$F_1 = (8.99 \times 10^{9})\,\frac{(3.0 \times 10^{-6})(1.0 \times 10^{-6})}{(0.40)^2} = 0.169\ \text{N}.$$
>>Charge $q_2$ attracts $Q$, so its force points in the $-x$ direction, toward $q_2$. Its size is
>>$$F_2 = (8.99 \times 10^{9})\,\frac{(2.0 \times 10^{-6})(1.0 \times 10^{-6})}{(0.20)^2} = 0.449\ \text{N}.$$
>>The net force is $F_x = 0.169 - 0.449 = -0.281\ \text{N}$. It has size $0.28\ \text{N}$ and points in the $-x$ direction. The closer charge gives the larger force even though its charge is smaller.

>[!example] (Medium) A Right-Angle Arrangement
>Charge $Q = -2.0\ \mu\text{C}$ sits at the origin. Charge $q_1 = +4.0\ \mu\text{C}$ sits at $(0.30\ \text{m}, 0)$, and $q_2 = +6.0\ \mu\text{C}$ sits at $(0.30\ \text{m}, 0.30\ \text{m})$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the size and direction of the net force on $Q$.
>>Both forces are attractions, so each arrow points from $Q$ toward the other charge. The force from $q_1$ points along $+x$ with size
>>$$F_1 = (8.99 \times 10^{9})\,\frac{(4.0 \times 10^{-6})(2.0 \times 10^{-6})}{(0.30)^2} = 0.799\ \text{N}.$$
>>The distance to $q_2$ is $\sqrt{0.30^2 + 0.30^2} = 0.424\ \text{m}$, so
>>$$F_2 = (8.99 \times 10^{9})\,\frac{(6.0 \times 10^{-6})(2.0 \times 10^{-6})}{(0.424)^2} = 0.599\ \text{N}.$$
>>The triangle to $q_2$ has equal legs, so both components of $F_2$ are $0.599 \times 0.30/0.424 = 0.424\ \text{N}$, and both are positive. Adding,
>>$$F_x = 0.799 + 0.424 = 1.223\ \text{N}, \qquad F_y = 0.424\ \text{N}.$$
>>The net force has size $\sqrt{1.223^2 + 0.424^2} = 1.29\ \text{N}$. It points $\arctan(0.424/1.223) = 19^\circ$ above the $+x$ axis.

>[!example] (Hard) A Charge on the Bisector
>Two charges $+Q$ are fixed at $(0, D/2)$ and $(0, -D/2)$. A charge $q < 0$ sits on the $x$-axis at position $x$. Take $Q = 5.0\ \mu\text{C}$, $q = -1.0\ \mu\text{C}$, $D = 0.20\ \text{m}$, and $x = 0.050\ \text{m}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the net force on $q$, first in symbols and then in numbers.
>>Both forces are attractions toward the fixed charges. By symmetry they have equal sizes, and their $y$-components cancel. Each has size
>>$$F_1 = k\,\frac{Q\,|q|}{x^2 + (D/2)^2}.$$
>>Each $x$-component points toward the origin and equals $F_1\cos\theta$, where $\cos\theta = x / \sqrt{x^2 + (D/2)^2}$ comes from the triangle. Adding the two,
>>$$F_x = -2F_1\cos\theta = -\frac{2kQ\,|q|\,x}{\left(x^2 + (D/2)^2\right)^{3/2}}.$$
>>With the numbers, $x^2 + (D/2)^2 = 0.0125\ \text{m}^2$, so $F_1 = 3.60\ \text{N}$ and $\cos\theta = 0.447$. The net force is $F_x = -3.22\ \text{N}$, pointing toward the origin. The force always points back toward the origin, from either side. Released at rest, the charge oscillates back and forth through the origin. The note [[3.2.5. Motion of a Charged Particle in an Electric Field]] finds the frequency for small $x$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The next example finds a point of zero force, which lecture did not cover.

>[!example] (Medium) Where the Forces Balance
>Charge $+4.0\ \mu\text{C}$ sits at $x = 0$ and charge $+1.0\ \mu\text{C}$ sits at $x = 0.30\ \text{m}$.
>
>>[!example]- Where on the $x$-axis does a third charge feel zero net force?
>>Outside the pair both forces point the same way, so the point lies between the charges. There the forces oppose each other, and they balance where
>>$$\frac{4.0}{x^2} = \frac{1.0}{(0.30 - x)^2}.$$
>>Taking square roots of both sides gives $2(0.30 - x) = x$, so $x = 0.20\ \text{m}$. The point sits twice as far from the $4\ \mu\text{C}$ charge as from the $1\ \mu\text{C}$ charge. A charge four times larger needs twice the distance to give the same force.
