>[!note] Price Discrimination
>**Price discrimination** means selling the same good at different per-unit prices, to different buyers or for different units. A single-price [[6.9.1 Monopoly|monopoly]] restricts its contracts to one price for every buyer and every unit. Discrimination relaxes that restriction, so profit never falls. It needs market power, a way to sort buyers, and no resale.
>- In **first-degree** (perfect) discrimination, the firm sets price and quantity consumer by consumer, and each buyer pays her full willingness to pay. The firm sells every unit whose value is at least marginal cost, so output equals the competitive output. The outcome is efficient, with no deadweight loss, and all the surplus goes to the firm.
>- In **second-degree** discrimination (self-selection), one menu of packages is offered to everyone, and the price depends on the quantity bought. Each buyer picks the package she likes best, so the menu must make each type prefer the package meant for it. Examples are bundling (copiers and toner), quantity discounts, and two-part tariffs (phone plans).
>- In **third-degree** discrimination (segmented markets), the firm separates markets and charges a constant per-unit price within each. With $TC(y) = cy$ it solves
>$$\max_{y_A,\,y_B}\ p_A(y_A)\,y_A + p_B(y_B)\,y_B - c\,(y_A + y_B).$$
>The first-order conditions set marginal revenue in each market equal to $c$. In elasticity form,
>$$p_A\left(1 + \frac{1}{\varepsilon_A}\right) = c = p_B\left(1 + \frac{1}{\varepsilon_B}\right),$$
>where $\varepsilon_A$ and $\varepsilon_B$ are the price elasticities of demand in each market. The firm charges more in the market with lower $|\varepsilon|$. Examples are student discounts and cross-country prices of drugs, books, flights, and cars.
>- Resale undoes all three forms, since low-price buyers can sell to high-price buyers. As markets integrate, for example through the internet, cross-country discrimination becomes harder.

# Intuition

A single price loses profit in two ways. Buyers who value the good above the price keep a surplus. Buyers who value it above marginal cost but below the price do not buy, which is the deadweight loss. Discrimination recovers both. The three forms differ in what the firm knows. If it knows each buyer's value, it charges that value. If it knows only which market a buyer belongs to, it sets a separate monopoly price in each market. If it knows nothing about individuals, it designs a menu and lets buyers sort themselves by what they choose.

The diagram shows nine buyers who each want one unit, with values $9, 8, \dots, 1$, and a marginal cost of $2.5$. The best single price is $6$. It serves buyers $1$ to $4$ and prices out buyers $5$ to $7$, whose values exceed the cost. Under perfect discrimination, the firm sells to buyers $1$ to $7$ and charges each one her value. # Properties

#### *(Demand Becomes Marginal Revenue)* Under perfect discrimination, selling one more unit no longer lowers the price of the other units.

A single-price monopolist must cut the price for everyone to reach one more buyer, which is why its MR lies below demand. A perfectly discriminating firm charges the new buyer her own value and leaves the other prices unchanged. Its marginal revenue is the price of the marginal unit, so the MR curve is the demand curve itself. Setting it equal to MC gives the competitive quantity. Total surplus is the same as under competition, but consumer surplus is zero.

#### *(Equal Marginal Revenue)* Third-degree pricing sets each market's marginal revenue equal to marginal cost, so each market gets its own monopoly markup.

If $MR_A > MR_B$, moving one unit from market B to market A raises revenue at the same cost. So at the optimum $MR_A = MR_B = c$. Each market then follows the markup rule $(p_i - c)/p_i = -1/\varepsilon_i$ of [[6.9.1 Monopoly]]. With constant marginal cost the two problems are separate. If marginal cost rises with total output, the markets are linked through $c'_y(y_A + y_B)$. The diagram shows the Hard example, with $p_A = 100 - y_A$, $p_B = 60 - y_B$, and $c = 20$. Market A is less elastic at the optimum ($-1.5$ versus $-2$), so it pays $60$ while market B pays $40$.

#### *(Relaxing a Constraint)* A single price is a constraint on the discriminating firm's problem, so discrimination weakly raises profit.

In the Hard example, profit as a function of the two quantities is $\pi = 80y_A - y_A^2 + 40y_B - y_B^2$, a dome with its peak at $(40, 20)$. A single price requires $100 - y_A = 60 - y_B$, so the firm must stay on the line $y_A = y_B + 40$. The best point on that line is $(50, 10)$, with profit $1800$ instead of $2000$. The same logic covers all three forms. Each one lets the firm choose from a larger set of contracts.

#### *(Resale Undoes It)* Every form of discrimination fails if buyers who pay less can resell to buyers who pay more.

In the photocopy menu of the Easy example, the large package costs $0.50$ per copy and the small one costs $1$. A reseller can buy large packages and sell $100$ copies to light users for less than $100$. The firm then sells every copy at about $0.50$. Across countries, a drug sold cheaply abroad can flow back to the high-price market. Discrimination works best for goods that are hard to transfer, such as services, tickets in the buyer's name, or student discounts checked at the door.

>[!warning] Lecture Wording: Why Self-Selection
>The lecture motivates second-degree pricing by saying that perfect discrimination is not legal. Rules differ by country and industry. A second obstacle is that the firm rarely observes each buyer's value. A menu needs no such information, since every buyer faces the same offer.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The two-part tariff calculation and the welfare comparison for third-degree pricing below are extensions.

#### *(Two-Part Tariff)* With identical buyers, a fixed fee plus a per-unit price equal to MC reaches the perfect-discrimination profit.

At the per-unit price $c$, each buyer buys the efficient quantity and earns consumer surplus $CS(c)$. A fee equal to $CS(c)$ takes that surplus. If each buyer demands $q = 10 - p$ and $c = 2$, the fee is $\frac12\cdot 8\cdot 8 = 32$ per buyer. A single price earns only $16$ ($p = 6$, $q = 4$). With different buyer types, one fee cannot match every type's surplus, and menus of plans take over.

# Derivation

We want the elasticity form of the third-degree conditions. The derivatives of profit are
$$\frac{\partial \pi}{\partial y_A} = p_A'(y_A)\,y_A + p_A(y_A) - c = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial \pi}{\partial y_B} = p_B'(y_B)\,y_B + p_B(y_B) - c = 0.$$
Factoring $p_A$ out of the first gives $p_A\big(1 + p_A'(y_A)\,y_A/p_A\big) = c$. As in [[6.9.1 Monopoly]], $p_A'\,y_A/p_A = 1/\varepsilon_A$, which gives $p_A(1 + 1/\varepsilon_A) = c$, and likewise for B. The cross derivatives of $\pi$ are zero, so the Hessian is diagonal and the SOC is $p_i''y_i + 2p_i' < 0$ in each market ([[7.1 Unconstrained Optimality Conditions]]). Dividing the two conditions gives
$$\frac{p_A}{p_B} = \frac{1 + 1/\varepsilon_B}{1 + 1/\varepsilon_A}.$$
If market A is less elastic, $|\varepsilon_A| < |\varepsilon_B|$, then $1/\varepsilon_A < 1/\varepsilon_B < 0$. Both factors $1 + 1/\varepsilon_i$ equal $c/p_i > 0$, and the denominator is the smaller one, so $p_A > p_B$.

For the first degree, the firm earns $\int_0^y p(q)\,dq$ from $y$ units, since each unit sells at its own height on the demand curve. The derivative with respect to $y$ is $p(y)$, so the FOC is $p(y) = c'_y(y)$, the competitive condition.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Selling Photocopies
>Consumer A values each copy at $1$, for up to $100$ copies a month. Consumer B values each copy at $0.50$, for up to $1{,}000$ copies. Copies cost the firm nothing. The firm offers everyone the same menu: $100$ copies for $100 - \epsilon$, or $1{,}000$ copies for $500 - \epsilon$, with $\epsilon > 0$ small.
>
>>[!example]- Check that each consumer picks the package meant for her. Compare profit with the best single price per copy.
>>A gets $100 - (100 - \epsilon) = \epsilon$ from the small package. The large one is worth only $100$ to her, since she uses at most $100$ copies, so it gives $100 - 500 + \epsilon < 0$. She picks the small package. B values the small package at $50$, which gives $50 - 100 + \epsilon < 0$, and the large package gives $500 - 500 + \epsilon = \epsilon$. She picks the large one. Profit is $600 - 2\epsilon$, almost all of the surplus. With one price per copy, $p = 1$ sells only to A and earns $100$, and $p = 0.50$ sells to both and earns $50 + 500 = 550$. The menu beats the best single price by about $50$. Here self-selection almost reaches the perfect-discrimination profit of $600$, because neither consumer wants the other's package.

>[!example] (Medium) Nine Buyers
>Nine buyers each want one concert ticket, with values $9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1$ (in tens of dollars). Each ticket costs $2.5$ to provide.
>
>>[!example]- Compare a single-price seller with a perfectly discriminating seller: price, tickets sold, profit, consumer surplus, and deadweight loss.
>>With one price $p$ equal to one of the values, the seller sells to every buyer with value at least $p$. Profits are $6.5, 11, 13.5, 14, 12.5, 9$ at $p = 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4$. The best is $p = 6$, which sells $4$ tickets for profit $4 \times 3.5 = 14$. Consumer surplus is $3 + 2 + 1 + 0 = 6$. Buyers with values $5$, $4$, and $3$ do not buy, which loses $2.5 + 1.5 + 0.5 = 4.5$. A perfect discriminator sells to all $7$ buyers with value at least $2.5$ and charges each her value. Profit is $(9 + 8 + \dots + 3) - 7 \times 2.5 = 42 - 17.5 = 24.5$, consumer surplus is $0$, and the deadweight loss is $0$. Under one price, total surplus is $14 + 6 = 20$, which falls short of the possible $24.5$ by the deadweight loss. Perfect discrimination reaches the full $24.5$.

>[!example] (Hard) One Drug, Two Countries
>A drug maker sells in country A, with inverse demand $p_A = 100 - y_A$, and in country B, with $p_B = 60 - y_B$. Marginal cost is $c = 20$, and resale between the countries is banned.
>
>>[!example]- Find the discriminating prices and check the elasticity rule. Then suppose resale is allowed, which forces one price. Find it and compare profit, consumer surplus, and total surplus.
>>In A, $MR_A = 100 - 2y_A = 20$ gives $y_A = 40$ and $p_A = 60$. In B, $60 - 2y_B = 20$ gives $y_B = 20$ and $p_B = 40$. Elasticities are $\varepsilon_A = -60/40 = -1.5$ and $\varepsilon_B = -40/20 = -2$. The rule gives $60(1 - 1/1.5) = 20$ and $40(1 - 1/2) = 20$, as required. Profit is $40 \cdot 40 + 20 \cdot 20 = 2000$.
>>
>>With one price $p \le 60$, total demand is $160 - 2p$. Profit $(p - 20)(160 - 2p)$ peaks at $p = 50$, with $y_A = 50$, $y_B = 10$, and profit $1800$. Prices above $60$ serve only A, where the best is $p = 60$ with profit $1600$. So the single price is $50$. Consumer surplus in A rises from $800$ to $1250$, and in B it falls from $200$ to $50$. Total surplus is $3000$ with discrimination and $3100$ with one price. Total output is $60$ in both cases. Discrimination gives the units to buyers with different marginal values ($60$ in A, $40$ in B), which wastes surplus. With linear demands, discrimination can raise total surplus only when it opens a market that a single price would not serve.
