>[!note] Electric Field
>A charge produces an **electric field** $\vec E$ at every point around it. The field at a point is the force $\vec F$ on a small **test charge** $q_0$ there, divided by $q_0$:
>$$\vec E(\vec r) = \lim_{q_0 \to 0} \frac{\vec F}{q_0}.$$
>The limit makes the test charge small enough that it does not move the charges that produce the field. The SI unit of the field is the newton per coulomb, which equals the volt per meter: $1\ \text{N/C} = 1\ \text{V/m}$.
>
>The electric field of a point charge $Q$ at position $\vec r_Q$ is the vector
>$$\vec E(\vec r) = k\,\frac{Q}{r^2}\,\hat r, \qquad r = |\vec r - \vec r_Q|, \qquad \hat r = \frac{\vec r - \vec r_Q}{r}.$$
>Here $\vec r$ is the point where the field is measured. The distance $r$ is measured from $Q$ to that point. The unit vector $\hat r$ points from $Q$ toward it. When $Q$ sits at the origin, $r$ is the distance from the origin and $\hat r = \vec r/r$. The magnitude of the field is $E = k|Q|/r^2$. The test charge does not appear in either formula. The sign of $Q$ sets the direction:
>- The field points away from a positive charge.
>- The field points toward a negative charge.
>
>A charge $q$ placed in a field feels the force
>$$\vec F = q\vec E.$$
>A positive $q$ is pushed along $\vec E$, and a negative $q$ is pushed opposite to $\vec E$. Fields from several charges add as vectors, $\vec E = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2 + \cdots$, in the same way that [[3.1.5. Superposition Principle|forces do]]. A field problem uses the same free-body procedure as a force problem, with magnitudes $E_i = k|q_i|/r_i^2$ on the diagram.

# Intuition

Coulomb's law describes a force between two separated charges. Nothing seems to connect them. That raises a problem of cause and effect. If one charge moves, the force on the other charge should change only afterward, never at the same instant. The electric field solves this. Each charge surrounds itself with a field, and a second charge feels the field at its own location. The force becomes a contact force between a charge and the field right where the charge sits. When the source charge moves, the change in its field spreads outward at the speed of light. The other charge notices only when the change arrives.

Nobody can see a field, so the definition is operational. Place a small test charge at a point, measure the force on it, and divide by its charge. Halve the test charge and repeat. If the ratio stays the same, that ratio is the field at that point. A classroom demonstration makes the field visible. Grass seeds float in a thin layer of liquid between two metal posts. With no charge on the posts, the seeds point in random directions. Once the posts are charged, the seeds line up along curves that run from one post to the other.

The animation carries out the operational definition. A test charge visits points around $Q$ and leaves the arrow $\vec F/q_0$ at each one. The last frame fills in the field everywhere.

The field is the more useful object. Once the field of a set of charges is known everywhere, the force on any charge follows from $\vec F = q\vec E$. Most of the problems from here on ask for fields rather than forces.

# Properties

#### *(Shape From Symmetry)* A point charge looks the same in every direction, so its field points radially and depends only on distance.

Nothing distinguishes one direction around a point from another. The field must therefore have the form $\vec E = E(r)\,\hat r$ before any formula is used. Coulomb's law then supplies $E(r) = kQ/r^2$. The note [[3.2.3. Symmetry Arguments]] gives the full argument and uses it for lines, rings, and spheres.

#### *(Field From Force)* The point-charge field follows from Coulomb's law by dividing out the test charge.

Put a test charge $q_0$ a distance $r$ from $Q$. [[3.1.4. Coulomb's Law|Coulomb's law]] gives the force on it,
$$\vec F = k\,\frac{Q\,q_0}{r^2}\,\hat r.$$
Dividing by $q_0$ removes the test charge completely, so the limit $q_0 \to 0$ changes nothing. What remains, $kQ\hat r/r^2$, belongs to $Q$ alone.

#### *(Equal Forces, Unequal Accelerations)* A proton and an electron at the same point feel forces of equal size, and the electron accelerates far more.

Both particles carry charge of size $e$, so the force $q\vec E$ has the same size $eE$ for each. The forces point in opposite directions. The electron's acceleration is about $1836$ times larger, because its mass is that much smaller.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Field Near a Point Charge
>A small sphere carries $Q = +4.0\ \text{nC}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field $20\ \text{cm}$ from it, and the force on an electron placed there.
>>The size of the field is
>>$$E = k\,\frac{|Q|}{r^2} = (8.99 \times 10^{9})\,\frac{4.0 \times 10^{-9}}{(0.20)^2} = 899\ \text{N/C}.$$
>>It points away from the sphere, since $Q > 0$. The electron feels
>>$$F = eE = (1.602 \times 10^{-19})(899) = 1.44 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{N}.$$
>>The electron is negative, so the force points toward the sphere.

>[!example] (Medium) Two Charges and a Corner
>Two charges of $+5.0\ \text{nC}$ sit at $(0, 0)$ and $(0, 0.30\ \text{m})$. Point A is at $(0.30\ \text{m}, 0)$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field at A, and the force on a $-2.0\ \text{nC}$ charge placed there.
>>The charge at the origin is $0.30\ \text{m}$ from A. Its field points away from it, along $+x$, with size
>>$$E_1 = (8.99 \times 10^{9})\,\frac{5.0 \times 10^{-9}}{(0.30)^2} = 499\ \text{N/C}.$$
>>The other charge is $\sqrt{0.30^2 + 0.30^2} = 0.424\ \text{m}$ from A. Its field points away from it, down and to the right at $45^\circ$, with size
>>$$E_2 = (8.99 \times 10^{9})\,\frac{5.0 \times 10^{-9}}{(0.424)^2} = 250\ \text{N/C}.$$
>>Each component of $E_2$ has size $250/\sqrt 2 = 177\ \text{N/C}$. Adding components,
>>$$E_x = 499 + 177 = 676\ \text{N/C}, \qquad E_y = -177\ \text{N/C}.$$
>>The field has size $\sqrt{676^2 + 177^2} = 699\ \text{N/C}$ and points $15^\circ$ below the $+x$ axis. The force on the $-2.0\ \text{nC}$ charge has size $qE = 1.40 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{N}$. It points opposite to the field, $15^\circ$ above the $-x$ axis.

>[!example] (Hard) Equal and Opposite Charges
>Charge $+3.0\ \text{nC}$ sits at $(0, 0)$ and charge $-3.0\ \text{nC}$ at $(0.40\ \text{m}, 0)$. Point P is at $(0.20\ \text{m}, 0.15\ \text{m})$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field at P, and the acceleration of an electron released there.
>>Point P is $\sqrt{0.20^2 + 0.15^2} = 0.25\ \text{m}$ from each charge, so both fields have the size
>>$$E_1 = E_2 = (8.99 \times 10^{9})\,\frac{3.0 \times 10^{-9}}{(0.25)^2} = 432\ \text{N/C}.$$
>>The field of the positive charge points away from it, along $(0.20, 0.15)/0.25 = (0.8, 0.6)$. The field of the negative charge points toward it, along $(0.20, -0.15)/0.25 = (0.8, -0.6)$. The $y$-components cancel by symmetry, and the $x$-components add:
>>$$E_x = 2 \times 432 \times 0.8 = 690\ \text{N/C}.$$
>>The field points along $+x$. The electron feels a force opposite to the field, of size $eE = 1.11 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{N}$. Its acceleration is
>>$$a = \frac{eE}{m_e} = \frac{1.11 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{N}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}} = 1.21 \times 10^{14}\ \text{m/s}^2,$$
>>directed along $-x$.
