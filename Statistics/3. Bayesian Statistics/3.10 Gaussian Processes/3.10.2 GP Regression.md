>[!note] Gaussian Process Regression
>**GP regression** estimates an unknown function $f : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ from noisy observations $y_i = f(x_i) + \epsilon_i$ with $\epsilon_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, by placing a Gaussian process prior $f \sim \text{GP}(0, K)$ on the function itself. Because the joint distribution of observations and test values is multivariate Gaussian, the posterior is a GP with closed-form mean and kernel.
>
>Given test point $x$ and Gram matrix $\mathbf{K}$ with $\mathbf{K}_{ij} = K(x_i, x_j)$ and $\mathbf{k}_i = K(x, x_i)$:
>$$
>f(x) \mid \mathbf{y} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\underbrace{\mathbf{k}^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{y}}_{\text{posterior mean}},\; \underbrace{K(x,x) - \mathbf{k}^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{k}}_{\text{posterior variance}}\right).
>$$
>
>Setting $\sigma^2 = 0$ gives the interpolation case; with noise, observations are allowed to disagree with the estimate and repeated measurements at the same $x_i$ become informative. The *marginal likelihood*
>$$p(\mathbf{y} \mid \sigma, \boldsymbol\theta) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}; \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{K}_{\boldsymbol\theta} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})$$
>is used to learn kernel hyperparameters by empirical Bayes or full posterior sampling.
>
>**Computational cost**: the naive posterior computation is $O(n^3)$ from inverting the $n \times n$ matrix $\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}$. Scalability beyond $n \approx 10^4$ requires inducing-point or low-rank approximations (not in scope here). When the kernel has a linear component with improper $\mathcal{N}(0, C)$ priors, the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury identity makes the $C \to \infty$ limit well-defined and computable.

# Intuition

GP regression is the nonparametric analog of [[3.8.1 Bayesian Linear Regression]]. Instead of putting a prior on a finite-dimensional coefficient vector $\boldsymbol\beta$, put a prior directly on the function $f$. The rest follows: the posterior of $f$ given noisy observations is a new GP, the posterior mean is a kernel-weighted smoother, and the posterior variance grows smoothly between observation points — small near data, large far from data.

Two kinds of uncertainty get combined. The *noise variance* $\sigma^2$ captures how much the $y_i$ deviate from $f(x_i)$. The *kernel variance* $K(x, x)$ captures how much prior uncertainty there is about $f(x)$ itself. The regularized-kernel matrix $\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}$ cleanly combines both: the $\sigma^2 \mathbf{I}$ term is the exact same diagonal loading that makes the linear-regression MAP into ridge regression, and here it plays the analogous role of keeping the matrix invertible and damping overfitting.

Unlike interpolation, observations are *averaged* rather than exactly matched. At an observed input $x_i$, the posterior mean of $f(x_i)$ need not equal $y_i$ — it typically pulls $y_i$ toward the GP prior mean (zero) and toward neighboring observations. Repeated noisy measurements at the same $x_i$ are informative: each reduces posterior variance at $x_i$ as more information accumulates.

# Properties

#### *(Posterior is a GP)* Closed-form posterior mean and kernel.

The posterior process $f \mid \mathbf{y}$ is itself a Gaussian process with mean function and covariance kernel
$$
\mu_{\text{post}}(x) = \mathbf{k}(x)^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{y},
$$
$$
K_{\text{post}}(x, x') = K(x, x') - \mathbf{k}(x)^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{k}(x'),
$$
where $\mathbf{k}(x)_i = K(x, x_i)$. Because the posterior is itself a GP, any functional of $f$ (derivative, integral, linear predictor) is also Gaussian with computable mean and variance. This closure under conditioning is what makes GPs so clean to work with analytically.

#### *(Posterior Mean is a Kernel Smoother)* Linear combination of observed $y$ values.

The posterior mean $\mu_{\text{post}}(x) = \mathbf{k}(x)^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{y}$ is a *linear* combination of the observations with weights depending only on inputs and kernel, not on $\mathbf{y}$. Writing $\boldsymbol\alpha = (\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{y}$, the predictor is $\mu_{\text{post}}(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i K(x, x_i)$ — a weighted sum of kernel functions centered at the training inputs. This is formally identical to the predictor in *kernel ridge regression*, which reveals that GP regression and kernel ridge regression are the same algorithm with different probabilistic interpretations.

#### *(Posterior Variance Ignores Observations)* Depends only on inputs and kernel.

The posterior variance $K(x,x) - \mathbf{k}^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{k}$ depends on the inputs $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ and the test point $x$, but *not* on the observed $y$ values. This is because the conditional covariance of a Gaussian depends only on the covariance matrix, not on the observed values. A counterintuitive consequence: the posterior variance can be computed before any data is collected. This is the basis for *Bayesian optimization* and *active learning* strategies, which choose new query points $x_{\text{new}}$ to minimize posterior variance before looking at $y_{\text{new}}$.

#### *(Hyperparameter Estimation via Marginal Likelihood)* Optimize evidence, not held-out loss.

The kernel depends on hyperparameters $\boldsymbol\theta$ (length-scale, signal variance, etc.) and the noise level $\sigma^2$. These are estimated by maximizing (or placing priors on) the log-marginal likelihood:
$$
\log p(\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol\theta, \sigma) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^\top (\mathbf{K}_{\boldsymbol\theta} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{y} - \tfrac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{K}_{\boldsymbol\theta} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}| - \tfrac{n}{2}\log 2\pi.
$$
The first term rewards fit, the second penalizes complexity — the log-determinant is large when the kernel allows high-variance functions, providing automatic regularization. Unlike cross-validation, marginal likelihood does not require held-out data and uses all $n$ observations. Full details and computational tricks in [[3.7 Marginal Likelihood]].

#### *(Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury for Infinite-Variance Priors)* Linear components with $C \to \infty$ priors are handled analytically.

When the kernel has a linear component $K(s,t) = C(1 + st) + \tau^2 K_I(s, t)$ with $C \to \infty$ (flat priors on intercept and slope), direct computation is unstable. Using the Woodbury identity
$$
(C \mathbf{X} \mathbf{X}^\top + \boldsymbol\Sigma)^{-1} = \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} - \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{X} (C^{-1} \mathbf{I}_d + \mathbf{X}^\top \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}
$$
and sending $C \to \infty$, the posterior mean for the combined kernel reduces to
$$
\hat{f}(x) = (1, x)(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{A}_\gamma^{-1} \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{A}_\gamma^{-1} \mathbf{y} + \gamma^2 \mathbf{k}_I^\top (\mathbf{A}_\gamma^{-1} - \mathbf{A}_\gamma^{-1} \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{A}_\gamma^{-1} \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{A}_\gamma^{-1})\mathbf{y},
$$
where $\mathbf{A}_\gamma = \mathbf{I} + \gamma^2 \mathbf{K}_I$ and $\gamma = \tau/\sigma$. This expression depends only on the identifiable ratio $\gamma$, not on $\tau$ and $\sigma$ separately. The first term is the ordinary-least-squares fit projected onto the linear component; the second is the IBM-smoothed residual.

#### *(Computational Cost is $O(n^3)$)* Dominated by the matrix solve.

Computing $(\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{y}$ requires a Cholesky factorization of the $n \times n$ matrix, which costs $O(n^3)$ time and $O(n^2)$ memory. This makes naive GP regression impractical beyond $n \approx 10^4$. Standard scaling strategies (not in scope here): inducing-point approximations ($O(nm^2)$ with $m$ inducing points), structured kernel interpolation, random-feature approximations, or Kronecker methods when inputs lie on a grid. For the problem sizes in STAT 238 (a few hundred observations), the $O(n^3)$ cost is inconsequential.

#### *(Separable-Noise Predictive Distribution)* Predictions account for both signal and noise variance.

To predict a *new noisy observation* $y_* = f(x_*) + \epsilon_*$ rather than the underlying $f(x_*)$, add $\sigma^2$ to the posterior variance:
$$
y_* \mid \mathbf{y} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{k}^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{y},\; K(x_*, x_*) - \mathbf{k}^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{k} + \sigma^2\right).
$$
The noise variance cleanly separates: predictions of the underlying function are narrower than predictions of new observations by exactly $\sigma^2$. This is the GP analog of the prediction-interval / confidence-interval distinction in linear regression.

# Derivation

Start from the joint distribution of the noisy observations and the latent value $f(x)$ at a test point. Under the prior, $f$ is a GP with mean zero and kernel $K$, so
$$
\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{y} \\ f(x) \end{pmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{0},\; \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I} & \mathbf{k} \\ \mathbf{k}^\top & K(x, x) \end{pmatrix}\right),
$$
using $\text{Cov}(y_i, y_j) = K(x_i, x_j) + \sigma^2 \delta_{ij}$ and $\text{Cov}(y_i, f(x)) = K(x_i, x)$. Applying the standard Gaussian conditioning formula
$$
\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{a} \\ b \end{pmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{0}, \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{A} & \mathbf{c} \\ \mathbf{c}^\top & d \end{pmatrix}\right) \implies b \mid \mathbf{a} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{c}^\top \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{a},\; d - \mathbf{c}^\top \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{c})
$$
with $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{y}$, $b = f(x)$, $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}$, $\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{k}$, $d = K(x,x)$ gives the headline result. The derivation extends immediately to joint prediction at multiple test points by replacing the scalar $b$ with a test vector and adjusting matrix dimensions.

# Examples

>[!example]- (Easy) Predict a GP with Brownian motion kernel between two observations.
>Observe $y_1 = 1.2$ at $x_1 = 0.2$ and $y_2 = 2.0$ at $x_2 = 0.8$, both with noise $\sigma = 0$. Use the scaled BM plus constant kernel $K(s, t) = C + \tau^2 \min(s, t)$ with $C \to \infty$ and $\tau = 1$. Predict $f(0.5)$.
>
>**Solution.** With $C \to \infty$, the posterior mean becomes the linear interpolant (Example 2.1 in L22):
>$$
>\hat{f}(0.5) = \frac{0.8 - 0.5}{0.8 - 0.2}(1.2) + \frac{0.5 - 0.2}{0.8 - 0.2}(2.0) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1.2) + \tfrac{1}{2}(2.0) = 1.6.
>$$
>Outside $[x_1, x_2]$, the posterior mean is constant-extrapolated: $\hat{f}(0.1) = 1.2$, $\hat{f}(0.9) = 2.0$. This confirms that the Brownian motion kernel induces *linear* interpolation inside the observed range and constant extrapolation outside.

>[!example]- (Medium) Hyperparameter fitting via marginal-likelihood grid.
>Given $n$ observations and the scaled-IBM-plus-linear-term kernel with ratio $\gamma$, sketch how to learn $\gamma$ and $\sigma$ from the data.
>
>**Solution.** Place a prior $p(\gamma) \propto 1/\gamma$ on $[\gamma_{\text{low}}, \gamma_{\text{high}}]$ (log-uniform). After integrating out $\sigma$ analytically (inverse-Gamma conjugacy) and letting $C \to \infty$ via Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury, the marginal posterior of $\gamma$ is
>$$
>p(\gamma \mid \mathbf{y}) \propto \frac{p(\gamma) \, |\mathbf{A}_\gamma|^{-1/2} \, |\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{A}_\gamma^{-1} \mathbf{X}|^{-1/2}}{\left(\mathbf{y}^\top [\mathbf{A}_\gamma^{-1} - \mathbf{A}_\gamma^{-1} \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{A}_\gamma^{-1} \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{A}_\gamma^{-1}] \mathbf{y}\right)^{(n/2) - 1}}
>$$
>with $\mathbf{A}_\gamma = \mathbf{I} + \gamma^2 \mathbf{K}_I$. Workflow:
>1. Choose a grid $\gamma_1, \ldots, \gamma_G$ (log-spaced between underfit and overfit extremes).
>2. Evaluate $\log p(\gamma_k \mid \mathbf{y})$ on the grid.
>3. Sample $\gamma$ from the discrete grid posterior.
>4. For each $\gamma$-sample, draw $\sigma$ from the inverse-Gamma conditional.
>5. For each $(\gamma, \sigma)$ pair, draw $\boldsymbol\beta$ and the posterior function values via the closed-form Gaussian formulas.
>This produces posterior samples of the function — full Bayesian inference without MCMC.

>[!example]- (Hard) Two noisy measurements at the same input reduce posterior variance.
>Observe $y_1 = 0.9$ and $y_2 = 1.1$ both at $x = 0.5$, with noise $\sigma = 0.3$. Use an RBF kernel with $\sigma_f^2 = 1$ and any length-scale $\ell > 0$. Compute the posterior mean and variance of $f(0.5)$.
>
>**Solution.** Both observations are at the same input, so the Gram matrix is
>$$
>\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I} = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_f^2 + \sigma^2 & \sigma_f^2 \\ \sigma_f^2 & \sigma_f^2 + \sigma^2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.09 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.09 \end{pmatrix}.
>$$
>Inverting gives $(\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} = \frac{1}{1.09^2 - 1}\begin{pmatrix} 1.09 & -1 \\ -1 & 1.09 \end{pmatrix} \approx \frac{1}{0.1881}\begin{pmatrix} 1.09 & -1 \\ -1 & 1.09 \end{pmatrix}$. The vector $\mathbf{k} = (1, 1)^\top$ (both at $x = 0.5$). Posterior mean:
>$$
>\mu_{\text{post}} = \mathbf{k}^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{y} = \frac{0.09}{0.1881}(y_1 + y_2) \approx 0.4785 \times 2.0 = 0.957,
>$$
>close to the mean of the two observations (1.0) but shrunk slightly toward the prior mean (0). Posterior variance:
>$$
>K(0.5, 0.5) - \mathbf{k}^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{k} = 1 - \frac{2 \times 0.09}{0.1881} \approx 1 - 0.957 = 0.043,
>$$
>smaller than the posterior variance from a single observation $\sigma_f^2 \sigma^2 / (\sigma_f^2 + \sigma^2) \approx 0.0826$. Replication at the same input did tighten the posterior — as expected.
