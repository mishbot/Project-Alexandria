>[!note] Softmax Regression
>**Softmax regression**, also called **multinomial logistic regression**, extends [[4.3. Logistic Regression|logistic regression]] to $K$ classes. Each class $k$ has a weight vector $\mathbf{w}_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and a bias $b_k$. They give the class a score called a **logit**, $z_k = \mathbf{w}_k^T\mathbf{x} + b_k$. The **softmax** turns the $K$ logits into probabilities:
>$$p_k(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{P}(y = k \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^K e^{z_j}}, \qquad k = 1, \dots, K.$$
>Each $p_k$ lies in $(0, 1)$, and the $K$ probabilities sum to one. Stacking the $\mathbf{w}_k^T$ as rows gives $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times d}$, so the logits are $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$. The prediction $\arg\max_k p_k = \arg\max_k z_k$ is the argmax rule of [[4.2. Linear Discriminants|linear discriminants]]. The decision regions are therefore convex, with linear boundaries.
>
>The label is encoded **one-hot**, as a vector $\mathbf{y} \in \{0, 1\}^K$ with a single $1$ at the true class $t$. Maximum likelihood under the categorical distribution gives the **cross-entropy** loss of one sample:
>$$\ell(\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{p}) = -\sum_{k=1}^K y_k \log p_k = -\log p_t = -z_t + \log\sum_{j=1}^K e^{z_j}.$$
>Here $\mathbf{p}$ is the vector of predicted probabilities and $z_t$ is the logit of the true class. The training loss sums $\ell$ over the $n$ samples. The gradients for one sample are
>$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}, \qquad \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf{W}} = (\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y})\,\mathbf{x}^T, \qquad \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf{b}} = \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}.$$
>For a whole dataset, put the probabilities and the one-hot labels in the rows of $\mathbf{P}, \mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$. The weight gradient is then $(\mathbf{P} - \mathbf{Y})^T\mathbf{X}$. The loss is convex in $(\mathbf{W}, \mathbf{b})$ and has no closed-form minimizer. Training uses gradient methods or Newton's method, as in the binary case.
>
>Three facts connect the model to its neighbors:
>
>- **Shift invariance**: adding the same vector $\mathbf{a}$ to every $\mathbf{w}_k$ and the same constant to every $b_k$ leaves every $p_k$ unchanged. One class is redundant, and fixing $\mathbf{w}_K = \mathbf{0}$ and $b_K = 0$ removes the redundancy.
>- **Two classes**: with $K = 2$, $p_2 = \sigma(z_2 - z_1)$ with the sigmoid $\sigma$. This is logistic regression with $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{w}_2 - \mathbf{w}_1$ and $b = b_2 - b_1$.
>- **Separable data**: as in logistic regression, the weights grow without bound. An $L_2$ penalty $\lambda\|\mathbf{W}\|_F^2 = \lambda\sum_{k,j} W_{kj}^2$ keeps them finite.

# Intuition

Logistic regression scores one class against the other. Softmax regression gives every class its own linear score and turns all the scores into probabilities at once. Exponentiating makes every score positive and turns differences into ratios. If one logit exceeds another by $1$, its class is $e \approx 2.72$ times as likely. Dividing by the sum then makes the probabilities add to one. The largest logit gets the largest share, and the other classes keep some probability. The name "softmax" refers to this smoothing. The output is a smoothed one-hot vector that marks the argmax, so "soft argmax" would be more accurate.

The loss looks only at the probability of the true class. Its gradient $\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}$ is the gap between the predicted distribution and the one-hot target. Gradient descent raises the true class's logit and lowers every other logit. Each logit moves in proportion to its error.

# Properties

#### *(Redundant Parameters)* Without a penalty the minimizer is not unique, and an $L_2$ penalty selects the weights that sum to zero.

By shift invariance, every weight matrix has a family of copies, $\mathbf{w}_k + \mathbf{a}$ for any $\mathbf{a}$, with the same loss. The Hessian is therefore singular along these directions. The penalty $\lambda\sum_k\|\mathbf{w}_k + \mathbf{a}\|^2$ is smallest at $\mathbf{a} = -\tfrac{1}{K}\sum_k \mathbf{w}_k$, so the penalized minimizer has $\sum_k \mathbf{w}_k = \mathbf{0}$. Unpenalized biases stay redundant, which does not affect predictions. Statistics software usually fixes a **baseline class** $K$ instead (ISLR, sec. 4.3.5). Each $\mathbf{w}_k$ then gives the log-odds of class $k$ against class $K$. A different baseline changes every coefficient and no prediction.

#### *(The Error Signal)* The logit gradient $\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}$ equals $p_t - 1$ for the true class and $p_k$ for each wrong class $k$.

The entries of $\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}$ sum to zero. The weight update $(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y})\mathbf{x}^T$ is a rank-one matrix: each class row moves along $\mathbf{x}$, by an amount set by that class's error. The gradient vanishes only when $\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{y}$, which needs infinitely large logit gaps. So the loss of a sample is never exactly zero. The simple form comes from a cancellation. The softmax Jacobian is $\partial p_k/\partial z_j = p_k(\delta_{kj} - p_j)$, where $\delta_{kj}$ is $1$ when $k = j$ and $0$ otherwise. The derivative of $-\log p_t$ multiplies it into $\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}$. Libraries therefore fuse the softmax and the loss into one function that takes logits, such as PyTorch's `CrossEntropyLoss`.

#### *(Temperature)* Dividing the logits by a temperature $T > 0$ changes how confident the probabilities are without changing the predicted class.

The tempered softmax is $p_k \propto e^{z_k/T}$. As $T \to 0$ it approaches the one-hot argmax, and as $T \to \infty$ it approaches the uniform distribution $1/K$. For logits $(4, 2, 0)$, $T = 0.5$ gives $(0.982, 0.018, 0.0003)$, $T = 1$ gives $(0.867, 0.117, 0.016)$, and $T = 4$ gives $(0.506, 0.307, 0.186)$. **Temperature scaling** (Guo et al. 2017) fits one $T$ on validation data by minimizing [[2.2.4. Log Loss|log loss]], which calibrates overconfident networks. **Knowledge distillation** (Hinton, Vinyals and Dean 2015) trains a small model on a large model's outputs at $T > 1$. Language models sample the next token at a chosen temperature, with lower $T$ giving more predictable text.

Linear scores split the plane into three argmax regions. The bars show the softmax probabilities at a moving probe point. Changing $T$ changes the bars but not the regions.

#### *(Numerical Stability)* Implementations subtract the largest logit before exponentiating.

The exponential overflows double precision for $z > 709.8$ and single precision for $z > 88.7$. Shift invariance allows subtracting $m = \max_j z_j$ from every logit. The largest term is then $e^0 = 1$, so nothing overflows and the sum is at least $1$. The loss uses the **log-sum-exp** identity $\log\sum_j e^{z_j} = m + \log\sum_j e^{z_j - m}$. Computing $\log p_t = z_t - \log\sum_j e^{z_j}$ directly also avoids taking $\log 0$ when $p_t$ underflows.

#### *(Exclusive Classes Only)* Softmax assumes each input has exactly one class, so tasks with several labels per input need separate sigmoids.

The softmax probabilities sum to one, so raising one class lowers the others. Softmax therefore suits mutually exclusive classes, such as the digit in an image. A photo tagged with both "beach" and "dog" breaks the assumption. Multi-label tasks use $K$ independent sigmoid outputs, one binary logistic regression per label. The **one-vs-rest** scheme also trains $K$ binary classifiers on exclusive classes. Its probabilities do not sum to one. Its $K$ scores are fit separately, so they are not on a common scale. Softmax regression trains all classes jointly and avoids both problems.

# Derivation

We want the gradient of the cross-entropy and the reduction to two classes. Write the loss as $\ell = -z_t + \log\sum_j e^{z_j}$. The derivative of the log-sum-exp term is
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial z_k}\log\sum_{j=1}^K e^{z_j} = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} = p_k.$$
The derivative of $z_t$ with respect to $z_k$ is $1$ when $k = t$ and $0$ otherwise, which is $y_k$. So $\partial\ell/\partial z_k = p_k - y_k$. Since $z_k = \mathbf{w}_k^T\mathbf{x} + b_k$, the chain rule gives $\partial\ell/\partial\mathbf{w}_k = (p_k - y_k)\,\mathbf{x}$ and $\partial\ell/\partial b_k = p_k - y_k$. Stacking the rows gives $(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y})\mathbf{x}^T$. The log-sum-exp is a convex function of $\mathbf{z}$, and $\mathbf{z}$ is linear in the parameters, so $\ell$ is convex.

For $K = 2$, dividing the numerator and denominator of $p_2$ by $e^{z_2}$ gives
$$p_2 = \frac{e^{z_2}}{e^{z_1} + e^{z_2}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(z_2 - z_1)}} = \sigma\big((\mathbf{w}_2 - \mathbf{w}_1)^T\mathbf{x} + (b_2 - b_1)\big).$$
The cross-entropy then equals the binary cross-entropy, with class $2$ as the positive label.

Politics, the most probable wrong class, lost the most probability.

>[!example] (Hard) Calibrating an Overconfident Model
>A trained topic model gives these logits for (sports, politics, tech) on four validation articles. Article 1 has $(4, 2, 0)$ and is sports. Article 2 has $(3, 0, 1)$ and is politics. Article 3 has $(2, 3, 0)$ and is politics. Article 4 has $(5, 1, 0)$ and is sports.
>
>>[!example]- What is the accuracy for any temperature? Compare the mean log loss at $T = 1$ and $T = 2$, and find the best $T$.
>>The model predicts sports, sports, politics and sports, so it gets articles 1, 3 and 4 right. Dividing by $T > 0$ keeps the order of the logits, so the accuracy is $75\%$ at every temperature. At $T = 1$ the true-class probabilities are $(0.867, 0.042, 0.705, 0.976)$. The mean log loss is $0.922$, and article 2 alone contributes $-\log 0.042 = 3.17$. At $T = 2$ they become $(0.665, 0.140, 0.547, 0.821)$, with mean log loss $0.793$. The three correct articles now cost more, but the confident mistake costs only $1.96$. Minimizing the mean log loss over $T$ gives $T = 1.88$ and a loss of $0.792$. In practice, temperature scaling is fit on thousands of validation examples.
