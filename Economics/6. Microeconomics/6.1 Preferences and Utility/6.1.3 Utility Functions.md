>[!note] Utility Functions
>A **utility function** $u : X \to \mathbb{R}$ assigns a number to each bundle in the commodity set $X$. It **represents** a [[6.1.1 Preference Relations|preference relation]] $\succsim$ if, for all $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in X$,
>$$\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y} \iff u(\mathbf{x}) \ge u(\mathbf{y}).$$
>Then $\mathbf{x} \succ \mathbf{y}$ exactly when $u(\mathbf{x}) > u(\mathbf{y})$, and $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathbf{y}$ exactly when $u(\mathbf{x}) = u(\mathbf{y})$. A higher number means a better bundle, and the numbers mean nothing more.
>
>A representation exists under two conditions. If $\succsim$ is rational (complete and transitive) and continuous, then there exists a continuous utility function $u : X \to \mathbb{R}$ that represents it. *(Proof omitted.)* In the other direction, any preference relation that a utility function represents is complete and transitive.
>
>Utility is **ordinal**, meaning that only the order of the numbers matters. If $u$ represents $\succsim$ and $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is strictly increasing, then the function
>$$v(\mathbf{x}) = f(u(\mathbf{x}))$$
>also represents $\succsim$. Here $f$ is the **transformation** and $v$ is the transformed utility. So a representation is never unique. For example, $3u + 7$ and $e^{u}$ represent the same preferences as $u$, and so does $\ln u$ when $u > 0$. A strictly decreasing $f$ reverses the ranking, so $-2u$ represents the opposite preferences. Two consumers whose utility functions differ by a strictly increasing transformation choose the same bundles from any set. Their indifference curves and their [[6.1.4 Indifference Curves and MRS|marginal rate of substitution]] are also the same.

>[!warning] No Cardinal Meaning
>Utility numbers say nothing about the intensity of a preference. If $u(\mathbf{x}) = 10$ and $u(\mathbf{y}) = 5$, the consumer prefers $\mathbf{x}$, but she is not "twice as happy" with it. The representation $u + 10$ gives the values 20 and 15, a ratio of $4/3$. Differences are also arbitrary, since $u^3$ turns the gap of 5 into a gap of 875. Only the order of the numbers is meaningful, because only the order stays the same under every strictly increasing transformation.

# Intuition

A ranking is hard to work with directly. There is no derivative of a relation like $\succsim$, and no first-order condition for "the best bundle". A utility function turns the ranking into numbers. Choosing the best bundle then becomes maximizing a function, and the tools of calculus and [[7.2 Lagrange Multipliers]] apply.

The numbers are labels that respect the order and carry no other content. Any relabeling that keeps the order describes the same consumer. The diagram shows three indifference curves of one consumer. The functions $u = x_1 x_2$ and $v = \sqrt{x_1 x_2}$ give the curves different numbers. Since $v = \sqrt{u}$ and the square root is strictly increasing, both functions rank every pair of bundles the same way.

# Properties

#### *(Same Choices)* A strictly increasing transformation changes the utility numbers but never the chosen bundle.

A strictly increasing $f$ satisfies $a \ge b \iff f(a) \ge f(b)$ for all real $a$ and $b$. Applying this with $a = u(\mathbf{x})$ and $b = u(\mathbf{y})$ gives
$$\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y} \iff u(\mathbf{x}) \ge u(\mathbf{y}) \iff f(u(\mathbf{x})) \ge f(u(\mathbf{y})),$$
which proves that $f(u)$ represents $\succsim$. A bundle that maximizes $u$ over a set therefore also maximizes $f(u)$ over that set. In practice, pick the representation that is easiest to work with. Taking logarithms turns a product into a sum, for example $\ln(x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{1-\alpha}) = \alpha \ln x_1 + (1 - \alpha) \ln x_2$. Its derivatives are simpler, and [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]] gives the same demand for either form.

#### *(Increasing on the Range)* The transformation needs to be strictly increasing only on the set of values that $u$ takes.

The logarithm is defined only for positive numbers, so $\ln u$ is a valid representation only when $u > 0$ on all of $X$. The square $u^2$ is strictly increasing on $u \ge 0$ and strictly decreasing on $u \le 0$. It is a valid representation of a Cobb-Douglas utility, which takes only values $u \ge 0$. It fails for a utility that also takes negative values, because it reverses the ranking of bundles with negative utility. The Hard example below works through such a case.

#### *(Represented Means Rational)* A utility representation forces completeness and transitivity, because the order $\ge$ on the real numbers has both properties.

For any bundles $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$, the numbers satisfy $u(\mathbf{x}) \ge u(\mathbf{y})$ or $u(\mathbf{y}) \ge u(\mathbf{x})$. So $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ or $\mathbf{y} \succsim \mathbf{x}$, which is completeness. If $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ and $\mathbf{y} \succsim \mathbf{z}$, then $u(\mathbf{x}) \ge u(\mathbf{y}) \ge u(\mathbf{z})$. Hence $u(\mathbf{x}) \ge u(\mathbf{z})$ and $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{z}$, which is transitivity. The contrapositive is useful. Preferences that fail an axiom have no utility representation at all. An example is the intransitive coffee drinker who cannot taste single grains of sugar.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Continuity can also block a representation. The lexicographic order is rational, yet no utility function represents it, continuous or not.

#### *(Concavity Is Not Ordinal)* An increasing transformation can make a concave utility function non-concave, while the convexity of the preferences stays the same.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The link between concave utility and convex preferences, and the term quasi-concave, were not covered. They are included because convex preferences are often confused with a convex utility function.

The functions $\sqrt{x_1 x_2}$ and $x_1 x_2$ represent the same preferences. The first is concave, since its [[3.7. Hessians|Hessian]] has non-positive diagonal entries and determinant 0. The second is not concave, since its Hessian $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $-1 < 0$. A concave $u$ always represents convex preferences. If $u(\mathbf{x}) \ge u(\mathbf{z})$ and $u(\mathbf{y}) \ge u(\mathbf{z})$, the definition of a concave function in [[2.2 Convex Functions]] gives $u(t\mathbf{x} + (1-t)\mathbf{y}) \ge t\,u(\mathbf{x}) + (1-t)\,u(\mathbf{y}) \ge u(\mathbf{z})$. The converse fails, as $x_1 x_2$ shows. So a concave $u$ proves that the preferences are convex. A $u$ that is not concave leaves the question open. A function whose sets $\{\mathbf{x} : u(\mathbf{x}) \ge c\}$ are all convex is called **quasi-concave**. Convex preferences are exactly the preferences with a quasi-concave representation.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Rescaling a Utility Function
>A streaming service models a subscriber's preferences over movies $x_1$ and series episodes $x_2$ with a utility function $u$. Two analysts propose the rescaled functions $v = 4u + 10$ and $w = -3u$. Bundle $A$ has $u(A) = 20$ and bundle $B$ has $u(B) = 10$.
>
>>[!example]- Which of $v$ and $w$ represents the subscriber's preferences? Is $A$ twice as good as $B$?
>>The function $v$ represents the same preferences. The map $f(u) = 4u + 10$ is strictly increasing, because its slope 4 is positive. The function $w$ does not. The map $f(u) = -3u$ is strictly decreasing, so $w$ reverses every ranking. Here $w(A) = -60 < w(B) = -30$, although the subscriber prefers $A$. An analyst who maximizes $w$ would recommend the bundle the subscriber likes least.
>>
>>The claim that $A$ is twice as good as $B$ has no meaning. Under $u$ the ratio is $20/10 = 2$. Under $v$, which represents the same preferences, the values are $v(A) = 90$ and $v(B) = 50$, a ratio of $1.8$. Only the statement "$A$ is better than $B$" holds for every representation.

>[!example] (Medium) Which Functions Agree?
>A student ranks bundles of coffees $x_1$ and croissants $x_2$, with positive quantities, by $u(x_1, x_2) = x_1^{1/4} x_2^{3/4}$. Consider four other functions:
>$$v_a = x_1 x_2^3, \qquad v_b = \ln x_1 + 3 \ln x_2, \qquad v_c = x_1^{1/2} x_2^{1/2}, \qquad v_d = x_1^{1/2} x_2^{3/2}.$$
>
>>[!example]- Which functions represent the same preferences as $u$? Would the student choose the same bundle under each from a given budget?
>>Write each function as a transformation of $u$ where possible. First, $v_a = u^4$, and $t \mapsto t^4$ is strictly increasing for $t > 0$. Second, $v_b = \ln(x_1 x_2^3) = 4 \ln u$, which is strictly increasing in $u > 0$. Third, $v_d = u^2$, again strictly increasing for $u > 0$. So $v_a$, $v_b$, and $v_d$ all represent the student's preferences.
>>
>>The function $v_c$ does not. Compare the bundles $(16, 1)$ and $(1, 4)$. Under $u$, $u(16, 1) = 2$ and $u(1, 4) = 4^{3/4} \approx 2.83$, so the student prefers $(1, 4)$. Under $v_c$, the values are $4$ and $2$, so $v_c$ prefers $(16, 1)$. A single reversed pair is enough to rule $v_c$ out. Scaling all exponents of a Cobb-Douglas function by a common positive factor keeps the preferences. Changing the ratio of the exponents changes them. The exponent of $x_2$ is 3 times the exponent of $x_1$ in $u$, $v_a$, and $v_d$, and only 1 times in $v_c$.
>>
>>From any budget, the student makes the same choice under $u$, $v_a$, $v_b$, and $v_d$. A consumer with utility $v_c$ generally chooses differently.

>[!example] (Hard) Utility With Negative Values
>An athlete scores weekly training plans by $u(x_1, x_2) = x_1 + 2x_2 - 6$, where $x_1$ is the number of runs and $x_2$ the number of swims. The constant 6 is the value of $x_1 + 2x_2$ at her current plan $(2, 2)$, so $u(2, 2) = 0$ and a negative score means a plan worse than the current one. Her coach suggests four other scoring rules: $u^3$, $u^2$, $e^{u}$, and $-1/u$.
>
>>[!example]- Which of the four rules represent her preferences on all of $X = \mathbb{R}^2_+$? Which would work for plans better than her current one?
>>On $X$, the score $u$ takes every value from $-6$ upward, including negative values and $0$.
>>
>>The rules $u^3$ and $e^{u}$ represent her preferences. Both are strictly increasing on the whole real line.
>>
>>The rule $u^2$ does not. Compare the plans $(0, 0)$ and $(3, 0)$, with scores $-6$ and $-3$. She prefers $(3, 0)$, but $u^2$ gives $36$ and $9$ and ranks $(0, 0)$ higher. Squaring reverses the order of negative numbers.
>>
>>The rule $-1/u$ does not either. It is undefined at the current plan $(2, 2)$, where $u = 0$. It also reverses rankings across zero. The plan $(4, 2)$ has $u = 2$ and the plan $(0, 2)$ has $u = -2$. She prefers $(4, 2)$, but $-1/u$ gives $-1/2$ and $1/2$ and ranks $(0, 2)$ higher.
>>
>>If she only compares plans better than her current one, then $u > 0$ on every plan considered. On that range both $u^2$ and $-1/u$ are strictly increasing, so both represent her preferences over those plans.
