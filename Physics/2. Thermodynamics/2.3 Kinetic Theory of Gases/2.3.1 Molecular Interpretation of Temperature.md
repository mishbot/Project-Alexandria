>[!note] Molecular Interpretation of Temperature
>The **kinetic theory of gases** derives the pressure of a gas from the motion of its molecules. It models an ideal gas with five assumptions. The gas holds a very large number $N$ of identical molecules of mass $m$. The molecules are far apart compared with their size. They move at random and obey [[1.2.1. Newton's Laws|Newton's laws]]. They exert no forces on one another except during collisions. Every collision, with a wall or with another molecule, is [[1.1p.2 Elastic Collisions|elastic]] and brief.
>
>Under these assumptions, the pressure that $N$ molecules in a volume $V$ exert on the walls is
>$$P = \frac{1}{3}\,\frac{N}{V}\, m\, \overline{v^2},$$
>where $\overline{v^2}$ is the average of the squared speed over all molecules. Comparing this result with the [[2.2.3 Ideal Gas Law|ideal gas law]] $PV = NkT$ gives the **molecular interpretation of temperature**,
>$$\overline{K} = \tfrac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2} = \tfrac{3}{2} kT,$$
>where $\overline{K}$ is the average translational [[1.3b Kinetic Energy|kinetic energy]] of one molecule and $k$ is Boltzmann's constant. The absolute temperature of an ideal gas is a direct measure of the average translational kinetic energy of its molecules. The relation holds for every ideal gas, whatever the mass of its molecules.
>
>Each of the three directions of motion carries one third of the total,
>$$\tfrac{1}{2} m \overline{v_x^2} = \tfrac{1}{2} m \overline{v_y^2} = \tfrac{1}{2} m \overline{v_z^2} = \tfrac{1}{2} kT.$$
>The total translational kinetic energy of the gas is $\tfrac{3}{2} NkT = \tfrac{3}{2} nRT$. A monatomic ideal gas has atoms with no rotation or vibration. For such a gas this total is the whole [[2.4.1 Internal Energy|internal energy]],
>$$E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{3}{2} NkT \qquad (\text{monatomic}).$$
>The internal energy depends on $T$ alone, and not on $P$ or $V$. Many texts write averages with angle brackets, $\langle v^2 \rangle$. These notes write the overline $\overline{v^2}$. The two symbols mean the same thing.

# Intuition

A gas presses on its container because molecules keep hitting the walls. Each hit pushes the wall outward for an instant. Billions of hits per second on every square millimeter blend into a steady force. That force per unit area is the pressure. Two things set the size of the push. The first is how many molecules hit the wall each second, which grows with the number density $N/V$ and with the speed. The second is how hard each one hits, which grows with the mass and the speed. Speed enters twice, so pressure depends on $\overline{v^2}$. The diagram shows one molecule in a box. It strikes the right wall, reverses its $x$ velocity, and crosses the box to strike again.

The pressure formula is a fact about molecules. The ideal gas law is a fact about thermometers and pressure gauges. Setting the two equal is what gives temperature its molecular meaning. A thermometer reads $T$. The molecules of an ideal gas at that temperature then have an average translational kinetic energy of exactly $\tfrac{3}{2} kT$. The mass of the molecules does not enter. Helium atoms and nitrogen molecules at the same temperature carry the same average kinetic energy, so the lighter helium atoms move faster. Heating a gas means making its molecules move faster, and nothing else.

# Properties

#### *(Pressure Is Kinetic Energy Density)* The pressure of an ideal gas is two thirds of the translational kinetic energy per unit volume.

Rewriting the pressure formula with $\overline{K} = \tfrac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2}$ gives $P = \tfrac{2}{3}\,(N/V)\,\overline{K}$. The pressure and the kinetic energy density have the same units, joules per cubic meter equal pascals. Doubling the number density at fixed temperature doubles the pressure, which is Boyle's law. Raising the temperature at fixed density raises $\overline{K}$ in proportion and therefore raises $P$ in proportion, which is Gay-Lussac's law. The [[2.2.2 Gas Laws|gas laws]] are consequences of molecules hitting walls.

#### *(Same Temperature, Same Kinetic Energy)* Two gases in thermal equilibrium have equal average kinetic energies per molecule.

Molecules of different masses in the same container collide with one another until neither kind gains energy from the other on average. That state is thermal equilibrium, and the [[2.1.3 Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics|zeroth law]] assigns it one temperature. Kinetic theory says what is equal at that temperature: the average translational kinetic energy, not the average speed. In air at room temperature the oxygen molecules and the nitrogen molecules have the same $\overline{K}$. The lighter nitrogen molecules move about $7$ percent faster.

#### *(Monatomic Internal Energy)* A monatomic ideal gas stores all of its internal energy as translational kinetic energy.

A single atom has nothing to rotate or vibrate, so its only energy is that of its motion through space. The internal energy is then $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{3}{2} NkT$, and a change in temperature $\Delta T$ changes the internal energy by $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{3}{2} Nk\, \Delta T$. Diatomic and larger molecules also rotate and vibrate, and those motions store additional energy. Their internal energy still depends on $T$ alone, but the factor in front is larger than $\tfrac{3}{2}$. The [[2.3.4 Equipartition of Energy|equipartition theorem]] gives the factor, and [[2.5.4 Molar Specific Heats]] connects it to measured heat capacities.

#### *(Equal Shares per Direction)* Random motion splits the kinetic energy equally among the three directions.

No direction in a gas at rest is special, so $\overline{v_x^2} = \overline{v_y^2} = \overline{v_z^2}$. Since $v^2 = v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2$, each of the three averages equals $\overline{v^2}/3$. The derivation below uses this step. It converts a count of hits on one wall into a statement about the full speed. The result that each direction carries $\tfrac{1}{2} kT$ is the simplest case of equipartition.

#### *(Absolute Zero)* At $T = 0$ the molecules of an ideal gas would have no translational kinetic energy.

The relation $\overline{K} = \tfrac{3}{2} kT$ reaches zero at [[2.2.1 Absolute Temperature|absolute zero]], and a kinetic energy cannot be negative. This is the molecular reason no temperature lies below $0\ \text{K}$. Real gases liquefy before they get there, and quantum mechanics leaves a small residual motion even at $T = 0$. Neither point changes the conclusion that $0\ \text{K}$ is the floor.

# Derivation

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not use the round-trip argument in the first three paragraphs. It used the slab argument that follows them and ran it forward to $PV = NkT$. The round-trip version follows Giancoli section 18.1.

We want to show that $P = \tfrac{1}{3}\,(N/V)\, m\, \overline{v^2}$. Consider a box of length $\ell$ along the $x$ axis and wall area $A$, so that $V = \ell A$. Take one molecule with velocity components $(v_x, v_y, v_z)$. When it strikes the right wall, the collision is elastic, so $v_x$ reverses and $v_y$ and $v_z$ are unchanged. The [[1.4.1. Linear Momentum|momentum]] of the molecule changes by $2 m v_x$ along $x$, and by Newton's third law the wall receives an impulse of $2 m v_x$.

The molecule crosses the box and returns after a time $\Delta t = 2\ell / v_x$. The average force it exerts on the right wall is the impulse per unit time,
$$F_1 = \frac{2 m v_x}{2 \ell / v_x} = \frac{m v_x^2}{\ell}.$$
Collisions with other molecules do not change this average. Momentum along $x$ is conserved in every collision, so some molecule always carries it to the wall. Summing over all $N$ molecules gives the total force on the wall,
$$F = \frac{m}{\ell} \left( v_{x1}^2 + v_{x2}^2 + \cdots + v_{xN}^2 \right) = \frac{m}{\ell}\, N\, \overline{v_x^2},$$
where $\overline{v_x^2}$ is the average of $v_x^2$ over the molecules. By the equal-shares property, $\overline{v_x^2} = \overline{v^2}/3$. Dividing by the wall area gives the pressure,
$$P = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{N m \overline{v^2}}{3 \ell A} = \frac{1}{3}\,\frac{N}{V}\, m\, \overline{v^2}.$$
Multiplying through by $V$ gives $PV = \tfrac{2}{3} N \left( \tfrac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2} \right)$. The ideal gas law says $PV = NkT$, so $\tfrac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2} = \tfrac{3}{2} kT$, the headline result.

A second route does not follow one molecule on a round trip. It looks at a thin slab of gas next to the wall, with thickness $\Delta x$ and area $A$. The slab holds a mass $m\,(N/V)\,A\,\Delta x$. Half of its molecules move toward the wall, so the mass that hits the wall is
$$\Delta m = \tfrac{1}{2}\, m\, \frac{N}{V}\, A\, \Delta x.$$
This mass arrives with momentum $+\Delta m\, v_x$ and leaves with $-\Delta m\, v_x$. The momentum of the gas changes by
$$\Delta p_{\text{gas}} = -\Delta m\, v_x - \Delta m\, v_x = -2\,\Delta m\, v_x.$$
The whole slab reaches the wall in the time $\Delta t = \Delta x / v_x$. The force on the gas is the rate of change of its momentum,
$$F_{\text{gas}} = \frac{\Delta p_{\text{gas}}}{\Delta t} = \frac{-2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\, m\, (N/V)\, A\, \Delta x\, v_x}{\Delta x / v_x} = -m\, \frac{N}{V}\, A\, v_x^2.$$
By Newton's third law the wall feels $F_{\text{wall}} = -F_{\text{gas}}$. Dividing by $A$ and averaging $v_x^2$ over the molecules gives
$$P = \frac{F_{\text{wall}}}{A} = \frac{N}{V}\, m\, \overline{v_x^2}.$$
The equal-shares step then gives the same $\tfrac{1}{3} (N/V)\, m\, \overline{v^2}$.

\foreach \x/\y/\d in {3.5/0.5/1, 3.9/1.7/-1, 4.2/1.0/1, 3.6/2.2/-1, 1.6/0.9/1, 2.2/1.9/-1, 0.9/1.5/1} {

The hatched slab holds every molecule that can reach the wall in $\Delta t$. Only those moving toward the wall hit it. Both routes replace $v_x^2$ by its average at one point and call the result exact. That step gets the right answer for a slightly wrong reason. The careful version averages over the full [[2.3.3 Maxwell Speed Distribution|speed distribution]]. It gives the same answer, because the average of a sum is the sum of the averages.

The argument also runs forward. Take $\tfrac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2} = \tfrac{3}{2} kT$ as the meaning of temperature. With equal shares, $\tfrac{1}{2} m \overline{v_x^2} = \tfrac{1}{2} kT$, so $m \overline{v_x^2} = kT$. Substituting into the pressure gives
$$P = \frac{N}{V}\, kT, \qquad PV = NkT.$$
Read this way, kinetic theory derives the ideal gas law instead of using it.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Kinetic Energy of an Air Molecule
>Air is at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- What is the average translational kinetic energy of one molecule, and does it differ between oxygen and nitrogen?
>>The average depends only on temperature,
>>$$\overline{K} = \tfrac{3}{2} kT = \tfrac{3}{2}\,(1.381 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K})(293.15\ \text{K}) = 6.07 \times 10^{-21}\ \text{J}.$$
>>Oxygen and nitrogen molecules have the same average kinetic energy, because both share the same $T$. The nitrogen molecules are lighter, so they move faster to carry the same energy.

>[!example] (Medium) Pressure from a Molecular Count
>A cubic box with sides of $10.0\ \text{cm}$ holds $1.00 \times 10^{22}$ argon atoms. The atomic mass of argon is $40.0\ \text{u}$, and the root-mean-square speed of the atoms is $400\ \text{m/s}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the pressure on the walls and the temperature of the gas.
>>The volume is $V = 1.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3$ and the atomic mass is $m = 40.0 \times 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg} = 6.64 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg}$. The rms speed gives $\overline{v^2} = (400\ \text{m/s})^2 = 1.60 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$. The kinetic theory pressure is
>>$$P = \frac{1}{3}\,\frac{N}{V}\, m\, \overline{v^2} = \frac{1}{3}\,\frac{1.00 \times 10^{22}}{1.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3}\,(6.64 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg})(1.60 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2) = 3.54 \times 10^{4}\ \text{Pa},$$
>>about a third of an atmosphere. The temperature follows from the average kinetic energy,
>>$$T = \frac{m \overline{v^2}}{3k} = \frac{(6.64 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg})(1.60 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2)}{3\,(1.381 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K})} = 257\ \text{K}.$$
>>The ideal gas law $P = NkT/V$ gives the same pressure, as it must.

>[!example] (Hard) Mixing Two Monatomic Gases
>Two rigid, insulated containers are joined by a closed valve. Container 1 holds $3N$ atoms of a monatomic ideal gas at temperature $T$. Container 2 holds $N$ atoms of a different monatomic ideal gas at temperature $3T$. The valve is opened and the gases mix.
>

>\end{tikzpicture}

>
>>[!example]- What is the final temperature?
>>The containers are rigid, so the gases do no work. They are insulated, so no heat enters or leaves. The total internal energy is therefore unchanged. Each gas is monatomic, so its internal energy is $\tfrac{3}{2} N_i k T_i$. Before mixing,
>>$$E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{3}{2}\,(3N)\,k\,T + \tfrac{3}{2}\,N\,k\,(3T) = \tfrac{3}{2}\,k\,(6NT).$$
>>After mixing, all $4N$ atoms share one temperature $T_f$, so $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{3}{2}\,(4N)\,k\,T_f$. Equating the two gives
>>$$T_f = \frac{6NT}{4N} = \tfrac{3}{2}\,T.$$
>>The answer does not depend on the atomic masses. Each atom carries $\tfrac{3}{2} kT_i$ on average whatever its mass, so only the counts and the temperatures matter. The final temperature is a count-weighted average of the two starting temperatures.
