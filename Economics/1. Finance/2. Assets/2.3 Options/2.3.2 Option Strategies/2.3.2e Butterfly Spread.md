>[!note] Butterfly Spread
>A **butterfly spread** buys European calls with strikes $K_1$ and $K_3$ and sells two European calls with strike $K_2$. The strikes are equally spaced: $K_1 < K_2 < K_3$ and $K_2 - K_1 = K_3 - K_2$. All options are on the same stock and have the same expiry $T$. Let $c_1, c_2, c_3$ be the call premiums. The payoff at $T$ is
>$$\max(S_T - K_1, 0) - 2\max(S_T - K_2, 0) + \max(S_T - K_3, 0) = \begin{cases} 0, & S_T \le K_1, \\ S_T - K_1, & K_1 < S_T \le K_2, \\ K_3 - S_T, & K_2 < S_T \le K_3, \\ 0, & S_T > K_3. \end{cases}$$
>The payoff is a tent. It rises from zero at $K_1$ to a peak of $K_2 - K_1$ at $K_2$, and falls back to zero at $K_3$. The net premium is $D = c_1 + c_3 - 2c_2$. The payoff is never negative, so by no [[4.2.2a Arbitrage|arbitrage]] the spread cannot cost less than zero. This gives the **strike-convexity bound**
>$$c_2 \le \frac{c_1 + c_3}{2}.$$
>In words, a call at the middle strike costs no more than the average of the calls at the outer strikes. Ignoring interest, the profit at $T$ is the payoff minus $D$. The break-even prices are $K_1 + D$ and $K_3 - D$. The maximum gain is $K_2 - K_1 - D$, at $S_T = K_2$. The maximum loss is $D$, for $S_T \le K_1$ or $S_T \ge K_3$. A butterfly bets that the stock ends near $K_2$. A short [[2.3.2c Straddle|straddle]] at $K_2$ takes the same view. It collects premium up front, but its loss has no limit. The butterfly's loss stops at $D$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Butterfly spreads do not appear in the lecture notes. The homework uses the butterfly portfolio to prove $c_2 \le (c_1 + c_3)/2$. Unequal spacing, put butterflies, and the link to state prices go beyond both.

# Intuition

A trader who expects the stock to stay near its current price wants to sell options and keep the premium. Selling two calls at $K_2$ does that, but it loses without limit if the stock rises. Buying one call at $K_1$ and one at $K_3$ removes that risk. Above $K_3$ the four calls cancel, so the payoff is zero on both sides. What remains is a payoff that is positive only near $K_2$, bought for a small premium. Traders call the middle strike the body and the outer strikes the wings.

The payoff never falls below zero, which is why the premium $D$ cannot be negative. The dashed profit line is the payoff shifted down by $D$. It is positive only between the break-even prices $K_1 + D$ and $K_3 - D$.

# Properties

#### *(Two Vertical Spreads)* A butterfly is a bull call spread on $[K_1, K_2]$ plus a bear call spread on $[K_2, K_3]$.

The bull call spread buys the $K_1$ call and sells one $K_2$ call. The bear call spread sells the other $K_2$ call and buys the $K_3$ call. The premium splits the same way,
$$D = (c_1 - c_2) - (c_2 - c_3).$$
The first bracket is the drop in the call price from $K_1$ to $K_2$, and the second is the drop from $K_2$ to $K_3$. The bound $D \ge 0$ says the call price falls by less over the higher interval. So the call price, as a function of the strike, falls at a slower and slower rate. A function with this shape is called convex. It follows that a [[2.3.2a Vertical Spread|vertical spread]] of fixed width costs less the higher its strikes are.

#### *(Unequal Spacing)* With unequal spacing the bound becomes a weighted average, with weights set by the distances between the strikes.

Let $K_1 < K_2 < K_3$ be any three strikes. Set $\lambda = (K_3 - K_2)/(K_3 - K_1)$, so that $K_2 = \lambda K_1 + (1 - \lambda)K_3$. Buy $\lambda$ calls at $K_1$, buy $1 - \lambda$ calls at $K_3$, and sell one call at $K_2$. The payoff is again a tent with its peak at $K_2$ and zero outside $[K_1, K_3]$. It is never negative, so
$$c_2 \le \lambda c_1 + (1 - \lambda)c_3.$$
The weight $\lambda$ on $c_1$ is larger when $K_2$ is closer to $K_1$. With equal spacing $\lambda = 1/2$, and the bound in the callout returns. With unequal spacing the plain average $(c_1 + c_3)/2$ is the wrong test. Since $c_1 > c_3$, it understates the bound whenever $K_2$ lies closer to $K_1$.

#### *(Put Butterfly)* The same tent can be built from puts, and put-call parity makes it cost the same.

A put butterfly buys puts at $K_1$ and $K_3$ and sells two puts at $K_2$. Its payoff at $T$ is the same tent. With $S$ the stock price today, [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity|put-call parity]] gives $c_i - p_i = S - K_ie^{-rT}$ at each strike. Combine the three identities with weights $1$, $-2$, and $1$:
$$(c_1 - 2c_2 + c_3) - (p_1 - 2p_2 + p_3) = (1 - 2 + 1)S - (K_1 - 2K_2 + K_3)e^{-rT} = 0.$$
The share terms cancel, and the strike terms cancel because $K_2$ is the midpoint. The two butterflies therefore cost the same, and puts also satisfy $p_2 \le (p_1 + p_3)/2$. A trader can use whichever options are easier to trade.

#### *(State Prices)* A narrow butterfly, scaled by its spacing, prices a claim that pays $\$1$ when the stock ends near $K_2$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Risk-neutral densities and their link to butterflies are not covered in the modules.

Let $h = K_2 - K_1$ and hold $1/h$ butterflies. The payoff is a tent of height $1$ on $[K_2 - h, K_2 + h]$. For small $h$ it acts like a claim that pays $\$1$ if $S_T$ lands within $h/2$ of $K_2$. Such a claim is an [[4.2.2b Arrow-Debreu Securities|Arrow-Debreu security]] for that range of prices. Dividing the price by $h$ and letting $h \to 0$ gives
$$\frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial K^2} = e^{-rT}\hat f(K),$$
where $\hat f$ is the risk-neutral density of $S_T$. Take a stock at $\$40$ with three months to expiry, $r = 4\%$, and call prices from the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]] at $35\%$ volatility. The $39$ / $40$ / $41$ butterfly costs $\$0.0563$. The [[2.2c.11 Lognormal Distribution|lognormal]] density gives $e^{-rT}\hat f(40) = 0.0564$. The market charges about $5.6$ cents for $\$1$ paid if the stock ends between $\$39.50$ and $\$40.50$. The convexity bound says that this price, like any density, is never negative.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Profit From a Butterfly
>A stock trades at $\$40$. Three-month European calls with strikes $\$36$, $\$40$, and $\$44$ cost $\$5.37$, $\$2.98$, and $\$1.47$. A trader expects the stock to stay near $\$40$ and buys the butterfly.
>
>>[!example]- Find the net premium, the break-even prices, the maximum gain, and the profit at expiry if the stock ends at $\$35$, $\$39$, $\$42$, or $\$46$.
>>The net premium is $D = 5.37 + 1.47 - 2 \times 2.98 = \$0.88$. The break-even prices are $36 + 0.88 = \$36.88$ and $44 - 0.88 = \$43.12$. The maximum gain is $4 - 0.88 = \$3.12$, at $S_T = 40$.
>>
>>At $S_T = 35$ all calls expire worthless, and the trader loses $\$0.88$. At $S_T = 39$ only the $\$36$ call pays, $\$3$, so the profit is $\$2.12$. At $S_T = 42$ the $\$36$ call pays $\$6$ and the two $\$40$ calls cost $\$4$, so the payoff is $\$2$ and the profit is $\$1.12$. At $S_T = 46$ the payoff is $10 - 12 + 2 = 0$, and the trader loses $\$0.88$.

>[!example] (Medium) A Put Butterfly Priced Against the Calls
>A stock trades at $\$40$ and pays no dividends. The risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding. The three-month European calls from the previous example cost $\$5.37$, $\$2.98$, and $\$1.47$ at strikes $\$36$, $\$40$, and $\$44$. Three-month European puts at the same strikes are quoted at $\$1.01$, $\$2.66$, and $\$5.03$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the cost of the put butterfly, compare it with the call butterfly, build a trade that profits from the gap, and find the quote that causes it.
>>The put butterfly buys the $\$36$ and $\$44$ puts and sells two $\$40$ puts. It costs $1.01 + 5.03 - 2 \times 2.66 = \$0.72$. The call butterfly costs $\$0.88$. Both pay the same tent at expiry, so they should cost the same.
>>
>>Buy the put butterfly for $\$0.72$ and sell the call butterfly for $\$0.88$. The trader receives $\$0.16$ today. At expiry the long tent and the short tent cancel. At $S_T = 38$, for example, the puts pay $0 - 2 \times 2 + 6 = \$2$ and the calls owe $\$2$. The net payoff is zero in every state, so the $\$0.16$ is a sure profit.
>>
>>Parity finds the bad quote. At each strike, $p = c - S + Ke^{-rT}$. This gives $5.37 - 40 + 35.64 = \$1.01$ at $\$36$, $2.98 - 40 + 39.60 = \$2.58$ at $\$40$, and $1.47 - 40 + 43.56 = \$5.03$ at $\$44$. The $\$40$ put is quoted $\$0.08$ too high. The trade sells two of these puts, which accounts for the gap of $2 \times 0.08 = \$0.16$.

>[!example] (Hard) Testing Unequal Strikes
>A stock trades at $\$46$ and pays no dividends. The risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding. Three-month European calls are quoted at $\$7.21$ (strike $\$40$), $\$4.50$ (strike $\$45$), and $\$0.78$ (strike $\$55$). A colleague claims an arbitrage, because $4.50 > (7.21 + 0.78)/2 = \$4.00$.
>
>>[!example]- Check the colleague's trade, apply the correct test, and find which quotes for the $\$45$ call would allow an arbitrage.
>>The colleague's trade sells two $\$45$ calls and buys the $\$40$ and $\$55$ calls. It receives $2 \times 4.50 - 7.21 - 0.78 = \$1.01$. For $S_T \ge 55$ it pays $(S_T - 40) + (S_T - 55) - 2(S_T - 45) = -\$5$. The trade can lose about $\$4$, so it is no arbitrage. The strikes are not equally spaced, so the tent does not close.
>>
>>The correct weight is $\lambda = (55 - 45)/(55 - 40) = 2/3$. The bound is
>>$$c(45) \le \frac{2}{3}(7.21) + \frac{1}{3}(0.78) = 4.81 + 0.26 = \$5.07.$$
>>The quote of $\$4.50$ satisfies it, so these prices allow no butterfly arbitrage.
>>
>>Any quote above $\$5.07$ would. Suppose the $\$45$ call trades at $\$5.20$. Multiply the weights by $3$ to get whole contracts. Sell three $\$45$ calls for $\$15.60$, and buy two $\$40$ calls and one $\$55$ call for $\$14.42 + \$0.78 = \$15.20$. The trader receives $\$0.40$ today. The payoff is $2\max(S_T - 40, 0) - 3\max(S_T - 45, 0) + \max(S_T - 55, 0)$. It is $0$ below $\$40$, peaks at $\$10$ at $\$45$, falls to $0$ at $\$55$, and stays at $0$ above.
