>[!note] Market Timing of Security Issuance
>**Market timing** is the observation that firms issue equity (and certain types of debt) when those securities appear overvalued and repurchase them when they appear undervalued. The pattern is the central prediction of Perspective 1 in [[8.13. Behavioral Corporate Finance|behavioral corporate finance]]: a rational manager who cares about long-run firm value should issue overpriced securities to existing shareholders' benefit and repurchase underpriced ones. The empirical record across equity, junk debt, and long-term debt confirms the pattern.
>
>**The Stein ($1996$) framework.** A rational manager who maximizes long-run firm value behaves as a contrarian against current sentiment. When investors are too optimistic about the firm's prospects, the manager issues equity at the inflated price, raising capital cheap relative to its long-run cost. When investors are too pessimistic, she repurchases shares cheap relative to long-run value. The strategy creates value for *long-run* shareholders even if it disadvantages current short-run buyers — and Stein argues that the long-run-shareholder objective is the right benchmark for a manager whose tenure outlasts current sentiment cycles.
>
>**Equity issuance — Loughran-Ritter ($1995$).** Firms that issue equity in IPOs and seasoned equity offerings (SEOs) underperform comparable non-issuing firms by approximately $5$–$8$ percentage points per year over the five years after issuance. The underperformance implies that issuing firms were on average overvalued at issuance, and that managers issued *because* of the overvaluation. Firms that *repurchase* equity (open-market buybacks) outperform comparable non-repurchasers by a similar magnitude over the following years (Ikenberry-Lakonishok-Vermaelen, $1995$). Both patterns are inconsistent with rational pricing of the issuance/repurchase decision but are exactly what market timing predicts.
>
>**Aggregate market timing — Baker-Wurgler ($2000$).** The ratio of equity issuance to total external finance (equity plus debt) varies dramatically over time. Baker and Wurgler show that high values of this *equity share* predict *low* future stock-market returns over the next $1$–$5$ years. Aggregate managers, in other words, lean toward equity issuance when the aggregate market is overvalued, just as Stein's framework predicts. The result is one of the cleanest aggregate-level tests of market timing.
>
>**Junk-debt and long-term-debt timing.** Greenwood-Hanson ($2013$) extend the Baker-Wurgler logic to junk debt: the share of junk-debt issuance in total corporate-debt issuance predicts *low* future returns on junk bonds, indicating issuance-timing in the junk-bond market. Baker-Greenwood-Wurgler ($2003$) document that the share of long-term debt in total corporate-debt issuance predicts the slope of the future yield curve, with managers issuing long-term debt when long-term debt is overpriced relative to short-term debt.
>
>**CFO survey evidence.** Graham-Harvey ($2001$) survey CFOs about issuance decisions. Roughly two-thirds of respondents agree that "the degree to which our stock is under- or over-valued is an important consideration when issuing equity." The CFOs themselves are explicit that they think about market timing — not a hidden mechanism but a stated managerial practice.

# Intuition

The most basic asset-pricing intuition says that issuing securities at the prevailing market price is value-neutral for existing shareholders: existing shareholders give up a fractional claim and receive the corresponding cash, so their total wealth is unchanged. This neutrality breaks down when the market price differs from the manager's estimate of true firm value. If the market price is *above* true value, issuing is a transfer from new shareholders (who pay too much) to existing shareholders (who hand over a less-valuable claim than they receive in cash). The opposite holds for repurchase: buying back below true value transfers from selling shareholders to those who remain.

A rational manager who maximizes the wealth of long-run shareholders therefore has a strong incentive to lean against sentiment-driven pricing errors. The two prerequisites for this strategy to operate are (i) the market is in fact mispriced — requiring [[8.4. Noise|limits to arbitrage]] — and (ii) the manager has an informational advantage that lets her detect the mispricing. The first is given by the limits-to-arbitrage pillar of behavioral finance; the second is generally accepted (managers know more about their own firm's prospects than outside investors do).

The market-timing implication is then that issuance should cluster in periods of high valuation and repurchase in periods of low valuation. The Loughran-Ritter and Baker-Wurgler results document exactly this pattern at both the firm level (issuing firms subsequently underperform) and the aggregate level (high equity-share periods predict low aggregate returns). The Greenwood-Hanson and Baker-Greenwood-Wurgler results extend the pattern to debt markets. The Graham-Harvey survey closes the loop by confirming that managers themselves describe their issuance decisions in market-timing terms.

# Properties

#### *(Stein 1996 Logic)* Issue when overvalued, repurchase when undervalued.

Stein ($1996$) develops a stylized model of an irrational stock market and a rational long-run-value-maximizing manager. The optimal issuance policy is fully contrarian against sentiment: issue equity when the market is willing to pay above true value; repurchase when the market is selling below true value. The key observation is that this strategy creates value for *long-run* shareholders without requiring the manager to time the market for trading purposes — it simply takes advantage of asymmetric information about firm value while the asymmetry exists.

#### *(Loughran-Ritter 1995)* Equity issuers underperform by $5$–$8$ pp annually.

Loughran and Ritter ($1995$) compare the long-run stock returns of firms that issued equity (IPOs, SEOs) to a matched sample of non-issuing firms with comparable size and book-to-market. Issuing firms underperform by roughly $5$–$8$ percentage points per year over the five years after issuance, with the gap appearing in both economic and statistical significance. The result is interpreted as evidence that issuance was timed to periods of overvaluation: the subsequent underperformance is the unwinding of the overvaluation. Ikenberry, Lakonishok, and Vermaelen ($1995$) document the symmetric pattern for repurchases: firms that announce open-market repurchases outperform by a similar magnitude over the following years.

#### *(Baker-Wurgler 2000)* Aggregate equity share predicts negative market returns.

Baker and Wurgler ($2000$) construct the aggregate equity share — the ratio of equity issuance to total external finance (equity plus debt) for U.S. nonfinancial firms in each year. They find that high equity-share years are followed by low aggregate stock-market returns over the following one to five years. The relation is robust to controlling for the price-dividend ratio and other return predictors, suggesting that the equity share carries independent information about market overvaluation. The interpretation is that aggregate managers behave as a class of rational market timers, leaning against aggregate sentiment.

#### *(Junk-Debt Timing — Greenwood-Hanson 2013)* Junk-debt share predicts negative junk-bond returns.

Greenwood and Hanson ($2013$) construct the analogue for the junk-bond market: the share of below-investment-grade (junk) issuance in total corporate-debt issuance. High junk-debt-share years are followed by low excess returns on junk bonds. Managers, in other words, time the junk-bond market in the same way they time the equity market, issuing junk when junk is overpriced. The result extends the market-timing pattern beyond equities into a major segment of the debt market.

#### *(Yield-Curve Timing — Baker-Greenwood-Wurgler 2003)* Long-debt share predicts yield-curve flattening.

Baker, Greenwood, and Wurgler ($2003$) document that the share of long-term debt in total corporate debt issuance predicts the *slope* of the future yield curve. When firms issue more long-term debt, long-term yields subsequently fall relative to short-term yields — consistent with managers issuing long-term debt when long-term debt is overpriced relative to short-term. The finding indicates that the market-timing instinct extends not just across the equity-debt margin but also along the maturity dimension within the debt market.

#### *(Survey Confirmation — Graham-Harvey 2001)* Two-thirds of CFOs cite valuation in issuance decisions.

The Graham-Harvey ($2001$) CFO survey directly asks the underlying question: do managers consider over- or undervaluation when issuing equity? Approximately two-thirds of respondents say yes. The survey closes the loop on the empirical-pattern evidence: market timing is not just a statistical regularity that researchers infer from issuance and return data, it is a stated managerial practice. CFOs are conscious participants in the market-timing strategy that the academic literature documents.

#### *(Why Arbitrage Cannot Eliminate the Pattern)* Manager has informational advantage and fewer constraints.

A natural question: if managers can systematically detect overvaluation and exploit it through issuance, why don't outside arbitrageurs do the same by shorting the overvalued stocks? The Stein-Baker-Wurgler answer is that managers face *fewer* constraints than arbitrageurs. Issuing equity is operationally simple — file a registration, conduct the offering — while shorting requires borrowing shares (often costly), facing margin calls, and accepting career-risk implications when the trade goes against you in the short run (see [[8.4. Noise|noise-trader risk]]). Managers also have superior information about their own firm. The asymmetric availability of the trade explains why the pattern is robust to arbitrage.

# Examples

>[!example] An IPO at the peak of a sentiment cycle
>A technology firm goes public in early $2000$, with the offering priced at $\$50$ per share. Investor enthusiasm for tech stocks is at a historic peak; the firm's price-earnings ratio at issuance is $80$. Loughran-Ritter ($1995$) predict that this firm should subsequently underperform comparable non-issuing firms by $5$–$8$ percentage points per year. Empirically, the post-$2000$ tech-IPO cohort underperformed the broader market by even larger margins through $2002$. The IPO timing was a transfer from new shareholders (who paid sentiment-inflated prices) to existing shareholders and the firm's coffers (which received cheap capital).

>[!example]- Aggregate equity share in 1929 and 2000
>Baker-Wurgler's equity-share series spikes to historic highs in $1929$ and again around $2000$ — both periods immediately preceding well-known market crashes. The aggregate firm-side decision to issue equity rather than debt at those moments was, in retrospect, well-timed: managers as a class shifted toward equity issuance precisely when equity was most overvalued, and the post-$1929$ and post-$2000$ markets confirmed the overvaluation through subsequent steep declines. The pattern is what makes the equity share a good predictor of future market returns.

>[!example]- Junk-bond timing in 2007
>Greenwood-Hanson ($2013$) document that the junk-debt share peaked in $2006$–$2007$ at historic highs. Subsequent returns on junk bonds — particularly through the $2008$ financial crisis — were sharply negative. The pattern fits the market-timing prediction: firms issued junk debt when the junk market was hot and underwriting standards were loose, and the subsequent unwinding of that overvaluation produced large losses for buyers. The aggregate-level test is identical in structure to the equity-share test of Baker-Wurgler.
