>[!note] Beta-Binomial Conjugacy
>Let $\theta \in [0, 1]$ be an unknown Bernoulli parameter (success probability). The **Beta-Binomial conjugate pair** is the prior-likelihood combination
>$$\theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta), \qquad y \mid \theta \sim \text{Binomial}(n, \theta),$$
>which produces the closed-form posterior
>$$\theta \mid y \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + y,\ \beta + n - y).$$
>The prior hyperparameters $(\alpha, \beta)$ update by adding successes $y$ to $\alpha$ and failures $n - y$ to $\beta$. Equivalently, the prior is interpreted as $\alpha$ "pseudo-successes" and $\beta$ "pseudo-failures" observed before the data.
>
>**Posterior summaries:**
>- **Posterior mean**: $\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = \frac{\alpha + y}{\alpha + \beta + n}$ — a weighted average of the prior mean $\alpha / (\alpha + \beta)$ and the MLE $y/n$, with weights proportional to prior and data sample sizes.
>- **Posterior mode (MAP)**: $\frac{\alpha + y - 1}{\alpha + \beta + n - 2}$ when $\alpha + y \ge 1$ and $\beta + n - y \ge 1$; equals the MLE under a uniform prior $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$.
>- **Posterior variance**: $\frac{(\alpha + y)(\beta + n - y)}{(\alpha + \beta + n)^2(\alpha + \beta + n + 1)}$; shrinks like $O(1/n)$.
>
>**Common prior choices:**
>- $\text{Beta}(1, 1) = \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$: flat prior; posterior is $\text{Beta}(1 + y,\ 1 + n - y)$ and the MAP equals the MLE.
>- $\text{Beta}(0.5, 0.5)$ (Jeffreys prior): noninformative prior based on the Fisher information, $p(\theta) \propto \theta^{-1/2}(1-\theta)^{-1/2}$; has a U-shape favoring extremes.
>- $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ with small $\alpha + \beta$: weakly informative; centered at $\alpha / (\alpha + \beta)$ with "prior sample size" $\alpha + \beta$.
>
>**Posterior predictive** for a new flip or new batch of flips:
>- Single new flip: $P(\tilde y = 1 \mid y) = \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = \frac{\alpha + y}{\alpha + \beta + n}$.
>- $m$ new flips: $\tilde y \mid y \sim \text{BetaBin}(m,\ \alpha + y,\ \beta + n - y)$, a beta-binomial with variance strictly larger than the plug-in $\text{Binomial}(m, \hat\theta)$.
>
>The $k$-success form (Bernoulli likelihood) generalizes trivially: for $n$ i.i.d. Bernoulli observations $y_1, \dots, y_n$ with $\sum y_i = y$, the posterior is identical to the single-binomial case.

# Intuition

The Beta prior's density $\theta^{\alpha - 1}(1 - \theta)^{\beta - 1}$ has exactly the same *shape* in $\theta$ as the binomial likelihood $\theta^y(1 - \theta)^{n - y}$. Multiplying the two adds exponents: $\alpha - 1 + y$ and $\beta - 1 + n - y$. Renormalizing recognizes the result as another Beta density. This shape-matching is the entire mechanism — Beta is conjugate to Binomial because their densities live in the same two-parameter exponential family parameterized by powers of $\theta$ and $1 - \theta$.

The update has a clean pseudo-data interpretation. A $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ prior is statistically indistinguishable from observing $\alpha$ prior successes and $\beta$ prior failures. Once real data arrive, the posterior counts are just $\alpha + y$ successes and $\beta + n - y$ failures total. The ratio $\alpha / (\alpha + \beta)$ is the prior mean; the sum $\alpha + \beta$ is the effective prior sample size. Small $\alpha + \beta$ means weak prior and fast washout; large $\alpha + \beta$ means strong prior that competes with the data.

# Properties

#### *(Posterior Mean as Shrinkage)* The posterior mean is a convex combination of the prior mean and the MLE.

Writing $\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = y/n$ and $\mu_0 = \alpha / (\alpha + \beta)$ for the prior mean,
$$\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = \frac{\alpha + y}{\alpha + \beta + n} = \underbrace{\frac{\alpha + \beta}{\alpha + \beta + n}}_{w_0}\,\mu_0 + \underbrace{\frac{n}{\alpha + \beta + n}}_{w_d}\,\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}},$$
with weights summing to $1$. When $n \ll \alpha + \beta$ the posterior is dominated by the prior; when $n \gg \alpha + \beta$ it is dominated by the MLE. The boundary $n \approx \alpha + \beta$ is the regime where both contribute comparably. This shrinkage structure is the reason Bayesian inference stabilizes estimates for small $n$: the MLE $y/n$ has variance $\theta(1 - \theta)/n$ that blows up relative to the parameter's range for $n$ of order $1$, while the posterior mean is automatically pulled toward the prior and never degenerates.

#### *(Uniform Prior Recovers MLE)* A $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$ prior makes the MAP equal the MLE.

Plugging $\alpha = \beta = 1$ into the MAP formula gives $\frac{y}{n}$, exactly the binomial MLE. This is a general pattern: under a flat prior, Bayesian MAP estimation coincides with maximum likelihood, because the prior does not shift the location of the mode. The posterior mean, however, differs from the MLE even under a flat prior — $(y + 1)/(n + 2)$ rather than $y/n$ — because the Beta density is asymmetric unless $\alpha = \beta$.

#### *(Jeffreys Prior)* The $\text{Beta}(1/2, 1/2)$ prior is the Jeffreys prior for the Bernoulli likelihood and is invariant under reparameterization.

The Jeffreys prior is $p(\theta) \propto \sqrt{\mathcal{I}(\theta)}$ where $\mathcal{I}$ is the Fisher information; for the Bernoulli, $\mathcal{I}(\theta) = 1/[\theta(1-\theta)]$, giving $p(\theta) \propto \theta^{-1/2}(1-\theta)^{-1/2}$, which is $\text{Beta}(1/2, 1/2)$. Its defining feature is that inference under the Jeffreys prior is invariant to one-to-one reparameterizations of $\theta$: posterior inference on $\theta$ and on, say, $\eta = \log[\theta/(1-\theta)]$ yields the same "objective" results. The Jeffreys prior is U-shaped, concentrating mass at $0$ and $1$, which can be a feature (encodes the principle of indifference on a bounded scale) or a bug (injects nontrivial information into the tails) depending on the application.

#### *(Predictive Distribution Is Beta-Binomial)* The posterior predictive distribution for $m$ new flips is a beta-binomial, not a binomial.

Marginalizing $\theta$ out of $m$ new flips,
$$P(\tilde y = k \mid y) = \int_0^1 \binom{m}{k}\theta^k(1-\theta)^{m-k}\,p(\theta \mid y)\,d\theta = \binom{m}{k}\,\frac{B(\alpha + y + k,\ \beta + n - y + m - k)}{B(\alpha + y,\ \beta + n - y)},$$
which is the $\text{BetaBin}(m,\ \alpha + y,\ \beta + n - y)$ pmf. Its variance is
$$\mathrm{Var}(\tilde y \mid y) = m\,\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y]\,(1 - \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y])\,\frac{\alpha + \beta + n + m}{\alpha + \beta + n + 1},$$
strictly larger than the plug-in binomial variance $m\,\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y](1 - \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y])$ — the inflation factor exceeds $1$ and accounts for posterior uncertainty in $\theta$. The inflation vanishes as $n \to \infty$ and the posterior concentrates.

#### *(Sequential Update)* The posterior after observing $k$ flips is the prior for the $(k+1)$-th flip.

Beta-Binomial conjugacy is sequentially self-updating: if $\theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ a priori and we observe flips one at a time, after seeing $y_1, \dots, y_k$ with total successes $s_k$, the posterior is $\text{Beta}(\alpha + s_k,\ \beta + k - s_k)$. Using this as the prior for flip $k + 1$ and updating with the single new observation produces $\text{Beta}(\alpha + s_{k+1},\ \beta + k + 1 - s_{k+1})$, the same as updating with all $k + 1$ flips at once. This is the concrete form of the **sequential updating** property from [[3.1 Bayesian Framework]] for this conjugate pair.

#### *(Laplace's Rule of Succession)* Under a uniform prior, the posterior probability that the next flip is a success is $(y + 1)/(n + 2)$.

Plugging $\alpha = \beta = 1$ into the posterior mean gives $P(\tilde y = 1 \mid y) = (y + 1)/(n + 2)$, known as **Laplace's rule of succession**. It is the oldest explicit Bayesian predictive formula (Laplace, 1774) and a classic example of the benefit of shrinkage: after seeing $n = 0$ flips the predictive probability is $1/2$ (not undefined); after seeing $y = n$ successes the predictive is $(n + 1)/(n + 2)$ (not the degenerate $1$). The MLE's pathologies at the boundary are gone.

# Examples

>[!example]- Three new flips after a uniform prior
>Place a $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$ (uniform) prior on $\theta$ and observe $y = 2$ heads in $n = 3$ flips. The posterior is $\theta \mid y \sim \text{Beta}(3, 2)$.
>
>**Posterior mean**: $\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = 3/5 = 0.6$. **Posterior mode (MAP)**: $(3 - 1)/(3 + 2 - 2) = 2/3 \approx 0.667$. The MAP equals the MLE $2/3$, as expected for the uniform prior.
>
>**$95\%$ equal-tail credible interval** for $\theta$: using the $\text{Beta}(3, 2)$ quantiles,
>$$[q_{0.025},\ q_{0.975}] \approx [0.194,\ 0.932].$$
>The Wald confidence interval $\hat\theta \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat\theta(1 - \hat\theta)/n} = 0.667 \pm 0.533 = [0.13, 1.20]$ is much wider and extends past $1$ — the Bayesian interval is automatically bounded inside $[0, 1]$.
>
>**Predictive for $m = 3$ new flips**: $\tilde y \mid y \sim \text{BetaBin}(3, 3, 2)$. Evaluating the pmf,
>$$P(\tilde y = 0) = \tfrac{1}{10},\ P(\tilde y = 1) = \tfrac{3}{10},\ P(\tilde y = 2) = \tfrac{3}{10},\ P(\tilde y = 3) = \tfrac{3}{10}.$$
>
>The plug-in binomial $\text{Bin}(3, 0.6)$ would give $P(\tilde y = 3) = 0.6^3 = 0.216$; the beta-binomial's $0.3$ correctly accounts for posterior uncertainty, giving fatter tails.

>[!example]- Using a strongly informative prior
>A manufacturer tests a new coin and claims the bias is very close to fair: $\theta \approx 0.5$ with $95\%$ prior certainty. Encode this as $\text{Beta}(100, 100)$, which has mean $0.5$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{100 \cdot 100 / (200^2 \cdot 201)} \approx 0.035$. A tester flips the coin $20$ times and observes $y = 15$ heads.
>
>**MLE-only analysis**: $\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = 15/20 = 0.75$; the coin appears strongly biased.
>
>**Bayesian posterior**: $\theta \mid y \sim \text{Beta}(115, 105)$ with posterior mean $115/220 \approx 0.523$, barely shifted from the prior mean $0.5$. Posterior variance is $\approx 0.00113$, standard deviation $\approx 0.034$ — essentially the prior's width. The strong prior swamps the small sample.
>
>$95\%$ credible interval: $[0.455, 0.589]$. The point estimate of $0.75$ from the MLE is *excluded* by the Bayesian credible interval. To override the prior, the tester needs substantially more data — if the coin is truly biased at $0.75$, the posterior mean will cross $0.6$ only after roughly $n \approx 100$ observations with the same success rate. This illustrates both the strength (prior pulls toward believable values) and the risk (misspecified prior suppresses real signal) of Bayesian inference with informative priors.

>[!example]- Sequential updating
>Start with $\text{Beta}(2, 2)$ and observe three flips in sequence: T, H, T.
>
>After flip 1 (T): posterior is $\text{Beta}(2, 3)$, mean $2/5 = 0.4$.
>After flip 2 (H): posterior is $\text{Beta}(3, 3)$, mean $3/6 = 0.5$.
>After flip 3 (T): posterior is $\text{Beta}(3, 4)$, mean $3/7 \approx 0.429$.
>
>Updating with all three flips at once: prior $\text{Beta}(2, 2)$, likelihood $\text{Bin}(3, \theta)$ with $y = 1$. Posterior: $\text{Beta}(2 + 1, 2 + 2) = \text{Beta}(3, 4)$. Identical to the sequential result, as the sequential-updating property guarantees. Every batch of data can be absorbed in any order or granularity; the final posterior depends only on the total successes and total failures, which is the **sufficient statistic** for the Bernoulli model.
