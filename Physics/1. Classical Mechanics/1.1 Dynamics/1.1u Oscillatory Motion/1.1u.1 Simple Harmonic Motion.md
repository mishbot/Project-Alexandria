>[!note] Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)
>Any oscillating system for which the net restoring force is directly proportional to the negative of the displacement is said to exhibit **simple harmonic motion (SHM)**, as it is sinusoidal with a single frequency. Such a system is often called a **simple harmonic oscillator (SHO)**. Due to its sinusoidal nature, the displacement of any system in simple harmonic motion can be modeled as
>$$x = x_{0}\cos(\omega t + \phi),$$
>where $x_{0}$ is the waveform's amplitude, representing the initial displacement of the oscillator from its equilibrium position), $\omega = 2\pi f = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$ is its natural frequency, and $\phi$ is its phase.
>
>The total energy of the simple harmonic oscillator is then given by
>$$E=\frac{1}{2}m\omega^2x_{0}^2\sin^2(\omega t+\phi)+\frac{1}{2}kx_{0}^2\cos^2(\omega t + \phi).$$

# Intuition
We start with Newton's second law: $\sum F=ma$. Since $a=\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}$, and the total force exerted by a spring is given as $\sum  F =-kx$. Therefore,
$$m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}=-kx \iff \frac{d^2x}{dt^2}+\frac{k}{m}x=0.$$
This is an un(der)damped ($\zeta = \frac{\alpha}{\omega_{0}}=0$) second order differential equation with natural frequency $\omega_{n}=\sqrt{ \frac{k}{m} }.$ Solving for it by definition, we get
$$\begin{align}
x(t)&=K_{1}\cos(\omega_{n}t)+K_{2}\sin(\omega_{n}t).
\end{align}$$
At $t=0$, $x(0)=K_{1}\cos(\omega_{n}0)=K_{1}=x_{0}$. Therefore, $K_{1}=x_{0}$.

If we take the derivative of $x(t)$, we get:
$$\dot{x}(t)=-\omega_{n}x_{0}\sin(\omega_{n}t)+\omega_{n}K_{2}\cos(\omega_{n}t).$$
Setting $t=0$, we get 
$\dot{x}(0)=\omega_{n}K_{2}=\dot{x_{0}}=v_{0}$. Therefore, $K_{2}=\frac{v_{0}}{\omega_{n}}$. We find that the general solution to the simple harmonic model is
$$x(t)=x_{0}\cos(\omega_{n} t)+\frac{v_{0}}{\omega_{n}}\sin(\omega_{n} t),$$
where $\omega_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$. If we assume that $v_{0}=0$ (i.e. the SHO starts motion after $t=0$), we get the final form,
$$x(t)=x_{0}\cos(\sqrt{ \frac{k}{m} }t).$$
