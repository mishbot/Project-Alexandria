>[!note] Perfect Complements
>Two goods are **perfect complements** when the consumer values them only in a fixed ratio, like left and right shoes. Their utility function is
>$$u(x_1, x_2) = \min(\alpha x_1, \beta x_2), \qquad \alpha, \beta > 0.$$
>Utility counts complete sets, where one set holds $1/\alpha$ units of good 1 and $1/\beta$ units of good 2. Indifference curves are L-shaped, with their corners on the ray $\alpha x_1 = \beta x_2$, that is, $x_2 = (\alpha/\beta)x_1$. On the horizontal arm good 1 is in excess and the [[6.1.4 Indifference Curves and MRS|MRS]] is $0$. On the vertical arm good 2 is in excess and the curve is vertical. At the corner the MRS is undefined, because $u$ has no derivative there. The consumer buys no unmatched units, so the optimum lies on the ray and on the budget line. At prices $\mathbf{p} = (p_1, p_2)$ and income $M$, the [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|Marshallian demands]] are
>$$x_1^*(\mathbf{p}, M) = \frac{\beta M}{\beta p_1 + \alpha p_2}, \qquad x_2^*(\mathbf{p}, M) = \frac{\alpha M}{\beta p_1 + \alpha p_2},$$
>which reduce to $x_1^* = x_2^* = M/(p_1 + p_2)$ when $\alpha = \beta$. Both goods are normal and gross complements, since a rise in either price lowers demand for both. One set costs $p_1/\alpha + p_2/\beta$. That cost gives the [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function|indirect utility]], the [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization|expenditure function]], and the Hicksian demands,
>$$v(\mathbf{p}, M) = \frac{M}{p_1/\alpha + p_2/\beta}, \qquad e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = \bar u\left(\frac{p_1}{\alpha} + \frac{p_2}{\beta}\right), \qquad h_1 = \frac{\bar u}{\alpha}, \quad h_2 = \frac{\bar u}{\beta},$$
>where $\bar u$ is the target utility. Hicksian demand does not depend on prices, so the substitution effect is zero and every price effect is an income effect. Perfect complements with $\alpha = \beta$ are the $\rho \to -\infty$ limit of [[6.4d CES Utility|CES utility]]. They are the opposite extreme from [[6.4b Perfect Substitutes|perfect substitutes]] and model goods that follow a fixed recipe.

# Intuition

Some goods are useful only together. A left shoe without a right shoe is worthless, and so is gasoline in an engine with no oil. Extra units of one good add nothing unless the consumer also has the matching units of the other. The consumer therefore never buys unmatched units. The only decision is how many complete sets the budget buys. A change in relative prices cannot make the consumer swap one good for the other, since the recipe does not allow it. The model gives up all substitution. It is exact for recipes and too rigid for most other pairs of goods.

The diagram uses $u = \min(x_1, 2x_2)$, so one set is $1$ unit of good 1 and $1/2$ unit of good 2. With $p_1 = 1$ and $p_2 = 2$ a set costs $1 + 1 = 2$. Income $M = 8$ buys $4$ sets, which is the bundle $A = (4, 2)$ at the corner of the curve $u = 4$. Any bundle on the budget line away from $A$ sits on an arm of a lower L, where the extra units are wasted.

# Properties

#### *(Reading the Weights)* The coefficient on good 1 fixes how much of good 2 the consumer wants.

On the kink ray the quantity ratio is $x_2/x_1 = \alpha/\beta$. In $\min(x_1, 2x_2)$ the consumer wants two units of good 1 per unit of good 2, although the $2$ sits on good 2. A recipe that uses $a$ units of good 1 and $b$ units of good 2 per set is $u = \min(x_1/a, x_2/b)$, so $\alpha = 1/a$ and $\beta = 1/b$. In this form the consumer buys $M/(ap_1 + bp_2)$ sets, with $x_1^* = a$ times the number of sets and $x_2^* = b$ times it. A positive factor in front, as in $5\min(x_1, x_2)$, changes the utility numbers and nothing else.

#### *(Tangency Fails)* The Lagrangian method cannot be used, because $u$ is not differentiable at the optimum.

The first-order conditions $u'_{x_i} = \lambda p_i$ need both marginal utilities, and neither exists at the kink. On the horizontal arm $u'_{x_1} = 0$, and on the vertical arm $u'_{x_2} = 0$, so the conditions fail there with $\lambda > 0$. At the kink, budget lines of every negative slope touch the L at that single point. The condition $\text{MRS} = -p_1/p_2$ therefore gives no information. On an exam, write the ray $\alpha x_1 = \beta x_2$, substitute it into the budget line, and solve. The smoothness that [[7.2 Lagrange Multipliers]] requires is missing here.

#### *(No Waste)* The budget binds and the optimum is on the kink ray, even though $u$ is not strictly monotone.

At the kink, adding good 1 alone leaves utility unchanged. So $u$ is monotone but not strictly monotone, and the usual argument for a binding budget does not apply directly. A direct argument works instead. At a bundle off the ray, the unmatched units can be dropped and their cost spent on more complete sets. At a bundle on the ray inside the budget set, the leftover income buys more sets. Either way utility rises, so every optimum is on the ray and on the budget line.

#### *(Pure Income Effects)* A price change moves the consumer along the kink ray and never along an indifference curve.

Hicksian demand does not depend on prices, so the substitution effect in the [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]] is zero. The cross-price response is then all income effect,
$$\frac{\partial x_1^*}{\partial p_2} = -x_2^*\,\frac{\partial x_1^*}{\partial M} = -\frac{\alpha\beta M}{(\beta p_1 + \alpha p_2)^2} < 0.$$
Here the middle term is the income effect of the Slutsky equation. The cross effect $\partial h_1/\partial p_2$ is exactly zero. With two goods the compensated cross effect is otherwise positive, and [[6.3.6 Complements and Substitutes]] calls such goods net substitutes. Perfect complements are the boundary case where that effect is zero.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Tires and Inner Tubes
>A cyclist repairs bikes and needs one tire and one inner tube per wheel. Utility is $u = 4\min(x_1, x_2)$, where $x_1$ is tires at $p_1 = 12$ and $x_2$ is inner tubes at $p_2 = 3$. The cyclist has $M = 45$.
>
>>[!example]- Which bundle does the cyclist buy: $(0, 15)$, $(3.75, 0)$, or $(3, 3)$?
>>The cyclist buys matched pairs. One pair costs $12 + 3 = 15$, so $M = 45$ buys $3$ pairs. The bundle is $(3, 3)$ with $u = 4 \times 3 = 12$. The formula gives the same answer, $x_1^* = x_2^* = 45/(12 + 3) = 3$.
>>
>>The bundle $(0, 15)$ holds the most units, yet it gives utility $0$ because no tire matches the tubes. The bundle $(3.75, 0)$ also gives $0$. The factor $4$ does not affect the choice.

>[!example] (Medium) Tacos
>A student makes tacos. Each taco uses $1$ tortilla and $2$ ounces of filling, so utility is $u = \min(x_1, x_2/2)$ with $x_1$ in tortillas and $x_2$ in ounces. A tortilla costs $p_1 = 0.50$, filling costs $p_2 = 0.75$ per ounce, and the student has $M = 30$. The price of filling then rises to $p_2' = 1.00$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the demands before and after the rise. How are tortillas and filling related?
>>One taco costs $0.50 + 2 \times 0.75 = 2.00$, so the student makes $15$ tacos. Demand is $x_1^* = 15$ tortillas and $x_2^* = 30$ ounces. In the form $\min(\alpha x_1, \beta x_2)$ the weights are $\alpha = 1$ and $\beta = 1/2$. The formula gives $x_1^* = (0.5 \times 30)/(0.5 \times 0.50 + 1 \times 0.75) = 15$, which matches.
>>
>>After the rise a taco costs $0.50 + 2 \times 1.00 = 2.50$, so the student makes $12$ tacos. Demand falls to $x_1^* = 12$ tortillas and $x_2^* = 24$ ounces. Tortilla demand fell by $20\%$ although the tortilla price did not change. Tortillas and filling are gross complements.

>[!example] (Hard) Compensating the Taco Maker
>Take the student from the previous example. A parent wants to add the smallest amount of income that restores the old utility of $15$ tacos after the filling price rises.
>
>>[!example]- How much must the parent add? Split the change in tortilla demand into substitution and income effects.
>>The expenditure function gives $e(\mathbf{p}', 15) = 15 \times (0.50 + 2 \times 1.00) = 37.50$, so the parent adds $7.50$. The old bundle of $15$ tortillas and $30$ ounces also costs $0.50 \times 15 + 1.00 \times 30 = 37.50$ at the new prices. The compensation equals the extra cost of the old bundle exactly, because no cheaper way to reach $15$ tacos exists. For a Cobb-Douglas consumer the compensation would be smaller, since that consumer could substitute.
>>
>>Hicksian demand stays at $h_1 = 15$ tortillas, so the substitution effect is $0$. The whole fall from $15$ to $12$ tortillas is the income effect. The derivatives at the old prices confirm it. The total effect is $\partial x_1^*/\partial p_2 = -(1 \times 0.5 \times 30)/(0.5 \times 0.50 + 0.75)^2 = -15$. The income effect is $-x_2^*\,\partial x_1^*/\partial M = -30 \times 0.5 = -15$, which is the same number.
