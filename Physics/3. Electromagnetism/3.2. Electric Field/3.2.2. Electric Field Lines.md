>[!note] Electric Field Lines
>**Electric field lines** are curves whose direction at every point matches the [[3.2.1. Electric Field|electric field]] there. An arrow on each line shows the direction of the field. One picture of field lines shows both parts of the field:
>- **Direction**: the field at a point points along the line through it, in the direction of the arrow.
>- **Size**: the field is stronger where the lines are closer together. The number of lines crossing a small area perpendicular to them is proportional to the field's magnitude.
>
>Field lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges, or run off to infinity. The number of lines leaving a charge is drawn proportional to its size. A few standard pictures:
>- A positive point charge sends straight lines outward in all directions, and a negative charge draws them inward.
>- A uniform field, the same size and direction everywhere, is a set of parallel lines with equal spacing.
>- A dipole is a positive and a negative charge of equal size. Its lines curve from the positive charge around to the negative charge.
>- Two equal positive charges have lines that bend away from each other. The field is zero at the midpoint between them.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not state that the number of lines is proportional to the charge. This rule follows Giancoli section 21.8.

# Intuition

A field assigns an arrow to every point in space, which is far too many arrows to draw. Field lines join the arrows into curves. A line traces the direction a small positive charge would be pushed at each step. Where many lines crowd together, the field is strong. Where they spread apart, it is weak. Around a point charge the lines spread over spheres of growing area, so their density falls off as $1/r^2$. That matches Coulomb's law.

The animation shows six standard pictures, traced from the real fields. It starts with a positive charge, a negative charge, and a dipole. Then come two equal positive charges, two equal negative charges, and two oppositely charged plates.

# Properties

#### *(Edges of a Uniform Field)* Near the edges of two charged plates the lines bow outward, and the field there is weaker.

Between the middle parts of two large, oppositely charged plates, the lines are parallel and evenly spaced. At the edges nothing outside pulls the lines straight. They bulge outward and spread apart, so the field weakens. This edge effect is called **fringing**. It matters little when the plates are much wider than their separation, as in [[3.3.3b Parallel Conducting Plates]].

#### *(Lines Never Cross)* At every point the field has exactly one direction, so two field lines cannot cross.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not state this rule. It follows Giancoli section 21.8.

If two lines crossed, the point of crossing would have two different field directions. The superposition of all the charges gives a single vector at each point, so this cannot happen. The one exception is a point where the field is zero, such as the midpoint between two equal charges. There the field has no direction at all, and lines can meet or stop.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Reading a Picture
>A field-line drawing shows twelve lines leaving a small sphere and spreading out evenly. Points A and B lie $2\ \text{cm}$ and $6\ \text{cm}$ from its center.
>
>>[!example]- Is the sphere positive or negative, and how do the field strengths at A and B compare?
>>The lines leave the sphere, so it is positive. The same twelve lines cross a sphere of radius $2\ \text{cm}$ and a sphere of radius $6\ \text{cm}$. The larger sphere has nine times the area, so the lines are nine times more spread out. The field at A is nine times the field at B, matching $E \propto 1/r^2$.

>[!example] (Medium) Where Is the Field Zero?
>Two charges of $+Q$ sit a distance $d$ apart. A sketch of their field lines shows a point between them where no lines pass.
>
>>[!example]- Where is that point, and what is the field there?
>>By symmetry the point is the midpoint. There the two fields have equal size and point in opposite directions, so they cancel and the field is zero. A positive test charge placed at the midpoint feels no force. Moved a little toward either charge, it is pushed back toward the midpoint. Moved sideways along the bisector, it is pushed away. The midpoint is not a stable resting point.

>[!example] (Hard) A Line of Charge With Two Signs
>A long straight line carries positive charge above a point O and negative charge of the same density below it.
>
>>[!example]- Sketch the field lines near O and say which way the field points at a point P right beside O.
>>Lines leave the positive half and end on the negative half. Near O they arc from the upper half down to the lower half. At P the field points downward, parallel to the line of charge. The positive half pushes a test charge down, and the negative half pulls it down. The two contributions add along the line instead of cancelling. A uniform positive line would give a field pointing straight away from the line instead.
