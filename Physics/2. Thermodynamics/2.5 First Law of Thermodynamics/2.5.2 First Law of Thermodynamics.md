>[!note] First Law of Thermodynamics
>The **first law of thermodynamics** is energy conservation written for a system that can exchange heat and do work. The change in the [[2.4.1 Internal Energy|internal energy]] of a system equals the heat that flows into it minus the work it does on its surroundings,
>$$\Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q - W.$$
>Here $Q$ is the [[2.4.2 Heat|heat]] added to the system, positive when heat flows in and negative when it flows out. $W$ is the [[2.5.1 Thermodynamic Work|work done by the system]] on the outside world, positive when the system pushes its surroundings back and negative when the surroundings compress it. Every $\Delta$ is final minus initial. For a small step of a process the law reads
>$$dE_{\text{int}} = dQ - dW.$$
>For a gas the small work is $dW = P\,dV$, so the law becomes
>$$dE_{\text{int}} = dQ - P\,dV, \qquad dQ = dE_{\text{int}} + P\,dV.$$
>This is the form used to work out a path. For an ideal gas $dE_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2} Nk\, dT$ and $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2} Nk\, \Delta T = \tfrac{d}{2}\,(P_2 V_2 - P_1 V_1)$, with $d$ the number of accessible [[2.3.4 Equipartition of Energy|degrees of freedom]].
>
>The three terms are different kinds of quantity. $E_{\text{int}}$ is a state function, fixed by the present state of the system. $Q$ and $W$ are process quantities, and each depends on the path taken between two states. Their difference does not. For a closed cycle the system returns to its starting state, so $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$ and $Q = W$ over the cycle.
>
>The sign of $W$ is a convention, and books differ. Physics texts count the work done *by* the system, because engines are built to deliver work, and this course follows that convention. Chemistry texts count the work done *on* the system and write $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q + W_{\text{on}}$. The two forms agree, since $W_{\text{on}} = -W$. Check which convention a source uses before copying a formula from it.

# Intuition

A gas in a cylinder has a store of internal energy. There are exactly two ways to change it. Heat can flow through the walls, and the piston can move. If the piston moves outward the gas pushes on it and spends energy, and if the piston moves inward the surroundings do work on the gas and its energy rises. Add up the two contributions and the total must match the change in the store. The first law says exactly this. It is [[1.3c Work-Energy Principle|energy conservation]] with heat added as a second way to move energy around. The sketch shows the two transfers and their signs.

The law fixes one unknown. A process on a $P$-$V$ diagram tells you the path, and the path gives $W$ as the area under it. The endpoints give $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$ for an ideal gas. There is no formula for $Q$ on its own. The recipe for every gas problem is therefore the same: find the work from the path, find the change in internal energy from the endpoints, and get the heat from the first law. The [[2.5.3 Thermodynamic Processes|standard processes]] are the cases where the first two steps have closed forms.

# Properties

#### *(Two Ways In)* Heat and work are two ways of moving energy into a system, and the final state does not record which one was used.

Raising a gas from one state to another can be done by heating it, by compressing it, or by any mixture of the two. The final state is the same, and so is $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$. Only the split between $Q$ and $W$ differs. A gas cannot be said to contain heat or to contain work. It contains internal energy, and $Q$ and $W$ are the two kinds of transfer that change it.

#### *(Path Dependence)* $Q$ and $W$ each depend on the path, but their difference depends only on the endpoints.

Three paths between the same two states on a $P$-$V$ diagram enclose three different areas, so $W$ differs. The first law then says $Q$ differs by the same amount, because $Q - W$ is fixed. Work done by gas pressure is in this sense a non-conservative work in the [[1.3d.1 Potential Energy|7A sense]]: it depends on the route. The energy it draws on is still conserved. Path dependence is a statement about $W$, not about $E_{\text{int}}$.

#### *(Cycles Convert Heat to Work)* Around any closed cycle the net heat absorbed equals the net work done.

A system that runs in a cycle ends where it began, so its internal energy is unchanged over each loop. Whatever net heat it took in must have left as net work, $Q_{\text{cycle}} = W_{\text{cycle}}$. This is the principle behind every [[2.7.1 Heat Engine|heat engine]]. On a $P$-$V$ diagram the net work is the area enclosed by the loop, positive for a clockwise loop and negative for a counterclockwise one.

#### *(Expanding Solids Do Work)* A solid that expands against a load does work $F\,\Delta L$, so it needs the heat $Q = mc\,\Delta T + F\,\Delta L$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture applied the first law only to gases. The homework applies it to a heated rod that lifts a weight.

The first law holds for any system, not only a gas. A heated rod grows by $\Delta L = \alpha L_0\,\Delta T$, from [[2.1.4 Thermal Expansion]]. If it pushes on a load with force $F$, it does work $W = F\,\Delta L$. Its internal energy rises by $mc\,\Delta T$, from [[2.4.3 Specific Heat]]. So the heat it needs is
$$Q = mc\,\Delta T + F\,\alpha L_0\,\Delta T.$$
For a vertical rod holding a weight $Mg$, $F = Mg$. The rod also lifts its own center of mass by $\Delta L/2$, which adds $\tfrac{1}{2} m g\,\Delta L$. The work term is tiny for ordinary loads. It matters when a problem compares two rods that carry different loads.

#### *(Check the Convention)* A source that writes $\Delta E = Q + W$ counts work done on the system.

Both forms are correct. In this course and in most physics texts, $W$ is the work done by the system, so an expanding gas has $W > 0$ and its internal energy falls unless heat makes up the difference. A chemistry text with $\Delta E = Q + W$ means the opposite sign for $W$. Mixing the two conventions in one calculation gives an answer wrong by twice the work.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Bookkeeping Between Two States
>A gas absorbs $2500\ \text{J}$ of heat while doing $1800\ \text{J}$ of work on a piston. Later it is taken between the same two states by a different process in which $400\ \text{J}$ of work is done *on* the gas.
>
>>[!example]- Find the change in internal energy, and the heat exchanged in the second process.
>>In the first process $W = +1800\ \text{J}$, so
>>$$\Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q - W = 2500 - 1800 = 700\ \text{J}.$$
>>The second process has the same endpoints, so the same $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$. Work done on the gas means $W = -400\ \text{J}$, and
>>$$Q = \Delta E_{\text{int}} + W = 700 + (-400) = 300\ \text{J}.$$
>>The gas absorbs only $300\ \text{J}$ of heat the second time, because compression supplied the other $400\ \text{J}$.

>[!example] (Medium) Final Temperature of a Heated Gas
>Two moles of a monatomic ideal gas at $300\ \text{K}$ absorb $1500\ \text{J}$ of heat while expanding and doing $600\ \text{J}$ of work.
>
>>[!example]- Find the final temperature.
>>The first law gives $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 1500 - 600 = 900\ \text{J}$. For a monatomic gas $d = 3$, so $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{3}{2} nR\, \Delta T$ and
>>$$\Delta T = \frac{900\ \text{J}}{(1.5)(2.00\ \text{mol})(8.314\ \text{J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}))} = 36\ \text{K}.$$
>>The final temperature is $336\ \text{K}$. The path was not specified and was not needed. The temperature change follows from the endpoints alone once $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$ is known.

>[!example] (Hard) Closing a Cycle
>A gas is carried around a cycle $A \to B \to C \to A$. Along $AB$ it absorbs $800\ \text{J}$ of heat and does $300\ \text{J}$ of work. Along $BC$ it gives up $200\ \text{J}$ of heat at constant volume. Along $CA$, $350\ \text{J}$ of work is done on the gas.
>
>>[!example]- Find the heat exchanged along $CA$ and the net work over the cycle, and say which way the cycle runs.
>>Tabulate each leg with the first law. Along $AB$, $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 800 - 300 = 500\ \text{J}$. Along $BC$, no work is done at constant volume, so $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = -200\ \text{J}$. The cycle closes, so the three changes sum to zero and $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$ along $CA$ is $-300\ \text{J}$. Work done on the gas along $CA$ means $W_{CA} = -350\ \text{J}$, so
>>$$Q_{CA} = \Delta E_{\text{int}} + W_{CA} = -300 + (-350) = -650\ \text{J}.$$
>>The net work is $W = 300 + 0 - 350 = -50\ \text{J}$, and the net heat is $800 - 200 - 650 = -50\ \text{J}$, which agrees. The net work is negative, so the surroundings do $50\ \text{J}$ of net work on the gas each cycle and the gas rejects $50\ \text{J}$ of net heat. The cycle runs counterclockwise on the $P$-$V$ diagram. It is a [[2.7.5a Refrigerator|refrigerator]], not an engine.
