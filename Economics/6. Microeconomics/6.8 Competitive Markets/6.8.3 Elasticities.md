>[!note] Elasticities
>The **elasticity** of a variable $x > 0$ with respect to a parameter $p > 0$ is
>$$\varepsilon_{x,p} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial p}\,\frac{p}{x} = \lim_{dp \to 0}\frac{dx/x}{dp/p} = \frac{\partial \ln x}{\partial \ln p}.$$
>- It is the percent change in $x$ per one percent change in $p$, so $\%\Delta x \approx \varepsilon_{x,p}\,\%\Delta p$ for small changes. It does not depend on the units of $x$ or $p$. It is defined only for $x > 0$ and $p > 0$.
>- Elasticities keep their sign. The own-price elasticity of demand $\varepsilon_{D,p}$ is negative when demand slopes down, and the elasticity of supply $\varepsilon_{S,p}$ is positive.
>- Demand is **elastic** if $|\varepsilon_{D,p}| > 1$, **unit elastic** if $|\varepsilon_{D,p}| = 1$, and **inelastic** if $|\varepsilon_{D,p}| < 1$.
>- The same definition gives the cross-price elasticity $\varepsilon_{x,p_y}$ and the income elasticity $\varepsilon_{x,M}$. A good is [[6.3.3 Income Changes|normal]] if $\varepsilon_{x,M} > 0$ and inferior if $\varepsilon_{x,M} < 0$.
>- [[6.4a Cobb-Douglas]] demand $x^* = \alpha M/p_x$ has $\varepsilon_{x,p_x} = -1$, $\varepsilon_{x,p_y} = 0$, and $\varepsilon_{x,M} = 1$.
>- Constant-elasticity demand $x^* = kp_x^{\eta}$ with $\eta < 0$ has $\varepsilon_{x,p_x} = \eta$ at every price.
>- Linear demand $X = a - bp$ has $\varepsilon_{D,p} = -bp/(a - bp)$. It runs from $0$ at $p = 0$ to $-\infty$ at the choke price $a/b$, and equals $-1$ at the midpoint $p = a/(2b)$.
>- Elasticities also restate equilibrium comparative statics. With supply $Y^S(p, \alpha)$, demand $X^D(p, \alpha)$, and $X^{D*} = Y^{S*}$ at the equilibrium, the formula of [[6.8.2 Short-Run Market Equilibrium]] becomes a ratio of elasticities:
>$$\varepsilon_{p,\alpha} = -\frac{\varepsilon_{S,\alpha} - \varepsilon_{D,\alpha}}{\varepsilon_{S,p} - \varepsilon_{D,p}}, \qquad \varepsilon_{Y,\alpha} = \frac{\varepsilon_{S,p}\,\varepsilon_{D,\alpha} - \varepsilon_{D,p}\,\varepsilon_{S,\alpha}}{\varepsilon_{S,p} - \varepsilon_{D,p}}.$$
>- These forms need only elasticities, which empirical studies estimate. [[6.8.4 Tax Incidence]] is the case where $\alpha$ is a tax.

# Intuition

The size of $\partial p^*/\partial \alpha$ depends on units. Measure coffee in cups instead of liters and every slope changes, while the market stays the same. An elasticity compares percent changes, so the units cancel. A demand elasticity of $-2$ means that a $1\%$ price rise cuts the quantity bought by about $2\%$, in any units and any currency. This makes elasticities comparable across goods and countries, and it lets results estimated in one study feed into a formula in another.

Elasticity is a ratio of percent changes, so it changes along a straight demand line. The diagram shows $X = 100 - 50p$, the market demand of [[6.8.1 Aggregation]]. The slope is $-50$ everywhere. Near the top, the quantity is small, so a given change is a large percent of it. Demand is elastic there. Near the bottom, the price is small, so a given change in price is a large percent of the price, and demand is inelastic.

# Properties

#### *(Products Become Sums)* The elasticity of a product is the sum of the elasticities of its factors.

Since $\ln(xy) = \ln x + \ln y$, the log form gives $\varepsilon_{xy,p} = \varepsilon_{x,p} + \varepsilon_{y,p}$, and likewise $\varepsilon_{x^k,p} = k\,\varepsilon_{x,p}$. Spending on a good is $R = px$, so $\varepsilon_{R,p} = 1 + \varepsilon_{x,p}$. A price rise raises spending when demand is inelastic and lowers it when demand is elastic. On $X = 100 - 50p$, spending peaks at $p = 1$, the unit-elastic point. A firm with market power never prices on the inelastic part, which [[6.9.1 Monopoly]] uses. Cobb-Douglas demand keeps spending fixed at $\alpha M$, which is why $\varepsilon_{x,p_x} = -1$. In the figure, with $\alpha M = 180$, the rectangles $p \times x$ at $p = 6$ and at $p = 2$ have the same area.

#### *(Percent Changes Are Approximate)* The rule $\%\Delta x \approx \varepsilon\,\%\Delta p$ is a first-order approximation and drifts for large changes.

For constant-elasticity demand the exact rule is $x_1/x_0 = (p_1/p_0)^{\eta}$. With $\eta = -2$, a $10\%$ price rise gives $x_1/x_0 = 1.1^{-2} = 0.826$, a fall of $17.4\%$, while the approximation gives $20\%$. The gap grows with the size of the change. The same holds for the equilibrium formulas, which are derivatives at the current equilibrium.

#### *(Shift Over Responsiveness)* The equilibrium price moves by the net percent shift of the two curves, divided by their combined price responsiveness.

At the old price, a $1\%$ rise in $\alpha$ raises supply by $\varepsilon_{S,\alpha}\%$ and demand by $\varepsilon_{D,\alpha}\%$. The numerator $\varepsilon_{S,\alpha} - \varepsilon_{D,\alpha}$ is the percent excess supply this creates. A $1\%$ price rise adds $\varepsilon_{S,p} - \varepsilon_{D,p} > 0$ percent of excess supply, since both terms work in the same direction. The price moves until the two cancel. A shock that shifts both curves by the same percent, $\varepsilon_{S,\alpha} = \varepsilon_{D,\alpha}$, leaves the price unchanged and moves the quantity by that percent. The step $X^{D*} = Y^{S*}$ matters, since it lets both elasticities share the same $Y$ in their denominators.

# Derivation

We want $\varepsilon_{x,p} = \partial \ln x/\partial \ln p$ for $x = f(p)$. Write $\hat x = \ln x$ and $\hat p = \ln p$, so $p = e^{\hat p}$ and $\hat x = \ln f(e^{\hat p})$. By the chain rule,
$$\frac{\partial \hat x}{\partial \hat p} = \frac{1}{f(e^{\hat p})}\,\frac{\partial f(e^{\hat p})}{\partial e^{\hat p}}\,e^{\hat p} = \frac{1}{x}\,\frac{\partial x}{\partial p}\,p = \varepsilon_{x,p}.$$
For Cobb-Douglas, $\partial x^*/\partial p_x = -\alpha M/p_x^2$, so $\varepsilon_{x,p_x} = -\frac{\alpha M}{p_x^2}\cdot\frac{p_x}{\alpha M/p_x} = -1$. For $x^* = kp_x^{\eta}$, $\ln x^* = \ln k + \eta \ln p_x$, so $\varepsilon_{x,p_x} = \eta$.

For the equilibrium, multiply both sides of the formula for $\partial p^*/\partial \alpha$ by $\alpha/p$. Then multiply the numerator and the denominator of the right side by $1/Y$:
$$\frac{\partial p^*}{\partial \alpha}\,\frac{\alpha}{p} = -\frac{\left(\frac{\partial Y^S}{\partial \alpha} - \frac{\partial X^D}{\partial \alpha}\right)\frac{\alpha}{Y}}{\left(\frac{\partial Y^S}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial X^D}{\partial p}\right)\frac{p}{Y}}.$$
At the equilibrium $Y = Y^{S*} = X^{D*}$, so each term becomes an elasticity, which gives $\varepsilon_{p,\alpha}$. For the quantity, $\varepsilon_{Y,\alpha} = \varepsilon_{S,p}\,\varepsilon_{p,\alpha} + \varepsilon_{S,\alpha}$. Substituting $\varepsilon_{p,\alpha}$ and canceling the terms $\varepsilon_{S,p}\varepsilon_{S,\alpha}$ gives the callout formula.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Two Standard Demands
>The two demands are (a) the constant-elasticity demand $x^* = kp_x^{\eta}$ with $\eta < 0$, and (b) the Cobb-Douglas demand $x^* = 0.3M/p_x$ with $M = 600$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the own-price elasticity of each demand. For (b), also find the income elasticity and spending at $p_x = 2, 3, 6$.
>>(a) $\partial x^*/\partial p_x = k\eta p_x^{\eta - 1}$, so $\varepsilon_{x,p_x} = k\eta p_x^{\eta-1}\cdot p_x/(kp_x^{\eta}) = \eta$. The elasticity is the same at every price, which explains the name. (b) $\varepsilon_{x,p_x} = -1$ from the derivation. Since $x^*$ is proportional to $M$, $\varepsilon_{x,M} = (0.3/p_x)\cdot M/(0.3M/p_x) = 1$. Demand is $90$, $60$, and $30$ at the three prices, and spending is $180$ each time. Unit elasticity means spending does not react to price.

>[!example] (Medium) Elasticity Along a Linear Market
>Five firms and ten consumers give aggregate supply $AY = 25p$ and aggregate demand $AD = 100 - 50p$, with equilibrium $p^* = 4/3$. A store owner claims that a lower price always raises spending in this market.
>
>>[!example]- Find $\varepsilon_{S,p}$ and $\varepsilon_{D,p}$ at the equilibrium. Test the claim at $p = 4/3$ and at $p = 0.5$.
>>Supply is a line through the origin, so $\varepsilon_{S,p} = 25p/(25p) = 1$ at every price. At $p^* = 4/3$, $X = 100/3$ and $\varepsilon_{D,p} = -50 \cdot \frac{4/3}{100/3} = -2$. Demand is elastic, so a lower price raises spending. Spending is $44.44$ at $p = 4/3$ and $50$ at $p = 1$. At $p = 0.5$, $X = 75$ and $\varepsilon_{D,p} = -25/75 = -1/3$. Demand is inelastic there, so a lower price lowers spending. Spending is $37.5$ at $p = 0.5$, below the $50$ at $p = 1$. The claim fails below $p = 1$.

>[!example] (Hard) Fuel Prices and Train Tickets
>Suppose studies give these elasticities for intercity train tickets: $\varepsilon_{D,p} = -0.6$ and $\varepsilon_{S,p} = 0.9$. The price of jet fuel $\alpha$ affects both sides. Dearer flights push travelers to trains, $\varepsilon_{D,\alpha} = 0.2$. Trains burn fuel too, $\varepsilon_{S,\alpha} = -0.1$. Fuel prices rise $20\%$.
>
>>[!example]- Predict the percent changes in ticket price and quantity, and check them on both curves.
>>The formulas give
>>$$\varepsilon_{p,\alpha} = -\frac{-0.1 - 0.2}{0.9 + 0.6} = 0.2, \qquad \varepsilon_{Y,\alpha} = \frac{0.9 \cdot 0.2 - (-0.6)(-0.1)}{1.5} = \frac{0.18 - 0.06}{1.5} = 0.08.$$
>>A $20\%$ fuel rise raises the ticket price by about $4\%$ and the quantity by about $1.6\%$. Check supply: $-0.1 \cdot 20 + 0.9 \cdot 4 = -2 + 3.6 = 1.6\%$. Check demand: $0.2 \cdot 20 - 0.6 \cdot 4 = 4 - 2.4 = 1.6\%$. Both curves give the same quantity, as they must. Both shocks raise the price, while their quantity effects pull in opposite directions, and the demand effect is larger. If both curves have constant elasticities, the exact answers are $+3.7\%$ and $+1.5\%$.
