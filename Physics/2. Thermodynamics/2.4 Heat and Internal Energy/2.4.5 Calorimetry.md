>[!note] Calorimetry
>**Calorimetry** is the bookkeeping of heat in an isolated system. When several bodies at different temperatures are placed in thermal contact and shielded from everything else, no energy enters or leaves the group. Heat moves between the bodies until they share one final temperature $T_f$. Energy conservation requires that the heat flows sum to zero,
>$$\sum_i Q_i = 0,$$
>where $Q_i$ is the heat absorbed by body $i$, positive if the body gains energy and negative if it loses energy. For a body that stays in one phase,
>$$Q_i = m_i\, c_i\, (T_f - T_{i}),$$
>with $m_i$ its mass, $c_i$ its [[2.4.3 Specific Heat|specific heat]], and $T_i$ its starting temperature. Written this way, every term has the same form, and the sign takes care of itself. A body that ends warmer than it started has $Q_i > 0$, and a body that ends cooler has $Q_i < 0$. A body that melts or boils adds a term $\pm m L$ with the [[2.4.4 Latent Heat|latent heat]] $L$, positive for melting or vaporizing and negative for freezing or condensing.
>
>A **calorimeter** is an insulated container built for such measurements. Its own mass and specific heat enter the sum like any other body. A calorimeter is used in two directions. Given all the specific heats, it predicts $T_f$. Given $T_f$ from a thermometer, it measures an unknown specific heat or latent heat, which is how the values in tables were found.
>
>The method applies to solids and liquids, where $Q = mc\,\Delta T$ holds. A gas absorbs heat in a way that depends on the process, so a gas in a calorimetry problem needs the [[2.5.2 First Law of Thermodynamics|first law]] instead.

# Intuition

Drop a hot horseshoe into a bucket of water. Energy leaves the iron and enters the water until both sit at the same temperature. Nothing is created and nothing escapes, so whatever the iron loses, the water gains. The method rests on that sentence. The only skill is writing it down without a sign error. The rule for every difference is final minus initial.

Textbooks often write "heat lost equals heat gained," with the loss counted as a positive number. That form works, but it forces a guess about which body loses and a minus sign to match. The safer habit is to write every $Q_i$ as $m_i c_i (T_f - T_i)$, final minus initial, and set the sum to zero. The algebra then produces the signs. A body that turns out to lose heat gets a negative $Q_i$ on its own. A wrong guess about the direction is impossible because no guess is made. # Properties

#### *(Weighted Average)* Without phase changes, the final temperature is an average of the starting temperatures weighted by heat capacity.

Solving $\sum m_i c_i (T_f - T_i) = 0$ for $T_f$ gives
$$T_f = \frac{\sum_i m_i c_i T_i}{\sum_i m_i c_i},$$
a weighted average with the heat capacities $m_i c_i$ as weights. The result always lies between the lowest and highest starting temperatures. When every body is the same substance, the $c$ cancels and the weights are the masses alone. For two volumes of the same liquid, the densities cancel too and $T_f = (V_1 T_1 + V_2 T_2)/(V_1 + V_2)$. Water has a density of $1.00\ \text{g/cm}^3$, so $300\ \text{cm}^3$ of water has a mass of $300\ \text{g}$. A computed $T_f$ outside the starting range signals an arithmetic error or a phase change that the equation left out.

#### *(Three Outcomes with Ice)* When ice meets warm water, the final state can be all water, all ice, or a mixture at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

Which outcome occurs depends on the numbers, so the first step is a comparison. Compute the heat needed to bring the ice to $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and melt all of it, $m_{\text{ice}} c_{\text{ice}} (0 - T_{\text{ice}}) + m_{\text{ice}} L_F$. Compute the heat the water can release by cooling to $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$, $m_w c_w T_w$. If the water can supply more than the ice needs, all the ice melts and $T_f > 0$. If the water cannot even bring the ice to $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$, the water freezes and $T_f < 0$. Otherwise the two meet at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ with some ice left, and the unknown is the mass melted rather than a temperature. Let $Q_{\text{ice}}$ be the heat that warms the ice to $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Let $Q_w$ be the heat the water gives while cooling to $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The balance $Q_{\text{ice}} + Q_w + m_{\text{melt}} L_F = 0$ gives the melted mass,
$$m_{\text{melt}} = -\frac{Q_{\text{ice}} + Q_w}{L_F},$$
where $Q_w$ is negative. A negative $m_{\text{melt}}$ means some water freezes instead. A tray of frozen peas covered with tap water shows the last case: a shell of ice forms on the peas.

#### *(Gases Mix by Internal Energy)* Ideal gases mixed in a rigid, insulated container keep their total internal energy, so $T_f = \sum d_i N_i T_i / \sum d_i N_i$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not mix gases. The homework uses this balance.

A gas needs the first law, not $mc\,\Delta T$. A rigid container does no work, and insulation lets no heat in. So $Q = W = 0$ for the whole contents, and the total internal energy stays fixed. Each gas has $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d_i}{2} N_i k T_i$, from [[2.4.1 Internal Energy]]. Equating the totals before and after gives
$$\sum_i \tfrac{d_i}{2} N_i k\,T_i = \left(\sum_i \tfrac{d_i}{2} N_i\right) k\,T_f, \qquad T_f = \frac{\sum_i d_i N_i T_i}{\sum_i d_i N_i}.$$
This is the weighted average above, with $\tfrac{d_i}{2} N_i k$ in place of $m_i c_i$. For gases with the same $d$, the weights are the molecule counts alone. It also holds when a partition between two insulated chambers is removed, since the whole system still does no work. The final pressure then follows from $PV = NkT$ with the total $N$ and total $V$. If the molecules react, the final $N$ and $d$ change. The final internal energy then also gains any chemical energy the reaction releases.

#### *(Count Every Body)* The container, the stirrer, and the thermometer absorb heat too.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not discuss the heat absorbed by the container or the thermometer. This property is standard textbook material.

A cup of aluminum weighing $100\ \text{g}$ has a heat capacity of $90\ \text{J/K}$, about two percent of that of $1\ \text{kg}$ of water. Leaving it out shifts a measured specific heat by that fraction. A careful measurement includes each part with its own $m_i c_i$. The alternative is to measure the calorimeter's total heat capacity once, by mixing known amounts of water, and treat it as one extra term.

#### *(Losses to the Room)* Real calorimeters leak, and the fix is to measure fast and correct for the leak.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not discuss heat leaking out of a calorimeter. This property is standard textbook material.

Insulation slows the flow of heat to the surroundings but does not stop it. Over a long experiment the mixture drifts toward room temperature, and the measured $T_f$ is wrong. Standard practice is to record the temperature against time, find the value it reaches right after mixing, and extrapolate the slow drift back to that instant. The rate of leakage follows the laws of [[2.6 Heat Transfer|heat transfer]].

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Mixing Two Volumes of Water
>A cup of $0.200\ \text{L}$ of water at $80\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is poured into $0.300\ \text{L}$ of water at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ in an insulated container.
>
>>[!example]- Find the final temperature.
>>With $m = \rho V$ for both portions, the balance is
>>$$\rho V_1 c_w (T_f - T_1) + \rho V_2 c_w (T_f - T_2) = 0.$$
>>The density and specific heat cancel, leaving
>>$$T_f = \frac{V_1 T_1 + V_2 T_2}{V_1 + V_2} = \frac{(0.200)(80) + (0.300)(20)}{0.500}\ ^\circ\text{C} = 44\ ^\circ\text{C}.$$
>>The answer is a volume-weighted average, and it lies between $20$ and $80\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The first term is $\rho V_1 c_w (44 - 80) < 0$, so the hot water lost heat, without any guess being made in advance.

>[!example] (Medium) Measuring a Specific Heat
>A $0.150\ \text{kg}$ sample of an unknown metal is heated to $100.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and dropped into $0.400\ \text{kg}$ of water at $20.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The water sits in an aluminum cup of mass $0.100\ \text{kg}$ ($c = 900\ \text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K})$). The final temperature is $23.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the specific heat of the metal and identify it.
>>Three bodies exchange heat, so
>>$$m_m c_m (T_f - 100.0) + m_w c_w (T_f - 20.0) + m_{\text{cup}} c_{\text{Al}} (T_f - 20.0) = 0.$$
>>The water and cup terms are $(0.400)(4186)(3.0) + (0.100)(900)(3.0) = 5023 + 270 = 5293\ \text{J}$. The metal term is $(0.150)\, c_m\, (-77.0) = -11.55\, c_m$. Setting the sum to zero,
>>$$c_m = \frac{5293\ \text{J}}{11.55\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{K}} = 458\ \text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}).$$
>>The value matches iron or steel. Omitting the cup would give $435\ \text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K})$, a $5$ percent error from a part that holds only $2$ percent of the heat capacity. The metal's small mass makes its $c$ sensitive to every joule.

>A $0.200\ \text{kg}$ block of ice at $-20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is dropped into $0.300\ \text{kg}$ of water at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ in an insulated container. Use $c_{\text{ice}} = 2100\ \text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K})$ and $L_F = 333\ \text{kJ/kg}$.

>>[!example]- Find the final temperature and the mass of ice that melts.
>>First decide which of the three outcomes applies. Warming the ice to $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ takes $(0.200)(2100)(20) = 8.4\ \text{kJ}$. Melting all of it would take a further $(0.200)(333) = 66.6\ \text{kJ}$. Cooling the water to $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ releases $(0.300)(4186)(20) = 25.1\ \text{kJ}$. The water can warm the ice to $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ with $25.1 - 8.4 = 16.7\ \text{kJ}$ to spare. That is far less than the $66.6\ \text{kJ}$ needed to melt all of it. The system ends at $T_f = 0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ with ice and water together.
>>
>>With $T_f$ known, the unknown is the melted mass $m_{\text{melt}}$. The balance has three terms, the ice warming, the ice melting, and the water cooling,
>>$$(0.200)(2100)(0 - (-20)) + m_{\text{melt}}(333 \times 10^{3}) + (0.300)(4186)(0 - 20) = 0.$$
>>Solving gives $m_{\text{melt}} = 16.7 \times 10^{3} / 333 \times 10^{3} = 0.050\ \text{kg}$. Only $50\ \text{g}$ melts, and $150\ \text{g}$ of ice floats in $350\ \text{g}$ of water at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The melted mass is small because the latent heat is large. Melting one kilogram of ice takes as much heat as cooling eight kilograms of water by ten degrees.
