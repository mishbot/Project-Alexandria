>[!note] Gas Laws
>The **gas laws** are three experimental rules that relate the [[1.7.1. Fluids|pressure]] $P$, volume $V$, and temperature $T$ of a fixed amount of gas. Each holds one variable fixed and relates the other two by a proportionality. At fixed temperature, $PV = \text{const}$, so halving the volume doubles the pressure (**Boyle's law**). At fixed pressure, $V/T = \text{const}$ (**Charles's law**). At fixed volume, $P/T = \text{const}$ (**Gay-Lussac's law**). In every case $P$ is the absolute pressure and $T$ is the [[2.2.1 Absolute Temperature|absolute temperature]] in kelvins. They hold for any gas at low density, which means far from the conditions at which it liquefies. Taken together, for any two states of the same sample,
>$$\frac{P_1 V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2 V_2}{T_2},$$
>which is the **combined gas law**. The three laws were found by experiment between 1662 and 1802, and each was a separate discovery. The next note combines them into one equation, the ideal gas law $PV = NkT$, and adds the dependence on the amount of gas. Read in reverse, $PV = NkT$ with one variable held fixed gives back each law. The three cases are three of the four standard [[2.5.3 Thermodynamic Processes|thermodynamic processes]]. The names are kept here because textbooks and problem sets use them.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not name Boyle's, Charles's, or Gay-Lussac's laws, and it did not state the combined gas law. It read the three fixed-variable cases off $PV = NkT$. The names and their history follow Giancoli section 17.6.

# Intuition

A gas is easy to describe because its molecules are far apart. According to the [[2.1.1 Atomic Theory of Matter|atomic theory]], a gas molecule spends almost all of its time in free flight, and the identity of the molecules matters little. Pressure is the force of the molecules striking the container walls, spread over the wall area. Squeezing the gas into half the volume at fixed temperature doubles the number of molecules near each part of the wall, so the wall receives twice as many hits per second and the pressure doubles. Heating the gas makes the molecules move faster. If a movable piston holds the pressure fixed, the faster molecules push it outward until the larger volume restores the old rate of hits. If instead the volume is fixed, the faster molecules hit the walls harder and more often, and the pressure rises. The [[2.3 Kinetic Theory of Gases|kinetic theory of gases]] turns this picture into a derivation. The first sketch shows how each variable is held fixed in practice. The second shows the graph of each law with the fixed variable named under it.

# Properties

#### *(Absolute Quantities Only)* The laws fail when written with gauge pressure or Celsius temperature.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture covered the Kelvin half of this property, not gauge pressure. The gauge half follows Giancoli section 13.4.

A tire gauge reads the **gauge pressure**, the amount by which the pressure inside exceeds atmospheric pressure. The molecules do not know about the atmosphere outside, so the laws need the absolute pressure, $P = P_{\text{gauge}} + P_{\text{atm}}$, with $P_{\text{atm}} = 1.013 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$ at sea level. Temperature must be measured from absolute zero for the same reason. A gas at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ warmed to $40\ ^\circ\text{C}$ does not double its pressure. Its absolute temperature goes from $293\ \text{K}$ to $313\ \text{K}$, a rise of $7$ percent, and its pressure rises by the same $7$ percent.

#### *(Each Law Is a Path)* Holding one variable fixed picks out a curve on the $P$-$V$ diagram, and moving along that curve is a named process.

Boyle's law is a hyperbola, the isotherm, and moving along it is an [[2.5.3c Isothermal Process|isothermal process]]. Charles's law is a horizontal line, the [[2.5.3b Isobaric Process|isobaric process]], and Gay-Lussac's law is a vertical line, the [[2.5.3a Isovolumetric Process|isovolumetric process]]. The gas laws say where the gas ends up. The process notes add how much work it did and how much heat it took in on the way.

#### *(Low Density Only)* The laws break down as a gas approaches liquefaction.

At high pressure the molecules are close enough to attract one another, and their own volume becomes a noticeable fraction of the container. Near the condensation temperature Charles's law fails outright, because the volume drops sharply when the gas becomes a liquid. Air, nitrogen, helium, and other common gases follow the laws to within a fraction of a percent at room temperature and atmospheric pressure.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Rising Bubble
>A diver at a depth of $20.0\ \text{m}$ in a lake releases an air bubble of volume $1.00\ \text{cm}^3$. The lake water has the same temperature at every depth, and the air pressure at the surface is $1.013 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$.
>
>```tikz
>\usepackage{tikz}
>\begin{document}
>\definecolor{cold}{HTML}{2F80ED}
>\definecolor{mid}{HTML}{8A8A8A}
>\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1cm,y=1cm,>=latex]
>\draw[cold,very thick] (-0.6,3.0) -- (3.9,3.0);
>\node[above right] at (-0.6,3.0) {surface, $P_0$};
>\begin{scope}
>\clip (-0.6,-0.3) rectangle (3.9,0);
>\foreach \i in {-0.9,-0.6,...,3.9} \draw[mid] (\i,-0.3) -- ++(0.3,0.3);
>\end{scope}
>\draw[thick] (-0.6,0) -- (3.9,0);
>\draw[thick] (1.2,0.5) circle (0.14);
>\draw[thick] (1.2,2.65) circle (0.2);
>\draw[->,thick,dashed] (1.2,0.75) -- (1.2,2.35);
>\node[left] at (1.0,0.5) {$V_1 = 1.00\ \mathrm{cm}^3$};
>\node[left] at (0.95,2.65) {$V_2$};
>\draw[<->] (3.1,3.0) -- (3.1,0.5) node[midway,right] {$20.0\ \mathrm{m}$};
>\draw[mid,dashed] (1.4,0.5) -- (3.1,0.5);
>\node[right] at (1.4,1.6) {$T$ fixed};
>\end{tikzpicture}
>\end{document}
>```
>
>>[!example]- What is the volume of the bubble when it reaches the surface?
>>The pressure at depth follows from [[1.7.2. Hydrostatic Equilibrium|hydrostatic equilibrium]],
>>$$P_1 = P_0 + \rho g h = 1.013 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa} + (1000\ \text{kg/m}^3)(9.80\ \text{m/s}^2)(20.0\ \text{m}) = 2.97 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}.$$
>>The temperature of the bubble stays fixed, so $PV$ is constant,
>>$$V_2 = V_1 \frac{P_1}{P_2} = (1.00\ \text{cm}^3)\,\frac{2.97 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}}{1.013 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}} = 2.93\ \text{cm}^3.$$
>>The bubble nearly triples in volume, because the pressure at $20\ \text{m}$ is nearly three atmospheres.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not use gauge pressure. This example follows Giancoli section 17.8.

>[!example] (Medium) Tire on a Hot Road
>A car tire is inflated to a gauge pressure of $220\ \text{kPa}$ at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$. After an hour of driving, the air in the tire reaches $50\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The tire volume does not change, and atmospheric pressure is $101\ \text{kPa}$.
>
>>[!example]- What does the gauge read after the drive?
>>The law needs the absolute pressure, $P_1 = 220 + 101 = 321\ \text{kPa}$. The volume is fixed, so
>>$$P_2 = P_1 \frac{T_2}{T_1} = (321\ \text{kPa})\,\frac{323.15\ \text{K}}{293.15\ \text{K}} = 354\ \text{kPa}.$$
>>The gauge reads the excess over atmospheric pressure, $354 - 101 = 253\ \text{kPa}$. Applying the law to the gauge reading itself would give $220 \times 323.15 / 293.15 = 243\ \text{kPa}$, which is $10\ \text{kPa}$ too low.
