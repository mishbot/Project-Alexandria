>[!note] Cost Minimization
>- **Cost minimization** is step one of the two-step approach to the firm. For a given output $y$, the firm finds the cheapest inputs. Step two picks $y$ ([[6.7.5 Profit Maximization and Supply]]). With wage $w$ for labor and rental rate $r$ for capital, the problem is
>$$\min_{L, K}\ wL + rK \quad \text{s.t.} \quad f(L, K) \ge y.$$
>- The constraint binds if $w > 0$, $r > 0$, and $f$ is continuous and strictly increasing in at least one input.
>- With $\mathcal{L} = wL + rK - \lambda\big(f(L, K) - y\big)$, the first-order conditions are $w - \lambda f'_L = 0$, $r - \lambda f'_K = 0$, and $f(L, K) = y$. Dividing the first two,
>$$\frac{f'_L(L^*, K^*)}{f'_K(L^*, K^*)} = \frac{w}{r}, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \text{MRTS} = -\frac{w}{r}.$$
>- Geometrically, the isoquant $Q(y)$ is tangent to the lowest **isocost line** $wL + rK = C$ that reaches it, which has slope $-w/r$. Convex isoquants make the tangency a minimum.
>- The solutions are the **conditional input demands** (derived demands) $L^*(w, r, y)$ and $K^*(w, r, y)$. The value function is the **cost function** $c(w, r, y) = wL^*(w, r, y) + rK^*(w, r, y)$.
>- For Cobb-Douglas $f(L, K) = AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta}$,
>$$L^* = \left(\frac{y}{A}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}}\left(\frac{w}{r}\,\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}, \qquad K^* = \left(\frac{y}{A}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}}\left(\frac{w}{r}\,\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}, \qquad c = B\left(\frac{y}{A}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}},$$
>$$B = w\left(\frac{w}{r}\,\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}} + r\left(\frac{w}{r}\,\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}} = (\alpha+\beta)\left(\frac{w}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}\left(\frac{r}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}.$$
>- Cobb-Douglas comparative statics: $\partial L^*/\partial A < 0$, $\partial L^*/\partial y > 0$, $\partial L^*/\partial w < 0$, $\partial L^*/\partial r > 0$, and the mirror results for $K^*$. Spending splits as $rK^* : wL^* = \alpha : \beta$.
>- The problem is [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]] with $(w, r)$ for prices, $f$ for $u$, $y$ for $\bar u$, $(L^*, K^*)$ for Hicksian demands, and $c$ for $e$.
>- By the [[7.3 Envelope Theorem|envelope theorem]] (not taught), $\lambda = \partial c/\partial y$ is marginal cost, and $\partial c/\partial w = L^*$, $\partial c/\partial r = K^*$ (Shephard's lemma).

>[!warning] Lecture Wording: Slope, Argument Order, and Continuity
>The slides say "MRTS (slope of isoquant) equals ratio of input prices." The slope of the isoquant is negative, so the precise statement is $\text{MRTS} = -w/r$, or $f'_L/f'_K = w/r$. The slides also write the demands both as $L^*(w, r, y)$ and as $L^*(r, w, y)$. This note uses $(w, r, y)$ throughout. For a binding constraint, the slides list only $w, r > 0$ and $f$ strictly increasing. This note adds continuity of $f$, since a jump in $f$ can force output above $y$.

# Intuition

A car wash has a contract to wash $y$ cars an hour. It can hire workers or rent washing machines, and the contract fixes output. The only question left is the cheapest mix. Each **isocost line** $wL + rK = C$ collects the bundles that cost $C$. The lines are parallel, with slope $-w/r$, and lower lines are cheaper. The animation slides the line inward. At $C = 800$ and $C = 720$ it crosses the isoquant twice, so cheaper bundles exist between the crossings. At $C = 640$ it touches the isoquant once, at the tangency. At $C = 560$ it misses, and $y$ is out of reach. The numbers are those of the Easy example.

The tangency condition says that the market rate and the technical rate agree. The market lets the firm trade one worker for $w/r$ machine rentals at equal cost. The technology lets one worker replace $|\text{MRTS}|$ machines at equal output. If one worker replaced more machines than her wage could rent, the firm would hire her and return machines until the two rates matched.

# Properties

#### *(Equal Output per Dollar)* At an interior optimum, the last dollar spent on each input adds the same output, $f'_L/w = f'_K/r = 1/\lambda$.

Rearranging the first-order conditions gives this form. Suppose instead that $f'_L/w > f'_K/r$. Moving one dollar from capital to labor raises output by $f'_L/w - f'_K/r > 0$ at the same cost. The firm could then cut spending and still make $y$, so the bundle was not cheapest. The common ratio $1/\lambda$ is the output bought by the marginal dollar. Its inverse $\lambda$ is the cost of one more unit of output, which the envelope property below identifies as marginal cost.

#### *(When the Constraint Binds)* A cost-minimizing firm makes exactly $y$ when both input prices are positive and output rises strictly in at least one input.

Take a bundle that makes more than $y$. If $f$ is continuous and strictly increasing in $L$, a small cut in $L$ still leaves output at least $y$. With $w > 0$ the cut saves money, so the bundle was not cheapest. Both conditions matter. If $w = 0$ and $f = L + K$, labor is free, and hiring $2y$ workers to make $2y$ units costs nothing, so an optimum with slack exists. If output comes in whole batches, as in $f(L, K) = \lfloor L \rfloor$, an order of $y = 2.5$ needs $L = 3$ and the firm makes $3$ units.

#### *(Substitution Away From Expensive Inputs)* A higher wage makes isocost lines steeper and moves the tangency up the isoquant, toward less labor and more capital.

The figure uses the Medium example. The wage rises from $16$ to $25$, and the tangency moves from $A = (20, 80)$ to $A' = (16, 100)$ on the same isoquant. For Cobb-Douglas the effects have constant elasticities, read off $\ln L^*$:
$$\frac{\partial \ln L^*}{\partial \ln w} = -\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}, \qquad \frac{\partial \ln L^*}{\partial \ln r} = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}, \qquad \frac{\partial \ln L^*}{\partial \ln y} = \frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}, \qquad \frac{\partial \ln L^*}{\partial \ln A} = -\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}.$$
The demands depend on $w$ and $r$ only through $w/r$, so doubling both prices doubles cost and leaves the inputs unchanged.

#### *(Output Held Fixed)* The result $\partial L^*/\partial A < 0$ compares technologies at the same output, and a better technology can still raise employment.

A higher $A$ makes every bundle produce more, so fewer inputs reach the same $y$. The elasticity is $-1/(\alpha+\beta)$ for both inputs. Output, however, is a choice in step two, and a lower cost per unit makes a larger output profitable.

>[!warning] Lecture Wording: Technological Progress and Unemployment
>The slides label $\partial L^*/\partial A < 0$ as "technological progress and unemployment." This holds only while $y$ is fixed. A more productive firm has lower marginal cost and, in step two, usually produces more. The Hard example shows employment rising after an improvement in technology.

#### *(Twin of Expenditure Minimization)* The firm's problem and the consumer's expenditure problem have the same mathematical form.

| Expenditure minimization | Cost minimization |
|---|---|
| prices $p_1, p_2$ | input prices $w, r$ |
| utility $u(x_1, x_2) \ge \bar u$ | output $f(L, K) \ge y$ |
| Hicksian demands $h_i(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$ | conditional demands $L^*(w, r, y)$, $K^*(w, r, y)$ |
| expenditure function $e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$ | cost function $c(w, r, y)$ |
| $\text{MRS} = -p_1/p_2$ | $\text{MRTS} = -w/r$ |

Every result about Hicksian demand has a version for conditional input demand. Two differences matter. The target $y$ is observable and cardinal, while $\bar u$ is neither. The firm also picks its own target in step two, whereas the consumer's target comes from outside the problem.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture did not interpret the multiplier, did not state Shephard's lemma for firms, and showed $\partial L^*/\partial w < 0$ only for Cobb-Douglas. All three carry over from the consumer in [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]].

#### *(Envelope Results)* The multiplier is marginal cost, and the derivative of cost with respect to an input price is the demand for that input.

By the [[7.3 Envelope Theorem|envelope theorem]], $\partial c/\partial y = \partial \mathcal{L}/\partial y = \lambda$ and $\partial c/\partial w = \partial \mathcal{L}/\partial w = L^*$. For Cobb-Douglas, $c \propto w^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}$, so $\partial c/\partial w = \frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\,c/w = L^*$, since labor's share of spending is $\beta/(\alpha+\beta)$. Likewise $c \propto y^{1/(\alpha+\beta)}$ gives $\partial c/\partial y = c/((\alpha+\beta)y)$, which equals $\lambda = wL^*/(\beta y)$. A small wage rise $dw$ raises cost by about $L^*\,dw$, because re-optimizing the mix saves nothing to first order. The own-price result also carries over. Hicksian demand never rises with its own price, and the proof in [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]] applies word for word, so $L^*$ never rises with $w$ for any technology.

# Derivation

We want $L^*$, $K^*$, and $c$ for $f = AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta}$, following [[7.2 Lagrange Multipliers]]. Using $f'_L = \beta y/L$ and $f'_K = \alpha y/K$ on the isoquant, the first-order conditions are $w = \lambda\beta y/L$ and $r = \lambda\alpha y/K$. Dividing gives $w/r = \beta K/(\alpha L)$, so $K = \frac{w}{r}\frac{\alpha}{\beta}L$. Substituting into $AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta} = y$,
$$A\left(\frac{w}{r}\,\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\alpha}L^{\alpha+\beta} = y \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad L^* = \left(\frac{y}{A}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}}\left(\frac{w}{r}\,\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}.$$
Multiplying by $\frac{w}{r}\frac{\alpha}{\beta}$ raises the exponent by one, from $-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}$ to $\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}$, which gives $K^*$. Then $c = wL^* + rK^*$ factors as $(y/A)^{1/(\alpha+\beta)}B$. Writing $R = \frac{w}{r}\frac{\alpha}{\beta}$, we get $B = R^{-\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}(w + rR) = R^{-\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}\,w\,\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\beta}$, which rearranges to the second form of $B$ in the callout. The same step shows $wL^* = \frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\,c$. The isoquants of Cobb-Douglas are convex ([[6.7.1 Production Functions]]), so the tangency is the cheapest bundle. The signs of the comparative statics follow from the exponents of $\ln L^*$ listed above.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) The Cheapest Car Wash
>A car wash has $f(L, K) = K^{1/2}L^{1/2}$ cars per hour. Workers cost $w = 16$ and machines rent for $r = 4$, both per hour. The contract requires $y = 40$.
>
>>[!example]- Find $L^*$, $K^*$, and the minimum cost. Compare with the bundle $L = K = 40$.
>>The tangency gives $K/L = w/r = 4$, so $K = 4L$. Then $\sqrt{4L^2} = 2L = 40$, so $L^* = 20$ and $K^* = 80$. Cost is $16 \cdot 20 + 4 \cdot 80 = 640$. The formula agrees. With $\alpha = \beta = \frac12$ and $A = 1$, $B = 16 \cdot 4^{-1/2} + 4 \cdot 4^{1/2} = 16$, so $c = 16 \cdot 40 = 640$. Spending splits evenly, $320$ on each input, as $\alpha : \beta = 1 : 1$ requires. The bundle $(40, 40)$ also washes $40$ cars but costs $16 \cdot 40 + 4 \cdot 40 = 800$. Its $\text{MRTS} = -1$ differs from $-w/r = -4$. Labor is four times as expensive as capital, yet the firm uses equal amounts.

>[!example] (Medium) A Minimum-Wage Rise
>The wage at the car wash rises from $16$ to $25$. The contract still requires $40$ cars per hour.
>
>>[!example]- Find the new bundle and cost. Estimate the cost increase with Shephard's lemma and explain the gap.
>>The tangency now needs $K/L = 25/4$, so $\sqrt{6.25}\,L = 2.5L = 40$. That gives $L^* = 16$ and $K^* = 100$, with cost $25 \cdot 16 + 4 \cdot 100 = 800$. Labor falls by $20\%$, matching the elasticity $-\frac12$: $\ln(16/20) = -0.223 = -\frac12\ln(25/16)$. Shephard's lemma estimates the increase as $L^*\,\Delta w = 20 \cdot 9 = 180$. The old bundle would cost $820$ at the new wage. Switching toward machines saves the remaining $20$, so the true increase is $160$.

>[!example] (Hard) Technology and Jobs
>A firm has $f(L, K) = AK^{1/4}L^{1/4}$, input prices $w = r = 1$, and output price $p = 4$. Technology improves from $A = 1$ to $A = 2$.
>
>>[!example]- Find employment at the old output, then at the output the firm chooses after the improvement.
>>With $\alpha = \beta = \frac14$, the ratio $\frac{w}{r}\frac{\alpha}{\beta} = 1$, so $L^* = K^* = (y/A)^2$ and $B = 2$. The cost function is $c = 2y^2/A^2$. At $A = 1$, the firm maximizes $4y - 2y^2$, so $y^* = 1$ and $L^* = 1$.
>>
>>If output stays at $y = 1$, the better technology needs $L^* = 1/4$. Employment falls by three quarters, which is the source of the fear that technology destroys jobs. But the firm re-optimizes. It maximizes $4y - y^2/2$, which gives $y^* = 4$ and $L^* = (4/2)^2 = 4$. Profit rises from $2$ to $8$, and employment quadruples. The decreasing returns ($\alpha + \beta = \frac12$) keep the expansion finite. The full effect of technology on jobs needs both steps.
