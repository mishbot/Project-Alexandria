>[!note] Indifference Curves and MRS
>Let $u(x_1, x_2)$ be a [[6.1.3 Utility Functions|utility function]] on $X = \mathbb{R}^2_+$. The **indifference curve** at level $\bar u$ is the set of bundles that give utility $\bar u$,
>$$\{(x_1, x_2) \in X : u(x_1, x_2) = \bar u\}.$$
>The consumer is indifferent between any two bundles on the curve. The collection of curves for all levels $\bar u$ is the **indifference map**. The equation $u(x_1, x_2) - \bar u = 0$ defines $x_2$ implicitly as a function of $x_1$ near any bundle where $u'_{x_2} \neq 0$. By the [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]], the slope of the curve is the **marginal rate of substitution**,
>$$\text{MRS} = \left.\frac{dx_2}{dx_1}\right|_{u = \bar u} = -\frac{u'_{x_1}}{u'_{x_2}}.$$
>Here $u'_{x_1}$ and $u'_{x_2}$ are the **marginal utilities**, the [[2.3. Partial Derivatives|partial derivatives]] of $u$ at the bundle. For small changes, $|\text{MRS}|$ is the amount of good 2 the consumer gives up per extra unit of good 1 while staying indifferent.
>
>The shape of the curves depends on the [[6.1.2 Monotone and Convex Preferences|assumptions on preferences]]. Strictly monotone preferences have downward-sloping curves. When both marginal utilities are positive, $\text{MRS} < 0$. Under monotone preferences the curves may also have horizontal pieces ($\text{MRS} = 0$) and vertical pieces (MRS undefined). Under convex preferences $|\text{MRS}|$ does not rise as $x_1$ increases along a curve, which is **diminishing MRS**. A strictly increasing transformation $f(u)$ with $f' > 0$ leaves the MRS unchanged. So the MRS depends only on the preferences.
>
>The standard families have these MRS formulas, with $\alpha, \beta > 0$ and $0 < \alpha < 1$ for Cobb-Douglas.
>
>| Family | $u(x_1, x_2)$ | $\text{MRS}$ |
>|---|---|---|
>| [[6.4a Cobb-Douglas]] | $x_1^{\alpha} x_2^{1-\alpha}$ | $-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\frac{x_2}{x_1}$ |
>| [[6.4b Perfect Substitutes]] | $\alpha x_1 + \beta x_2$ | $-\alpha/\beta$, constant |
>| [[6.4c Perfect Complements]] | $\min(\alpha x_1, \beta x_2)$ | $0$ where $\beta x_2 < \alpha x_1$; undefined at the kink $\beta x_2 = \alpha x_1$ and on the vertical part $\beta x_2 > \alpha x_1$ |
>| [[6.4d CES Utility]] | $(\alpha x_1^{\rho} + \beta x_2^{\rho})^{1/\rho}$ | $-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\left(\frac{x_1}{x_2}\right)^{\rho - 1}$ |
>
>At an interior optimum the MRS equals minus the price ratio, $\text{MRS} = -p_1/p_2$, as derived in [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]].

>[!warning] Sign of the MRS
>Here the MRS is the slope $dx_2/dx_1$ of the indifference curve, a negative number for goods the consumer likes. Many textbooks define the MRS as the positive number $u'_{x_1}/u'_{x_2}$, the absolute value of the slope. Under that convention the tangency condition reads $\text{MRS} = p_1/p_2$ instead of $\text{MRS} = -p_1/p_2$. Check which convention a problem uses before comparing signs.

# Intuition

An indifference curve lists the trades a consumer would accept. Moving along the curve, she gives up some of good 2, receives some of good 1, and ends up exactly as well off. The MRS measures the rate of this trade at one point. It is her personal exchange rate between the two goods. A market has its own exchange rate, the price ratio. If she values one coffee at 3 croissants and a café swaps one coffee for 2 croissants, she gains by trading croissants for coffee.

The diagram shows the curve $x_1^{1/3} x_2^{2/3} = 4$ and its tangent line at $A = (4, 4)$. The slope of the tangent is the MRS, here $-1/2$. Along the tangent, 2 more units of good 1 come with 1 unit less of good 2. Near $A$ the curve and the tangent almost coincide, so the MRS describes small trades well.

# Properties

#### *(Reading the Number)* The MRS is exact only for small trades and overstates the exchange for a whole unit on a convex curve.

The ratio $u'_{x_1}/u'_{x_2}$ values the marginal unit of good 1 in units of good 2. For a whole extra unit, the consumer must stay on the curve, which bends away from the tangent. A convex curve lies above its tangent line. So the amount of good 2 she gives up for one more unit of good 1 is at most $|\text{MRS}|$. At $A$ in the diagram, $|\text{MRS}| = 1/2$. Staying on the curve with $x_1 = 5$ requires $x_2 = \sqrt{64/5} \approx 3.58$, so a full unit of good 1 costs her only about $0.42$ units of good 2.

#### *(Diminishing MRS)* Along the curve of a convex preference, $|\text{MRS}|$ falls as the consumer holds more of good 1 and less of good 2.

With strictly monotone and convex preferences, the curve is the graph of a convex, decreasing function $x_2 = g(x_1)$. The slope of a convex function never falls, so $g'(x_1) = \text{MRS}$ rises toward 0 and its absolute value shrinks. In economic terms, a consumer with many croissants and few coffees gives up many croissants for a coffee. A consumer with many coffees gives up few. For Cobb-Douglas utility, $|\text{MRS}| = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{x_2}{x_1}$ falls because $x_2/x_1$ falls along the curve. The diagram shows the values $-4$, $-1/2$, and $-1/16$ at three points of the curve $x_1^{1/3}x_2^{2/3} = 4$. Perfect substitutes are the edge case, with a constant MRS and straight curves that still count as convex. For CES utility with $\rho > 1$, $|\text{MRS}| = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}\left(\frac{x_1}{x_2}\right)^{\rho-1}$ rises along the curve, and the preferences are not convex. [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions]] shows what this does to the consumer's choice.

#### *(MRS Is Ordinal)* The chain rule cancels the transformation from the ratio of marginal utilities.

Let $v = f(u)$ with $f' > 0$. By the chain rule, $v'_{x_i} = f'(u)\,u'_{x_i}$ for each good. The common factor $f'(u)$ cancels in the ratio,
$$-\frac{v'_{x_1}}{v'_{x_2}} = -\frac{f'(u)\,u'_{x_1}}{f'(u)\,u'_{x_2}} = -\frac{u'_{x_1}}{u'_{x_2}}.$$
So the MRS can be computed from whichever representation is easiest to differentiate. A quick test follows. If two utility functions have different MRS at some bundle, they cannot represent the same preferences. The condition $f' > 0$ matters at isolated points. For example, $f(u) = u^3$ is strictly increasing, but $f'(0) = 0$, and the ratio for $v$ is $0/0$ wherever $u = 0$.

#### *(Where the Formula Fails)* The formula needs a differentiable $u$ and $u'_{x_2} \neq 0$, so kinks, vertical pieces, and the axes need separate treatment.

Perfect complements have a kink on the line $\beta x_2 = \alpha x_1$, where $u$ has no derivative. Above that line $u = \alpha x_1$, so $u'_{x_2} = 0$ and the curve is vertical. Near the $x_2$ axis, a Cobb-Douglas curve at a positive level becomes very steep, since $|\text{MRS}|$ grows without bound as $x_1 \to 0$. Near the $x_1$ axis it becomes flat. The curve never reaches either axis. In these cases a graph of the indifference curves is more reliable than the formula.

# Derivation

We want the slope of the indifference curve through a bundle $(x_1^0, x_2^0)$ with $u(x_1^0, x_2^0) = \bar u$. Write the curve as the equation
$$F(x_1, x_2) = u(x_1, x_2) - \bar u = 0.$$
Assume that $u$ is continuously differentiable near the bundle and that $u'_{x_2}(x_1^0, x_2^0) \neq 0$. The implicit function theorem then gives a unique function $x_2 = g(x_1)$ near $x_1^0$ with $g(x_1^0) = x_2^0$ and $F(x_1, g(x_1)) = 0$. Its derivative is
$$g'(x_1) = -\frac{F'_{x_1}}{F'_{x_2}} = -\frac{u'_{x_1}}{u'_{x_2}}.$$
The constant $\bar u$ has zero derivative, so the partial derivatives of $F$ equal those of $u$. The same result follows from the chain rule. Differentiating the identity $u(x_1, g(x_1)) = \bar u$ with respect to $x_1$ gives
$$u'_{x_1} + u'_{x_2}\,g'(x_1) = 0.$$
Solving for $g'(x_1)$ gives the MRS in the callout. In terms of the total differential, $du = u'_{x_1}\,dx_1 + u'_{x_2}\,dx_2 = 0$ along the curve. The utility gained from $dx_1$ exactly offsets the utility lost from $dx_2$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Tickets and Books
>A student's utility over cinema tickets $x_1$ and paperback books $x_2$ is $u(x_1, x_2) = x_1^{2/3} x_2^{1/3}$. The bundles $(4, 9)$ and $(6, 4)$ lie on the same indifference curve, since $x_1^2 x_2 = 144$ at both.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the MRS at both bundles and interpret the numbers.
>>This is Cobb-Douglas with $\alpha = 2/3$, so $\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} = 2$ and $\text{MRS} = -2\,\frac{x_2}{x_1}$. Directly, $u'_{x_1} = \frac{2}{3}x_1^{-1/3}x_2^{1/3}$ and $u'_{x_2} = \frac{1}{3}x_1^{2/3}x_2^{-2/3}$, and their ratio is $2x_2/x_1$.
>>
>>At $(4, 9)$ the MRS is $-2 \cdot 9/4 = -4.5$. With many books and few tickets, she would give up about 4.5 books for one more ticket. At $(6, 4)$ the MRS is $-2 \cdot 4/6 = -4/3$. Now she would give up only about 1.33 books for a ticket. Tickets are worth less to her, measured in books, once she has more tickets and fewer books. This is diminishing MRS along one curve.

>[!example] (Medium) Same Curves, Different Formulas
>A student's utility over dining-hall meals $x_1$ and snacks $x_2$ is $u(x_1, x_2) = x_1 (x_2 + 2)$. The 2 counts the snacks her roommate leaves in the fridge. Consider also $v = \ln u$, $w = e^{u}$, and $z = x_1^2 (x_2 + 2)$, all for $x_1 > 0$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the MRS of $u$, $v$, $w$, and $z$. Which represent the same preferences as $u$?
>>For $u$, $u'_{x_1} = x_2 + 2$ and $u'_{x_2} = x_1$, so $\text{MRS} = -(x_2 + 2)/x_1$.
>>
>>For $v = \ln x_1 + \ln(x_2 + 2)$, $v'_{x_1} = 1/x_1$ and $v'_{x_2} = 1/(x_2 + 2)$. The ratio gives $\text{MRS} = -(x_2 + 2)/x_1$, the same as for $u$.
>>
>>For $w = e^{u}$, $w'_{x_1} = e^{u}(x_2 + 2)$ and $w'_{x_2} = e^{u} x_1$. The factor $e^{u}$ cancels, and the MRS is again $-(x_2 + 2)/x_1$. Both $v$ and $w$ are strictly increasing transformations of $u$, so they represent the same preferences.
>>
>>For $z$, $z'_{x_1} = 2x_1(x_2 + 2)$ and $z'_{x_2} = x_1^2$, so $\text{MRS} = -2(x_2 + 2)/x_1$. At the bundle $(4, 2)$, $u$ gives $-1$ and $z$ gives $-2$. Different MRS at one bundle means different preferences. With $z$, the student values meals twice as highly relative to snacks.

>[!example] (Hard) Swapping on a Meal Card
>A student's utility over coffees $x_1$ and croissants $x_2$ is $u(x_1, x_2) = x_1^{1/3} x_2^{2/3}$, so $\text{MRS} = -\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{x_2}{x_1}$. Her meal card holds $(1, 16)$ for the week. The café lets her swap one coffee for two croissants in either direction, in fractions if she likes. Each swap keeps $2x_1 + x_2 = 18$.
>
>>[!example]- In which direction should she swap, and where should she stop?
>>At $(1, 16)$ the MRS is $-\frac{1}{2} \cdot 16 = -8$. She would give up 8 croissants for one more coffee, and the café asks only 2. So she should swap croissants for coffee.
>>
>>Along the line $2x_1 + x_2 = 18$, let $U(x_1) = u(x_1, 18 - 2x_1)$. By the chain rule,
>>$$U'(x_1) = u'_{x_1} - 2u'_{x_2} = u'_{x_2}\left(|\text{MRS}| - 2\right).$$
>>Since $u'_{x_2} > 0$, utility rises while $|\text{MRS}| > 2$ and falls once $|\text{MRS}| < 2$. On the line, $|\text{MRS}| = \frac{18 - 2x_1}{2x_1}$. This equals 2 when $18 - 2x_1 = 4x_1$, that is $x_1 = 3$. She stops at $(3, 12)$.
>>
>>Check the utility values. At $(1, 16)$, $u = 16^{2/3} \approx 6.35$. At $(3, 12)$, $u = (3 \cdot 144)^{1/3} \approx 7.56$. One more swap, to $(4, 10)$, gives $u = 400^{1/3} \approx 7.37$, which is lower. Her stopping rule sets her personal exchange rate equal to the café's. Utility maximization uses the same tangency condition, with prices in place of the swap rate.
