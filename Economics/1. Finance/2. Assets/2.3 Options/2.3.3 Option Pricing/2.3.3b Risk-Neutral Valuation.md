>[!note] Risk-Neutral Valuation
>**Risk-neutral valuation** prices a derivative as its expected payoff discounted at the risk-free rate, where the expectation uses a special set of probabilities. In a binomial step of length $\Delta t$, the stock moves from $S$ to $uS$ or to $dS$. The **risk-neutral probability** of the up move is
>$$p = \frac{e^{r\Delta t} - d}{u - d}.$$
>Here $r$ is the continuously compounded risk-free rate. The number $p$ lies strictly between $0$ and $1$ exactly when $d < e^{r\Delta t} < u$, which is the condition for the model to be free of arbitrage. Under $p$ the stock earns the risk-free rate on average, $\hat{\mathbb{E}}[S_{\Delta t}] = Se^{r\Delta t}$. The hat marks an [[2.5a Expectation|expectation]] computed with risk-neutral probabilities. A derivative that pays $f_T$ at time $T$ has price
>$$f = e^{-rT}\,\hat{\mathbb{E}}[f_T].$$
>The rule holds for one step, where $T = \Delta t$, and for a tree of many steps.
>
>The price does not depend on the real-world probability of an up move or on anyone's expected return. It depends only on $u$ and $d$, which describe how the stock price moves, and on $r$. In the real world the stock has expected return rate $\mu$, and the up probability is $p^* = (e^{\mu\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)$. Passing from $p^*$ to $p$ changes the probabilities and leaves the volatility unchanged. Risk-neutral pricing, the hedged portfolio, and replication from [[2.3.3a One-Step Binomial Model]] give the same price. Risk-neutral probabilities exist in every arbitrage-free model. The [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]] applies the same rule to a continuous model of the stock.

# Intuition

The one-step price $f = e^{-r\Delta t}[p f_u + (1-p) f_d]$ has the form of an expected payoff discounted at $r$. Risk-neutral valuation treats $p$ as a probability and turns that form into a pricing rule. The rule is quick to apply on large trees and in continuous time, where building each hedge by hand is slow.

The real-world probability drops out because the option can be copied with shares and cash. The cost of the copy depends on the prices at each node. It does not depend on how likely each node is. So the pricing rule may use any probabilities that price the stock and the bond correctly. Only one set does that, and it is the set under which the stock grows at $r$.

Both worlds use the same tree. The nodes $uS$ and $dS$ are the same, and only the branch probabilities differ. Under $p^*$ the stock grows at its real expected rate $\mu$. Under $p$ it grows at $r$. Prices come from the second set.

# Properties

#### *(No-Arbitrage Condition)* Each way that $p$ can fall outside $(0, 1)$ is an arbitrage built from the stock and the bond.

Since $u - d > 0$, $p > 0$ holds exactly when $e^{r\Delta t} > d$, and $p < 1$ exactly when $e^{r\Delta t} < u$. Suppose $u \le e^{r\Delta t}$. The stock never beats the bond and loses to it in the down state. Short the stock and lend the proceeds at $r$. The position costs nothing, never loses, and gains in the down state. Suppose instead $e^{r\Delta t} \le d$. The stock never loses to the bond and beats it in the up state. Borrow at $r$ and buy the stock. In both cases the model itself is flawed, and the formula gives absurd prices, such as a negative price for a put. The general statement is that risk-neutral probabilities exist in every model without arbitrage.

#### *(Every Asset Earns $r$ Under $p$)* Under risk-neutral probabilities, the stock, the bond, and every derivative have expected return $r$.

The stock's risk-neutral expected price is $p\,uS + (1-p)\,dS = S\,[d + p(u - d)] = Se^{r\Delta t}$. The pricing rule $f = e^{-rT}\hat{\mathbb{E}}[f_T]$ says the same for any derivative, $\hat{\mathbb{E}}[f_T] = fe^{rT}$. A **risk-neutral** investor cares only about expected return and asks for no extra return for bearing risk. In a world of such investors, every asset would be priced to earn $r$, which explains the name. The probabilities $p$ make no claim that real investors are risk neutral. They are a tool for computing prices that hold whatever investors' attitudes to risk are.

#### *(Real-World Beliefs Drop Out)* Investors with different values of $p^*$ agree on the option price but disagree on its expected return.

The real-world probability solves $p^* uS + (1 - p^*) dS = Se^{\mu\Delta t}$, which gives $p^* = (e^{\mu\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)$. Investors usually demand $\mu > r$ for holding a risky stock, so $p^* > p$. Discounting the real-world expected payoff at $r$ gives a wrong price. For a call it overstates the price, because $p^*$ puts more weight on the up state where the call pays. The correct discount rate for a call under $p^*$ exceeds $\mu$, because the replicating portfolio holds shares bought partly with borrowed money. The (Medium) example shows the numbers.

#### *(Same Volatility in Both Worlds)* Passing from $p^*$ to $p$ changes the drift from $\mu$ to $r$ and keeps $\sigma$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules name [[Girsanov's Theorem]] as the continuous-time version of this fact. They do not state or prove it.

In a Cox-Ross-Rubinstein tree, $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ and $d = 1/u$ are set by the volatility ([[2.3.3c Binomial Tree]]). The variance of a one-step return is $p(1-p)(u-d)^2$ under $p$ and $p^*(1-p^*)(u-d)^2$ under $p^*$. Both $p$ and $p^*$ approach $1/2$ as $\Delta t \to 0$, so both variances equal $\sigma^2\Delta t$ to first order. With $\sigma = 30\%$, $\mu = 12\%$, $r = 4\%$, and monthly steps, $p = 0.4976$ and $p^* = 0.5363$. The two variances differ from $\sigma^2\Delta t$ by $0.25\%$ and $0.28\%$. So the $\sigma$ estimated from real price data is the right input for pricing.

#### *(Continuous-Time Version)* The Black-Scholes-Merton call price is the risk-neutral expected payoff of the call, discounted at $r$.

In the continuous model of [[7.6 Geometric Brownian Motion]], $\ln S_T$ is normal under risk-neutral probabilities, with mean $\ln S_0 + (r - \sigma^2/2)T$ and variance $\sigma^2 T$. The stock's real drift $\mu$ is replaced by $r$, as in the tree. The call price is
$$c = e^{-rT}\,\hat{\mathbb{E}}\big[\max(S_T - K, 0)\big] = S_0 N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT} N(d_2).$$
Here $N(x)$ is the standard normal CDF, the function written $\Phi(x)$ in [[2.2c.3 Gaussian Distribution]], and $d_1$, $d_2$ are defined in [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula]]. The term $N(d_2)$ is the risk-neutral probability that the call ends in the money.

#### *(State Prices)* The discounted risk-neutral probabilities are the prices of securities that pay $\$1$ in one state only.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules do not link risk-neutral probabilities to Arrow-Debreu securities or state prices.

A security that pays $\$1$ in the up state and nothing in the down state costs $e^{-r\Delta t}p$, by the pricing rule. Its partner for the down state costs $e^{-r\Delta t}(1 - p)$. These are the state prices of the one-step model ([[4.2.2b Arrow-Debreu Securities]]). Every derivative is a bundle of the two, so $f = f_u \cdot e^{-r\Delta t}p + f_d \cdot e^{-r\Delta t}(1-p)$. The two state prices add up to $e^{-r\Delta t}$, the price of a bond that pays $\$1$ in both states. Both are positive exactly when $0 < p < 1$.

# Derivation

We want to show that $p$ is the only probability under which the stock earns $r$, and that $f = e^{-rT}\hat{\mathbb{E}}[f_T]$ holds on a tree with any number of steps.

Let $p'$ be any up probability. The stock earns $r$ under $p'$ when
$$p'\,uS + (1 - p')\,dS = Se^{r\Delta t}.$$
The left side is the expected stock price after one step, and the right side is $S$ grown at $r$. The equation is linear in $p'$, and its coefficient $(u - d)S$ is not zero. So it has the single solution $p' = (e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u - d) = p$.

The one-step pricing formula from [[2.3.3a One-Step Binomial Model]] is $f = e^{-r\Delta t}[p f_u + (1-p) f_d] = e^{-r\Delta t}\hat{\mathbb{E}}[f_{\Delta t}]$. On a two-step tree, apply it at the two nodes after the first step, then at the root:
$$f = e^{-r\Delta t}\Big[p\,e^{-r\Delta t}\big(p f_{uu} + (1-p) f_{ud}\big) + (1-p)\,e^{-r\Delta t}\big(p f_{ud} + (1-p) f_{dd}\big)\Big].$$
Here $f_{uu}$, $f_{ud}$, and $f_{dd}$ are the payoffs after two up moves, one of each, and two down moves. Collecting terms gives
$$f = e^{-2r\Delta t}\big[p^2 f_{uu} + 2p(1-p) f_{ud} + (1-p)^2 f_{dd}\big].$$
The weights are the risk-neutral probabilities of the three end states, and $2\Delta t = T$. So $f = e^{-rT}\hat{\mathbb{E}}[f_T]$. The same steps extend to $N$ steps, where the end state with $i$ up moves has probability $\binom{N}{i}p^i(1-p)^{N-i}$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Six-Month Call
>A stock trades at $\$40$. In six months it will be either $\$48$ or $\$34$, so $u = 1.2$ and $d = 0.85$. The risk-free rate is $6\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A six-month European call has strike $\$42$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the risk-neutral probability, check that the stock earns the risk-free rate under it, and price the call.
>>The growth factor of the bond over six months is $e^{0.06 \times 0.5} = 1.0305$. The risk-neutral probability is
>>$$p = \frac{1.0305 - 0.85}{1.2 - 0.85} = 0.5156.$$
>>Under $p$, the expected stock price is $0.5156 \times 48 + 0.4844 \times 34 = \$41.22$, and $40 \times 1.0305 = \$41.22$. The stock earns exactly $r$.
>>
>>The call pays $\$6$ in the up state and $0$ in the down state. Its price is
>>$$c = e^{-0.03}\,(0.5156 \times 6 + 0.4844 \times 0) = \$3.00.$$
>>A hedge gives the same answer: $\Delta = 6/14 = 0.4286$ shares and $A = -\$14.14$ in cash cost $0.4286 \times 40 - 14.14 = \$3.00$.

>[!example] (Medium) Two Investors, One Price
>Take the stock and call of the (Easy) example. Investor A believes the up move has probability $p^* = 0.70$. Investor B believes it has probability $p^* = 0.55$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the expected stock return each investor believes in, show that both must accept the same call price, and compare the call's expected return under each belief.
>>Investor A expects the stock at $0.70 \times 48 + 0.30 \times 34 = \$43.80$. That is a six-month return of $9.50\%$, or $\mu = 2\ln(43.80/40) = 18.15\%$ per year. Investor B expects $\$41.70$, a return of $4.25\%$, or $\mu = 8.32\%$ per year.
>>
>>Both investors can buy $0.4286$ shares and borrow $\$14.14$ for a total of $\$3.00$. That portfolio pays exactly what the call pays in both states. Neither investor's belief enters this cost. If the call traded above or below $\$3.00$, both would see the same arbitrage. So both accept $c = \$3.00$.
>>
>>Each investor's expected call payoff is $0.70 \times 6 = \$4.20$ for A and $0.55 \times 6 = \$3.30$ for B. Against the price of $\$3.002$, the call's expected six-month return is $39.90\%$ for A and $9.92\%$ for B. Both exceed the stock's expected return for the same investor, because the call behaves like shares bought partly with borrowed money. Discounting $\$4.20$ or $\$3.30$ at $r$ would give $\$4.08$ or $\$3.20$, and both are wrong.

>[!example] (Hard) A Model With Arbitrage
>An analyst models a stock at $\$50$ that moves in six months to $\$51.50$ or $\$47.00$, so $u = 1.03$ and $d = 0.94$. The risk-free rate is $8\%$ per year with continuous compounding, so $e^{r\Delta t} = e^{0.04} = 1.0408$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute $p$, find an arbitrage in the model, and show what the pricing formula gives for a call and a put with strike $\$50$.
>>The formula gives $p = (1.0408 - 0.94)/(1.03 - 0.94) = 1.1201$, which is not a probability. The bond grows by $4.08\%$ in six months, while the stock grows by at most $3\%$.
>>
>>Short $100$ shares for $\$5{,}000$ and lend the proceeds at $8\%$. In six months the loan returns $\$5{,}204.05$. Buying back the shares costs $\$5{,}150$ in the up state and $\$4{,}700$ in the down state. The profit is $\$54.05$ or $\$504.05$. The trade costs nothing today and gains in every state, so the model allows arbitrage.
>>
>>The call pays $\$1.50$ in the up state and $0$ in the down state. The formula prices it at $e^{-0.04} \times 1.1201 \times 1.50 = \$1.61$. That is more than $\$1.44$, the present value of the call's largest possible payoff. The put pays $0$ in the up state and $\$3$ in the down state. The formula prices it at $e^{-0.04} \times (1 - 1.1201) \times 3 = -\$0.35$, a negative price for a payoff that is never negative. Both results show that the model must satisfy $d < e^{r\Delta t} < u$ before any pricing starts.
