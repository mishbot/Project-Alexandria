> [!note] Lagrangian Duality
> A large class of constrained optimization problems can be written in the **primal form**
>$$ \begin{aligned}
> p^* = \min_{x \in X} \quad & f_0(x) \\
> \text{s.t.} \quad & f_i(x) \le 0, \quad i=1,\ldots,m, \\
> & h_j(x) = 0, \quad j=1,\ldots,k.
> \end{aligned}$$
>
>Even when this problem is convex, the constraints may make it difficult to solve directly. **Lagrangian duality** handles constraints by embedding them into the objective using multipliers.
> 
>The **Lagrangian** is defined as
>$$\mathcal{L}(x,\lambda,\mu) = f_0(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i f_i(x) + \sum_{j=1}^k \mu_j h_j(x),$$
> where $\lambda_{i} \ge 0$ are multipliers for inequality constraints and $\mu_{j}$ are unrestricted multipliers for equality constraints.
> 
>For fixed $(\lambda,\mu)$, minimizing $\mathcal{L}$ over $x$ produces a **lower bound** on the primal objective. This gives the **dual function**
>$$g(\lambda,\mu) = \inf_{x \in X} \mathcal{L}(x,\lambda,\mu),$$
 and the **Lagrangian dual problem**,
 >$$d^* = \sup_{\lambda \ge 0,;\mu} g(\lambda,\mu).$$
 >
> A fundamental property is **weak duality**, where $d^* \le p^*$, meaning the dual problem always provides a lower bound on the primal problem. Any feasible dual variables therefore act as a **certificate of optimality**.
> 
>When the primal problem is convex, stronger results are possible. **Slater’s condition** ensures that the feasible set has “room” inside its boundary by requiring the existence of some $x_0$ such that $f_{i}(x_{0}) < 0$ for all inequality constraints, while satisfying all equality constraints.
>
>If Slater’s condition holds, then **strong duality** applies (i.e., $p^* = d^*$), and optimal dual variables $(\lambda, \mu)$ exist.
>
> Under strong duality, optimality can be characterized entirely through the **Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions**. A triple $(x^*, \lambda^*, \mu^*)$ is optimal if and only if it meets the following conditions:
> 1. **Primal feasibility:** $f_i(x^*) \le 0 \text{ for } i=1,\ldots,k, \quad h_j(x^*)=0 \text{ for } j=1,\ldots,m$.
> 2. **Dual feasibility:** $\lambda_{1}^{*},\ldots,\lambda_{k}^{*} \ge 0$.
> 3. **Complementary slackness:** $\lambda_i^* f_i(x^*) = 0, \quad i=1,\ldots,k$.
> 4. **Stationarity:** $\nabla f_0(x^*) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i^* \nabla f_i(x^*) + \sum_{j=1}^k \mu_j^* \nabla h_j(x^*) = 0$.
> 
> Complementary slackness encodes the idea that a constraint is either **active** or its multiplier is zero, while stationarity balances the objective gradient against the gradients of active constraints.
> 
> In the **unconstrained** convex case, the KKT conditions reduce to $\nabla f_{0}(x^{*})=0$, which is both necessary and sufficient for global optimality. For **non-convex problems**, KKT conditions remain necessary but are no longer sufficient; they become first-order necessary optimality conditions.
>
>Lagrangian duality is closely related to **convex relaxation**. If the feasible set $S$ is non-convex, replacing it with a convex superset $\tilde S$ yields a relaxed problem whose optimal value lower-bounds the original. Duality formalizes this idea: the dual problem searches over the best possible convex lower bounds.

# KKT Examples
>[!example] KKT Conditions I
>Consider the convex problem
>$$\begin{aligned}
>\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^4} \quad & x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 + x_4^2 \\\
>\text{s.t.} \quad & x_1 + x_2 + x_3 + x_4 = 1.
>\end{aligned}$$
>>[!example]- Write down the KKT conditions and compute the optimal solution.
>>We have that 
>>$$\mathcal{L}(x, \mu) = x_{1}^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 + x_4^2 + \mu(x_1 + x_2 + x_3 + x_4-1)$$.
>>Therefore,
>>$$\nabla \mathcal{L} = \begin{bmatrix}2x_{1}+\mu \\ 2x_{2}+\mu \\ 2x_{3} + \mu \\ 2x_{4} + \mu\end{bmatrix}=0,$$
>>such that $x_{1}^*=x_{2}^*=x_{3}^*=x_{4}^*=-\mu^* / 2$.
>>Substituting into the equality constraint, we obtain
>>$$4 \cdot (-\mu^* / 2) = 1 \iff \mu^* = -\frac{1}{2}.$$
>>Therefore, $x_{1}^*=x_{2}^*=x_{3}^*=x_{4}^*=1 / 4$.

>[!example] KKT Conditions II
>Consider the convex problem
>$$
>\begin{aligned}
>\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2} \quad & x_1^2 + x_2^2 \\\
>\text{s.t.} \quad
>& x_1 + x_2 = 1, \\
>& x_2 \le \alpha.
>\end{aligned}
>$$
>>[!example]- Write down the KKT conditions and compute the optimal solution.
>>We have that
>>$$\mathcal{L}(x, \lambda, \mu) = x_{1}^{2} + x_{2}^{2} + \lambda(x_{2} - \alpha) + \mu(x_{1}+x_{2}-1).$$
>>By stationarity, it follows that
>>$$\nabla \mathcal{L}=\begin{bmatrix}2x_{1}+\mu \\ 2x_{2} + \lambda + \mu\end{bmatrix} = 0,$$
>>where $x_{1}=-\mu / 2$ and $x_{2} = - \frac{\lambda+\mu}{2}$. Plugging this into the equality constraint,
>>$$-\mu / 2 - (\lambda+\mu) / 2 = 1 \iff \lambda + 2\mu = -2.$$
>>By complementary slackness, we have 2 cases: either $\lambda=0$ or $x_{2}=\alpha$. In case 1, the equation above simplifies to $\mu=-1$, and therefore $x_{1}=1 / 2$, $x_{2}=1 / 2$, such that feasibility requires $\alpha \geq 1 / 2=x_{2}$. In case 2, we have that 
>>$$\alpha = -(\lambda+\mu) / 2 \iff \lambda+\mu=-2\alpha,$$
>>so together with the equality constraint $\lambda + 2\mu = - 2$ we get
>>$$-2\mu+\mu-2=-2\alpha \iff \mu=2\alpha-2.$$
>>As such, $\lambda=2-4\alpha\geq 0$ by dual feasibility, and therefore $\alpha \le 1 / 2$. To summarize, we get the following two cases:
>>$$(\lambda^*, \mu^*) = \begin{cases}(0, -1) & \alpha \ge 1 / 2, \\ (2-4\alpha, 2\alpha-2) & \alpha < 1/2.\end{cases}$$
>>The neat thing about this is that at $\alpha=1 / 2$, both simplify to $(0, -1)$. Then,
>>$$(x_{1}^*, x_{2}^*) = \begin{cases}(1 / 2, 1 / 2) & \alpha \ge 1 / 2, \\ (1-2\alpha, \alpha) & \alpha < 1/2.\end{cases}$$

>[!example] Equality-Constrained Least Squares (KKT Conditions III)
>Consider the equality constrained least-squares problem
>$$
>\begin{aligned}
>\min_{x} \quad & \|Ax - b\|_2^2 \\
>\text{s.t.} \quad & Gx = h,
>\end{aligned}
>$$
>where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with $\text{rank}(A) = n$ and $G \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times n}$ with $\text{rank}(G) = p$.
>>[!example]- Write down the KKT conditions and compute the optimal solution.
>>The Lagrangian is
>>$$\mathcal{L}(x, \mu) = x^TA^TAx + (G^T\mu-2A^Tb)^Tx - \mu^Th,$$
>>where the term $b^Tb$ is a constant irrelevant to the optimization problem, and therefore is omitted. The stationary condition is
>>$$\nabla \mathcal{L} = 2A^TAx + G^T\mu - 2A^Tb = 2A^T(Ax-b)+G^T\mu=0,$$
>>which yields $x^* =(A^TA)^{-1}\left( A^Tb - \frac{1}{2}G^T\mu \right)$. Plugging $x^*$ into the equality constraint,
>>$$G(A^TA)^{-1}\left( A^Tb - \frac{1}{2}G^T\mu \right)=h,$$
>>such that $\mu^*=-2(G(A^TA)^{-1}G^T)^{-1}(h-G(A^TA)^{-1}A^Tb)$. Substituting into $x^*=\ldots$ yields the solution.

>[!example] KKT Conditions IV
>Consider the optimization problem
>$$
>\begin{aligned}
>\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2} \quad
>& 8x_1^2 + 10x_2^2 - 12x_1 x_2 + 50x_1 - 80x_2 \\
>\text{s.t.} \quad
>& x_1 + x_2 \le 1, \\
>& x_1^2 + 4x_2^2 \le 6, \\
>& x_1 \ge 0, \\
>& x_2 \ge 0.
>\end{aligned}
>$$
>>[!example]- Prove that the optimization problem is convex.
>>All constraints are trivially convex. To prove that the objective is convex, we take its gradient and Hessian:
>>$$\nabla f_{0}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}16x_{1}-12x_{2}+50 \\ 20x_{2} - 12x_{1} -80\end{bmatrix},$$
>>$$\nabla^2 f_{0}(x) = \begin{bmatrix}16 & -12 \\ -12 & 20\end{bmatrix},$$
>>and find that the eigenvalues of the Hessian are $\frac{\lambda_{1,2} = 36 \pm \sqrt{ 36^2 - 4\cdot 176 }}{2}$, which are both positive.
>>
>>We can also test this using the determinant and the trace, which are both positive:
>>- If $\det(H)> 0$, then $\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}>0$, which means that the eigenvalues are either both positive or negative.
>>- If $\text{tr}(H) >0$, then both eigenvalues are positive.
>>This is sufficient to show that $H$ is positive semi-definite, and therefore the problem is convex.
>
>>[!example]- Show that Slater's condition holds.
>>All inequality constraints have domains that meet $f_{i}(x) < 0$. Therefore, Slater's condition holds. An example of this is setting $x_{1}=x_{2}=\epsilon$, which strictly satisfies all constraints.
>
>>[!example]- Write the KKT conditions for this problem.
>>We have the following functions:
>>$$\begin{align}
>>f_{0}(x) &= 8x_{1}^2+10x_{2}^2-12x_{1}x_{2}+50x_{1}-80x_{2} \\
>>f_{1}(x) &= x_{1}+x_{2}-1 \\
>>f_{2}(x) &= x_{1}^2 + 4x_{2}^{2}-6 \\
>>f_{3}(x) &= -x_{1} \\
>>f_{4}(x) &= -x_{2}
>>\end{align}$$
>>Taking their gradients, we have
>>$$
>>\begin{align}
>>\nabla f_{0}(x) &= \begin{bmatrix}
>>16x_{1}-12x_{2}+50 \\ 20x_{2}-12x_{1}-80
>>\end{bmatrix} \\
>>\nabla f_{1}(x) &= \begin{bmatrix}
>>1 \\ 1
>>\end{bmatrix} \\
>>\nabla f_{2}(x) &= \begin{bmatrix}
>>2x_{1} \\ 8x_{2}
>>\end{bmatrix} \\
>>\nabla f_{3}(x) &= \begin{bmatrix}
>>-1 \\ 0
>>\end{bmatrix} \\
>>\nabla f_{0}(x) &= \begin{bmatrix}
>>0 \\ -1
>>\end{bmatrix}
>>\end{align}
>>$$
>>For the KKT conditions, all are satisfied:
>>$$
>>\begin{align}
>>(1) \quad & f_{i}(x) \leq 0 \\
>>(2) \quad & \lambda_{i}^{*} \geq 0 \\
>>(3) \quad & \lambda_{i}^{*}f_{i}(x) = 0 \\
>>(4) \quad & \nabla \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) = \begin{bmatrix}
>>16x_{1}-12x_{2}+50+\lambda_{1}+2\lambda_{2}x_{1}-\lambda_{3} \\
>>20x_{2}-12x_{1}-80 + \lambda_{1} + 8\lambda_{2}x_{2}-\lambda_{4}
>>\end{bmatrix}=0.
>>\end{align}
>>$$
>
>>[!example]- By studying the KKT conditions, show that $x^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\end{bmatrix}^T$ is a global minimum of the problem. 
 
>>Note that $x^*$ satisfies all primal constraints in $(1)$. We must now analyze the Lagrange multiplies $L$. We see that the nonzero equations for $(3)$ complementary slackness are
>>$$-2\lambda_{2}^*=0, \quad -\lambda_{4}^*=0,$$
>>therefore $\lambda_{2}^*=\lambda_{4}^*=0$. We then solve the stationarity condition, to see that
>>$$\begin{align}
>>16 \cdot 0 - 12 \cdot 1 + 50 + \lambda_{1}^*+2\cdot 0 - \lambda_{3} &= 0 \\
>>20-12\cdot 0-80+\lambda_{1}^*+8 \cdot 0 \cdot 1 - 0 &= 0,
>>\end{align}$$
>>so we have $\lambda_{1}^*=60$, $\lambda_{2}^*=98$, which satisfy stationarity and are nonnegative. Therefore, the given $x^*$ is a global minimum.

>[!example] Markov Transition Probabilities (KKT Conditions V)
>Consider the optimization problem
>$$\begin{aligned}
>\max_{x \in \mathbb{R}^n} \quad & \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i \log(x_i) \\
>\text{s.t.} \quad & x_i \ge 0, \quad i = 1,2,\ldots,n, \\
>& \sum_{i=1}^n x_i = c .
>\end{aligned}$$
>where $\alpha_{1}, \ldots, \alpha_{n}$ and $c$ are strictly positive constants. Problems of this form arise in maximum-likelihood estimation of the transition probabilities of discrete-time Markov chains.
>
>>[!example]- Show that Slater's condition holds.
>>We can set each $x_{i}=c / n$. Then, both constraints are strictly satisfied since $c>0$.
>
>>[!example]- Write the KKT conditions for this problem.
>>We know that $\log$ is concave. As such, $-\log$ is convex. We have:
>>$$\begin{align}
>>f_{0}(x)&=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\log(x_{i}) \\
>>f_{i}(x)&=-x_{i}, \quad i=1,2,\ldots,n \\
>>h(x) &= 1^Tx-c.
>>\end{align}$$
>>The gradients become:
>>$$\begin{align}
>>\nabla f_{0}(x) &= \begin{bmatrix}-\alpha_{1} / x_{1} & \ldots & -\alpha_{n} / x_{n}\end{bmatrix}^T &\\
>>\nabla f_{i}(x) &= -e_{i}, & i = 1,2,\ldots, n \\
>>\nabla h_{i}(x) &= 1.
>>\end{align}$$
>>Here, $-e_{i}$ is the negative of the standard basis vector with $1$ at position $i$ and zeros everywhere else. Now, let $x^* \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a global minimum of the convex problem. Then, $x^*$ must satisfy:
>>$$\begin{align}
>>(1) & \quad f_{i}(x) \leq 0 \quad \text{and} \quad h(x)=0 \\
>>(2) & \quad \lambda_{i}^* \geq 0 \\
>>(3) & \quad \lambda_{i}^{*}f_{i}(x)=0 \\
>>(4) & \quad \nabla \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda, \mu)=0
>>\end{align}$$
>>We check that stationarity is satisfied:
>>$$\nabla \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda, \mu) =-\alpha_{i} / x_{i}-\lambda_{i}+\mu \quad i=1,\ldots,n.$$
>
>>[!example]- Show that the Lagrange multipliers for the inequality constraints are all zero while the Lagrange multiplier for the equality constraint is equal to $-\frac{{\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{n}}}{c}$ (note that depending on your choice of $h$ you may also get $\frac{{\alpha_{i}+\ldots+\alpha_{n}}}{c}$).
>>Take a look at $(3)$ stationarity from the previous subpart. Either $f_{i}(x)=-x_{i}=0$ or $\lambda_{i}^*=0$. However, $x_{i}=0$ is not valid if you take a look at condition $(4)$, since each $x_{i}$ is a denominator, and therefore the stationary condition will be undefined. As such, all $\lambda_{i}^{*}=0$. Stationarity then simplifies to:
>>$$-\alpha_{i} / x_{i}+\mu=0 \iff x_{i}=\alpha_{i} / \mu.$$
>>Plug this into $h$:
>>$$\sum^{n}_{i=1}\alpha_{i} / \mu - c = 0 \iff \mu = \frac{1}{c}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\alpha_{i}.$$
>
>>[!example]- By solving the KKT conditions, find all optimal solutions $x^*$ of the optimization problem, and calculate the optimal objective.
