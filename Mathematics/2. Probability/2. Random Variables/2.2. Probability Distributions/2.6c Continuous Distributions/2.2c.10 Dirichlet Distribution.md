>[!note] Dirichlet Distribution
>A continuous random vector $\boldsymbol{\Theta} = (\Theta_1, \dots, \Theta_k)$ on the $(k-1)$-simplex $\Delta_{k-1} = \{\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^k : \theta_i \geq 0,\; \sum_{i=1}^k \theta_i = 1\}$ follows the **Dirichlet distribution** $\boldsymbol{\Theta} \sim \text{Dir}(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_k)$ with **concentration** parameters $\alpha_i > 0$, defined by the [[2.2c.1 Probability Density Function|probability density function]]
>$$f(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\prod_{i=1}^k \theta_i^{\alpha_i - 1}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\theta} \in \Delta_{k-1},$$
>where $B(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \dfrac{\prod_{i=1}^k \Gamma(\alpha_i)}{\Gamma\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^k \alpha_i\right)}$ is the **multivariate Beta function**, generalizing the two-parameter Beta function $B(\alpha, \beta)$.
>
>The Dirichlet distribution is the natural extension of the [[2.2c.9 Beta Distribution|Beta distribution]] from a single proportion to a **vector of proportions** that must sum to one. It models random probability vectors—any collection of non-negative quantities that partition a whole.
>
>Special cases:
>- If $k = 2$, the Dirichlet reduces to the [[2.2c.9 Beta Distribution|Beta]]: $\Theta_1 \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$ and $\Theta_2 = 1 - \Theta_1$.
>- If $\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \cdots = \alpha_k = 1$, then $\boldsymbol{\Theta} \sim \text{Uniform}(\Delta_{k-1})$—the uniform distribution on the simplex.
>- If all $\alpha_i = \alpha$ (the **symmetric Dirichlet**), the density is invariant to permutations of the components.
>
>The **concentration sum** $\alpha_0 = \sum_{i=1}^k \alpha_i$ controls how tightly the distribution concentrates around its mean. Large $\alpha_0$ yields vectors close to $(\alpha_1/\alpha_0, \dots, \alpha_k/\alpha_0)$; small $\alpha_0$ pushes mass toward the corners of the simplex (sparse vectors where one component dominates).
>
>>[!warning] Bayesian Interpretation
>>The Dirichlet is the **conjugate prior** for the [[2.2b.4 Multinomial Distribution|Multinomial]] likelihood. If $\boldsymbol{\Theta} \sim \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})$ and $\mathbf{X} \mid \boldsymbol{\Theta} \sim \text{Mult}(n, \boldsymbol{\Theta})$, then the posterior is
>>$$\boldsymbol{\Theta} \mid \mathbf{X} \sim \text{Dir}(\alpha_1 + x_1, \dots, \alpha_k + x_k).$$
>>The parameters $\alpha_i$ act as **pseudo-counts**: $\alpha_i - 1$ prior observations of category $i$. This generalizes [[2.2c.9 Beta Distribution|Beta]]–[[2.2b.3 Binomial Distribution|Binomial]] conjugacy to the multivariate setting.

# Intuition
The Dirichlet distribution is best understood as a distribution **over probability vectors**. Just as the [[2.2c.9 Beta Distribution|Beta distribution]] models uncertainty about a single probability $\theta \in [0,1]$, the Dirichlet models uncertainty about a vector of probabilities $(\theta_1, \dots, \theta_k)$ constrained to lie on the simplex.

Imagine you are modeling a $k$-sided die whose face probabilities are unknown. Before rolling, your prior belief about $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_k)$ is $\text{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})$. Each $\alpha_i$ represents how strongly you believe face $i$ will appear—larger $\alpha_i$ pulls more probability toward face $i$. After observing $x_i$ occurrences of face $i$ in $n$ rolls, the posterior is $\text{Dir}(\alpha_1 + x_1, \dots, \alpha_k + x_k)$, just as in the Beta-Binomial case but for all $k$ categories simultaneously.

The concentration sum $\alpha_0$ controls **confidence**: a symmetric $\text{Dir}(100, 100, 100)$ is tightly concentrated near $(1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$, expressing strong belief in a fair die, while $\text{Dir}(0.1, 0.1, 0.1)$ concentrates near the corners, expressing a belief that one face dominates.

# Properties
#### (*Mean*) $\mathbb{E}[\Theta_i] = \dfrac{\alpha_i}{\alpha_0}$, where $\alpha_0 = \sum_{j=1}^k \alpha_j$.
#### (*Variance*) $\text{Var}(\Theta_i) = \dfrac{\alpha_i(\alpha_0 - \alpha_i)}{\alpha_0^2(\alpha_0 + 1)}$.
As $\alpha_0 \to \infty$, the variance shrinks like $1/\alpha_0$, concentrating the distribution around its mean.

#### (*Covariance*) For $i \neq j$, $\text{Cov}(\Theta_i, \Theta_j) = \dfrac{-\alpha_i\alpha_j}{\alpha_0^2(\alpha_0 + 1)}$.
The components are **negatively correlated**, mirroring the simplex constraint $\sum \Theta_i = 1$: if one component increases, the others must collectively decrease.

#### (*Marginals*) Each component is Beta: $\Theta_i \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_i, \alpha_0 - \alpha_i)$.
#### (*Aggregation*) If components $i$ and $j$ are merged, $\Theta_i + \Theta_j \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_i + \alpha_j,\; \alpha_0 - \alpha_i - \alpha_j)$. More generally, any partition of $\{1, \dots, k\}$ into $m$ groups produces a $\text{Dir}$ with $m$ components whose parameters are the group sums.
#### (*Gamma Construction*) If $Y_i \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha_i, 1)$ are independent, then
$$\left(\frac{Y_1}{\sum_{j=1}^k Y_j}, \dots, \frac{Y_k}{\sum_{j=1}^k Y_j}\right) \sim \text{Dir}(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_k).$$
This is the standard constructive definition of the Dirichlet, generalizing the [[2.2c.9 Beta Distribution|Beta]]'s Gamma construction. It gives a concrete sampling algorithm: draw $k$ independent Gamma random variables and normalize.

#### (*Neutrality*) For any subset $S \subset \{1, \dots, k\}$, the sum $\sum_{i \in S}\Theta_i$ is independent of the **ratios** $\Theta_i / \sum_{j \in S}\Theta_j$ for $i \in S$. This "neutral" property means the total share allocated to a group is independent of how that share is distributed within the group.
#### (*Posterior Predictive*) Under a $\text{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})$ prior and Multinomial likelihood, the posterior predictive probability of the next observation falling in category $i$ after seeing counts $\mathbf{x}$ is $\dfrac{\alpha_i + x_i}{\alpha_0 + n}$, the Laplace smoothing estimator.

# Examples
>[!example] Document Topic Modeling
>In a topic model, each document has a topic distribution $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3)$ drawn from $\text{Dir}(0.5, 0.5, 0.5)$. A document contains $n = 100$ words, with each word assigned to a topic according to $\text{Mult}(100, \boldsymbol{\theta})$. Suppose the observed topic counts are $(x_1, x_2, x_3) = (60, 30, 10)$.
>>[!example]- What is the posterior distribution over topic proportions?
>>By Dirichlet-Multinomial conjugacy,
>>$$\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathbf{x} \sim \text{Dir}(0.5 + 60,\; 0.5 + 30,\; 0.5 + 10) = \text{Dir}(60.5,\; 30.5,\; 10.5).$$
>>The posterior mean is $\left(\frac{60.5}{101.5},\; \frac{30.5}{101.5},\; \frac{10.5}{101.5}\right) \approx (0.596,\; 0.300,\; 0.103)$, close to the observed proportions but slightly smoothed toward equality by the symmetric prior. The posterior is concentrated (large $\alpha_0 = 101.5$), reflecting the substantial sample size.

>[!example] Simplex Uniformity
>Let $\boldsymbol{\Theta} \sim \text{Dir}(1, 1, 1)$—the uniform distribution on the $2$-simplex $\{(\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3) : \theta_i \geq 0,\; \sum \theta_i = 1\}$.
>>[!example]- What is the marginal distribution and expected value of $\Theta_1$?
>>By the marginals property, $\Theta_1 \sim \text{Beta}(1, 2)$, which has density $f(\theta_1) = 2(1 - \theta_1)$ on $[0, 1]$. Its expected value is $\mathbb{E}[\Theta_1] = \frac{1}{1+2} = \frac{1}{3}$. Although the distribution on the simplex is uniform, each marginal is **not** uniform on $[0,1]$—it is skewed toward $0$ because the constraint $\sum \theta_i = 1$ limits how large any single component can be.
