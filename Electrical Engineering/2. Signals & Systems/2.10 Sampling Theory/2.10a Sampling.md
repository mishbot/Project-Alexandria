>[!note] Nyquist-Shannon Sampling Theorem
>**Sampling** is the process of converting a **continuous-time signal** into a **discrete-time signal** by taking measurements (samples) of a continuous-time signal $x_{C}(t)$ at a regular sampling period of $T_{s}$ to create a discrete-time signal $x_{D}[n]$ , such that
>$$x_{D}[n]=\left.x_{C}(t) \right\vert_{t=kT_{S}} \text{ for } k \in \mathbb{N}.$$
>This process is also outlined in the block diagram below, where a spike train $z(t)=\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}\delta(t-kT_{S})$ is multiplied with the input to produce the sampled signal $x_{Z}(t)= x_{C}(t) \times z(t)$, which is then converted to a discrete-time signal, $x_{D}[n]$:
>```tikz
>\usepackage{tikz}
>\usetikzlibrary{arrows.meta, positioning}
>
>\begin{document}
>\begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=2cm, >=Stealth, thick]
>
>% Nodes
>\node (input1) at (0,0) {};
>\node[draw, circle, minimum size=0.5cm, right=of input1] (adder) {$\times$};
>\node[below=1.5cm of adder] (input2) {};
>\node[draw, rectangle, minimum height=1.2cm, minimum width=1.8cm, right=of adder] (box) {Dirac-to\newline-Kronecker};
>\node[right=of box] (output) {};
>
>% Arrows
>\draw[->] (input1.east) -- (adder.west) node[at start, left] {$x_{C}(t)$};    % Input from the left to adder
>\draw[->] (input2.north) -- (adder.south) node[at start, below] {$z(t)$};  % Input from below to adder
>\draw[->] (adder.east) -- (box.west) node[midway, above]{$x_{Z}(t)$};       % Adder output to box
>\draw[->] (box.east) -- (output.west) node[right] {$x_{D}[n]$};      % Box output to the right
>
>\end{tikzpicture}
>\end{document}
>```
>
>Sampling theory is primarily governed by the **Nyquist-Shannon Sampling Theorem**, which states that a signal $x_{C}(t)$ that is band-limited (i.e. bounded) to a frequency $B$, such that $X(\omega) = 0 \text{ for all }|\omega| \geq B$, can be fully recovered from its sample $x_{D}[n]$ if
>$$\omega_{S} \geq 2B,$$
>where $\omega_{S}=\frac{2\pi}{T_{S}}$ is the sampling frequency, and $B$ is called the **Nyquist rate**.
>
>>[!warning] Note (Aliasing)
>>If the condition is not met ($\omega_{s} < 2B$), frequency components overlap with one another and cause distortion known as **aliasing**. Aliasing makes it impossible to distinguish higher frequencies from lower ones, leading to an inaccurate representation of the original signal. The effects of aliasing can be mitigated by pre-processing the $x_{D}[n]$ with a low pass (anti-aliasing) filter, as explained below.

# Examples
>[!example] The Carriage Wheel Effect
>Have you ever seen the wheels move the opposite way in a car commercial? That is called the carriage wheel effect. Suppose a wheel can be modeled by $x(t)=e^{j\omega_{0} t}$; intuitively, this makes sense, since as $t \to \infty$, $x(t)$ moves periodically anti-clockwise. Then, using CTFT, $X(\omega) = 2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_{0})$. We sample $x(t)$ at $\omega_{S}=\frac{2\pi}{T_{S}}$. 
>
>Suppose we choose the sampling frequency $\omega_{S}=\omega_{0}$. Then, $x[n]$ appears to be stationary. Intuitively, if $\omega_{S}$ is the frame rate (frames per second), and $\omega_{0}$ measures the spin of the wheels of the car, then each frame is recorded exactly at the same spot of the wheel. We can verify this by taking the CTFT of the impulse train, $Z(\omega)=\omega_{S}\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}\delta\left( \omega - k\omega_{S} \right)$, and performing the modulation
>$$\begin{align}
>x(t) \times z(t)&=\frac{1}{2\pi}(X * Z)(\omega) \\
>&= \omega_{S}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}\delta\left( \omega - k\omega_{S} \right)\delta(\omega-\omega_{0})d\omega  \\
>&= \omega_{S}\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}\delta\left( \omega - k\omega_{S} - \omega_{0} \right) \\
&= \omega_{S}\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}\delta\left( \omega - [k\omega_{S} + \omega_{0}] \right),
\end{align}$$
>by the sifting property of the Dirac delta. Then, $X_{Z}(\omega)$ can be graphically represented as:
>```tikz
>\usepackage{tikz}
>\usetikzlibrary{arrows.meta}
>
>\begin{document}
>\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.2, thick]
>
>% Axes
>\draw[->] (-4, 0) -- (6, 0) node[right] {$\omega$};
>\draw[->] (0, -0.5) -- (0, 2.5) node[above] {$X_z(\omega)$};
>\draw[-] (-0.125, 1.5) -- (0.125, 1.5) node[at start, left]{$\omega_S$};
>
>% Lines for arrows
>\draw[->] (1, 0) -- (1, 1.5) node[at start, below]{$\omega_0$};
>\draw[->] (5, 0) -- (5, 1.5) node[at start, below]{$\omega_0+\omega_S$};
>\draw[->] (-3, 0) -- (-3, 1.5) node[at start, below]{$\omega_0-\omega_S$};
>
>\end{tikzpicture}
>\end{document}
>```
>
>For $\omega_{S}=\omega_{0}$, this means that only one of the impulses will be preserved after reconstructing the signal:
