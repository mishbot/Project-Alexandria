>[!note] Second-Price Auctions
>In a **sealed-bid second-price auction**, each bidder submits one bid without seeing the others. The highest bid wins the object, and the winner pays the second-highest bid. With two bidders $i \in \{1, 2\}$, bidder $i$ knows her own value $v_i$ for the object, and her strategy is a bid $b_i \ge 0$. Her payoff is
>$$u_i(b_i, b_{-i}) = \begin{cases} v_i - b_{-i} & \text{if } b_i > b_{-i}, \\ (v_i - b_{-i})/2 & \text{if } b_i = b_{-i}, \\ 0 & \text{if } b_i < b_{-i}, \end{cases}$$
>where a tie is broken by a fair coin.
>- Bidding one's value, $b_i^* = v_i$, is weakly dominant ([[2.2b Strategic Dominance]]), which means
>$$u_i(v_i, b_{-i}) \ge u_i(b_i, b_{-i}) \quad \text{for all } b_i, \text{ all } b_{-i}, \text{ and } i = 1, 2.$$
>Every other bid does strictly worse against some rival bids.
>- The reason is that a bidder's own bid decides only whether she wins, never what she pays. Bidding $v_i$ wins exactly when the price $b_{-i}$ is below her value.
>- The profile $(v_1, v_2)$ is an equilibrium in dominant strategies, and therefore a [[2.2c Nash Equilibrium]]. The bidder with the higher value wins and pays the other bidder's value, so the object goes to the person who values it most.
>- The result needs no beliefs about the rival's value and no assumption about attitudes to risk.
>- With $n$ bidders, the same argument holds with $b_{-i}$ replaced by the highest rival bid, $\max_{j \ne i} b_j$.
>- In a **first-price auction** the winner pays her own bid. Bidding $v_i$ then always earns zero, so bidders bid below their values.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture defines the two-bidder auction and proves, case by case, that bidding one's value is weakly dominant. The rest is an extension: the strict losses from other bids, the $n$-bidder and first-price lines of the callout, efficiency and the winner's surplus, proxy bidding, and the other Nash equilibria.

# Intuition

A seller who does not know the buyers' values can still sell to the one who values the object most, if the buyers reveal their values. In most selling formats they have no reason to. A buyer who bids her value in a first-price auction wins at a price that leaves her nothing. The second-price rule removes this temptation. The winner's price is set by the rival, so the only thing a bid changes is whether she wins. She should win when the price is below her value and lose when it is above. Bidding her value achieves both.

On the right the rival bids $b_{-i} = 8$. Every bid above $8$ wins and loses $2$, and every bid below $8$ earns $0$. In both panels the bid $b_i = 6$ sits on the highest part of the graph. The bidder does not need to know which panel she is in.

# Properties

#### *(Strict Somewhere)* Each other bid gives the same payoff as $v_i$ for most rival bids and a strictly lower payoff for some.

An overbid $b_i > v_i$ changes the payoff only when $v_i < b_{-i} \le b_i$. There the overbid wins or ties at a price $b_{-i} > v_i$, a loss, while the truthful bid loses and earns $0$. An underbid $b_i < v_i$ changes the payoff only when $b_i \le b_{-i} < v_i$. There the underbid loses or ties, while the truthful bid wins and earns the full $v_i - b_{-i} > 0$. The diagram plots payoff against the rival's bid for $v_i = 6$, an underbid of $4$, and an overbid of $8$. The shaded triangles are the only places where the deviations differ from the truthful bid, and they always lose.

#### *(The Rival Sets the Price)* A bid works like an instruction to buy at any price up to the bid.

Given the rival's bid, bidder $i$ faces a take-it-or-leave-it price $b_{-i}$, which she cannot change. Her bid only says which prices she accepts. A buyer who faces a posted price buys exactly when the price is below her value, and bidding $v_i$ follows that rule for every possible price. Proxy bidding on online auction sites works the same way. The bidder states a maximum, and the site raises her bid only as far as needed to beat the next-highest maximum.

#### *(Efficient but Not Fully Extracting)* Truthful bidding sends the object to the highest-value bidder at a price equal to the second-highest value.

The seller does not need to know the values to allocate the object efficiently. The bids reveal them. The winner keeps the surplus $v_{(1)} - v_{(2)}$, the gap between the highest and second-highest values. A seller who knew every value could charge the winner her full value, as in first-degree [[6.9.2 Price Discrimination]]. The seller gives up this amount because she does not know the values. It shrinks as more bidders join, because the second-highest value moves closer to the highest.

#### *(Other Nash Equilibria)* The auction has Nash equilibria in which the lower-value bidder wins.

Nash equilibrium asks only that each bid be a best response to the other's actual bid. If the low-value bidder bids very high and the high-value bidder bids $0$, neither gains by deviating, as the Hard example shows. These equilibria use weakly dominated bids. The dominance argument rules them out, which is why the truthful prediction is stronger than Nash equilibrium alone.

# Derivation

We want to show $u_i(v_i, b_{-i}) \ge u_i(b_i, b_{-i})$ for every $b_i$ and every $b_{-i}$. Fix the rival's bid and consider three cases.

1. If $b_{-i} > v_i$, bidding $v_i$ loses and earns $0$. Any $b_i < b_{-i}$ also loses and earns $0$. A tie, $b_i = b_{-i}$, earns $(v_i - b_{-i})/2 < 0$. Winning with $b_i > b_{-i}$ earns $v_i - b_{-i} < 0$. No bid beats $0$.
2. If $b_{-i} = v_i$, bidding $v_i$ ties and earns $(v_i - v_i)/2 = 0$. Losing earns $0$, and winning earns $v_i - b_{-i} = 0$. Every bid earns $0$.
3. If $b_{-i} < v_i$, bidding $v_i$ wins and earns $v_i - b_{-i} > 0$. Any $b_i > b_{-i}$ earns the same. A tie earns half as much, and losing earns $0$. No bid does better.

In each case $v_i$ is a best response ([[2.2a Best Response]]) to $b_{-i}$, so it is weakly dominant. The strict losses come from case 1 with $b_i \ge b_{-i}$ and from case 3 with $b_i \le b_{-i}$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Bidding for a Guitar
>Ana values a vintage guitar at $v = 80$ dollars. She does not know her rival's bid, which may be $60$, $75$, $85$, or $100$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute her payoff from bidding $70$, $80$, and $90$ against each rival bid. Which bid is never worse?
>>| Rival bid | Bid $70$ | Bid $80$ | Bid $90$ |
>>| --- | --- | --- | --- |
>>| $60$ | $20$ | $20$ | $20$ |
>>| $75$ | $0$ | $5$ | $5$ |
>>| $85$ | $0$ | $0$ | $-5$ |
>>| $100$ | $0$ | $0$ | $0$ |
>>
>>Against $60$ every bid wins and pays $60$. Against $75$ the bid of $70$ loses a trade worth $5$. Against $85$ the bid of $90$ wins and pays $85$ for a guitar worth $80$. Bidding $80$ is never worse than the others, and each other bid is strictly worse in one row.

>[!example] (Medium) Three Bidders for a Used Car
>Three dealers value a used car at $30$, $45$, and $60$ (hundreds of dollars), and each bids her value in a second-price auction.
>
>>[!example]- Find the winner, the price, and each payoff. Then check two deviations: the dealer with value $60$ bids $50$ or $100$, and the dealer with value $45$ bids $70$.
>>The dealer with value $60$ wins and pays the second-highest bid, $45$. Her payoff is $15$, and the others earn $0$. The seller receives $45$, the second-highest value. If the winner bids $50$ or $100$, she still wins and still pays $45$, so her payoff stays at $15$. Her bid matters only if she bids $45$ or less. A tie at $45$ halves her payoff, and a lower bid loses a trade worth $15$. If the dealer with value $45$ bids $70$, she wins but pays $60$, so her payoff is $45 - 60 = -15$. Neither deviation helps.

>[!example] (Hard) An Inefficient Equilibrium
>Two bidders have values $v_1 = 10$ and $v_2 = 20$.
>
>>[!example]- Show that the bids $(b_1, b_2) = (25, 0)$ form a Nash equilibrium. Who wins, and is the outcome efficient? Explain why the dominance argument still predicts that bidder 2 wins.
>>Bidder 1 wins and pays $0$, so $u_1 = 10$ and $u_2 = 0$. Bidder 1 cannot do better. Any positive bid still wins at price $0$, and a bid of $0$ ties and earns $(10 - 0)/2 = 5$. Bidder 2 cannot do better either. A bid below $25$ loses and earns $0$, a tie earns $(20 - 25)/2 = -2.5$, and a bid above $25$ wins and earns $20 - 25 = -5$. So $(25, 0)$ is a Nash equilibrium.
>>
>>The outcome is inefficient. The object goes to the bidder who values it at $10$ instead of $20$, and total surplus falls from $20$ to $10$. Both bids are weakly dominated. If bidder 2 bid anything between $10$ and $25$, bidder 1's bid of $25$ would make her pay more than $10$ and lose money. Bidder 2's bid of $0$ throws away the profit available against any rival bid below $20$. With dominant strategies, each bidder bids her value without relying on the other's mistakes. Bidder 2 then wins, pays $10$, and earns $10$, and the seller receives $10$.
