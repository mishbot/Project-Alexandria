>[!note] CES Utility
>The **constant elasticity of substitution (CES)** utility function is
>$$u(x_1, x_2) = \left(\alpha x_1^{\rho} + \beta x_2^{\rho}\right)^{1/\rho}, \qquad \alpha, \beta > 0, \quad \rho < 1, \quad \rho \neq 0.$$
>The weights $\alpha$ and $\beta$ set the relative importance of the two goods. The parameter $\rho$ sets how easily the consumer substitutes one good for the other. The [[6.1.4 Indifference Curves and MRS|MRS]] is
>$$\text{MRS} = -\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\left(\frac{x_1}{x_2}\right)^{\rho-1},$$
>so for $\rho < 1$ the indifference curves are smooth and strictly convex, and every optimum is interior. At prices $\mathbf{p} = (p_1, p_2)$ and income $M$, the Marshallian demands derived in [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]] are
>$$x_1^* = \frac{M}{p_1\left[1 + \left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{\rho-1}}\left(\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}}\right]}, \qquad x_2^* = \frac{M}{p_2\left[1 + \left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\rho-1}}\left(\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right)^{\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}}\right]}.$$
>They imply the quantity ratio $x_2^*/x_1^* = \left(\beta p_1/(\alpha p_2)\right)^{1/(1-\rho)}$. The other three families are limits or special cases. As $\rho \to 0$, CES becomes [[6.4a Cobb-Douglas]] with exponents $\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}$ and $\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}$, and $x_1^* = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\frac{M}{p_1}$. At $\rho = 1$ it is [[6.4b Perfect Substitutes|perfect substitutes]] $\alpha x_1 + \beta x_2$, and as $\rho \to 1^-$ demand tends to $M/p_1$ if $p_1/p_2 < \alpha/\beta$ and to $0$ if $p_1/p_2 > \alpha/\beta$. As $\rho \to -\infty$ it becomes [[6.4c Perfect Complements|perfect complements]] $\min(x_1, x_2)$, with $x_1^* = x_2^* = M/(p_1 + p_2)$. For $0 < \rho < 1$ the goods are gross substitutes, $\partial x_1^*/\partial p_2 > 0$. For $\rho < 0$ they are gross complements, $\partial x_1^*/\partial p_2 < 0$. For every $\rho < 1$ they are net substitutes, as in every two-good model with smooth indifference curves. For $\rho > 1$ the preferences are not convex, and the interior critical point is a minimum ([[6.2.3 Corner Solutions]]). Write $P(\mathbf{p})$ for the cost of one unit of utility,
>$$P(\mathbf{p}) = \left(\alpha^{\frac{1}{1-\rho}}\,p_1^{\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}} + \beta^{\frac{1}{1-\rho}}\,p_2^{\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}}\right)^{\frac{\rho-1}{\rho}}.$$
>Then the [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function|indirect utility]], the [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization|expenditure function]], and the Hicksian demands are
>$$v(\mathbf{p}, M) = \frac{M}{P(\mathbf{p})}, \qquad e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = \bar u\,P(\mathbf{p}), \qquad h_1 = \bar u\left(\frac{\alpha P(\mathbf{p})}{p_1}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\rho}}, \quad h_2 = \bar u\left(\frac{\beta P(\mathbf{p})}{p_2}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\rho}},$$
>where $\bar u$ is the target utility. CES is the general family of the four, and $\rho$ moves it continuously from perfect complements through Cobb-Douglas to perfect substitutes.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture derived only the Marshallian demand for CES. The formulas for $P(\mathbf{p})$, $v$, $e$, and $h_i$ follow from it through the definitions in [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function]] and [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]].

# Intuition

Each of the other three families fixes how easily the consumer substitutes. Perfect complements allow no substitution. Perfect substitutes allow unlimited substitution at one fixed rate. Cobb-Douglas sits at one point in between. A researcher who studies real demand does not know in advance which case applies. CES turns the degree of substitution into a parameter, $\rho$, that data can determine. The cost is a longer demand formula.

The diagram draws CES indifference curves with $\alpha = \beta$ for five values of $\rho$. All of them pass through $A = (2, 2)$, and every curve with finite $\rho$ has slope $-\alpha/\beta = -1$ there. The limiting curve for $\rho \to -\infty$ has its kink at $A$. A consumer facing equal prices therefore chooses $A$ whatever $\rho$ is. The $\rho = 1$ consumer is indifferent along the whole budget line, which coincides with that consumer's indifference line. The curves differ in how sharply they bend. With $\rho$ close to $1$ they are nearly flat, and the consumer trades one good for the other readily. With very negative $\rho$ they are nearly L-shaped, and the consumer hardly trades at all.

# Properties

#### *(Sign of $\rho$)* The goods are gross substitutes when $0 < \rho < 1$ and gross complements when $\rho < 0$.

The demands imply a ratio of spending on the two goods of
$$\frac{p_2x_2^*}{p_1x_1^*} = \left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\rho}}\left(\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right)^{\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}}.$$
When $p_2$ rises, this ratio falls if $\rho > 0$ and rises if $\rho < 0$. The budget is fixed, so spending on good 1 moves in the opposite direction to the ratio. With $p_1$ unchanged, $x_1^*$ therefore rises when $0 < \rho < 1$ and falls when $\rho < 0$. As $\rho \to 0$ the ratio stops depending on prices, which is the Cobb-Douglas case $\partial x_1^*/\partial p_2 = 0$. Differentiating $x_1^*$ directly gives the same signs. The bracket in its denominator contains $(p_2/p_1)^{\rho/(\rho-1)}$, whose exponent is negative for $0 < \rho < 1$ and positive for $\rho < 0$.

>[!warning] Net or Gross
>The lecture stated this rule as "$\rho > 0$: goods are (net) substitutes; $\rho < 0$: goods are (net) complements". With two goods no pair of goods can be net complements, and for CES $\partial h_1/\partial p_2 > 0$ at every $\rho < 1$. The sign of $\rho$ classifies gross substitutes and gross complements, as stated above. The (Hard) example below shows a pair that is gross complements and net substitutes at the same time.

#### *(Substitution Along the Curve)* The larger $\rho$ is, the farther the cheapest bundle moves along an indifference curve when relative prices change.

At the optimum $x_2^*/x_1^* = \left(\beta p_1/(\alpha p_2)\right)^{1/(1-\rho)}$, so a change in $p_1/p_2$ enters raised to the power $1/(1-\rho)$. This power is near $0$ for very negative $\rho$, equals $1$ for Cobb-Douglas, and grows without bound as $\rho \to 1$. The diagram keeps three curves through $A = (2, 2)$ and raises $p_1$ to four times $p_2$. The cheapest bundle on each curve moves from $A$ to the dot of the same color. The $\rho = -1$ consumer moves to $(1.5, 3)$, the Cobb-Douglas consumer to $(1, 4)$, and the $\rho = 1/2$ consumer to $(0.32, 5.12)$. A $\rho \to -\infty$ consumer would stay at $A$, and a $\rho = 1$ consumer would jump to the corner $(0, 4)$. Each move is that consumer's substitution effect in the [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]].

#### *(Interior Optimum)* Every $\rho < 1$ gives an interior optimum, $\rho = 1$ gives a corner, and $\rho > 1$ turns the tangency into a minimum.

For $\rho < 1$ the exponent $\rho - 1$ in the MRS is negative. As $x_1 \to 0$ the MRS goes to $-\infty$, and as $x_2 \to 0$ it goes to $0$. Near the $x_2$ axis each indifference curve is steeper than any budget line, and near the $x_1$ axis it is flatter. The tangency therefore lies strictly inside, and no corner check is needed. For $0 < \rho < 1$ the curves reach the axes with a vertical or horizontal slope. For $\rho < 0$ they never reach the axes, because a bundle with $x_i = 0$ has utility $0$ and each good is essential. For $\rho > 1$ the MRS becomes steeper as $x_1$ grows along a curve, so the curves bend away from the origin. The preferences are then not convex, and the tangency is the worst point on the budget line. The consumer buys only one good, as in [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions]].

#### *(Limits Drop the Weights)* As $\rho \to -\infty$ the limit is $\min(x_1, x_2)$ for any $\alpha$ and $\beta$, so the weights play no role.

Suppose $x_1 < x_2$ and write $u = x_1\left(\alpha + \beta(x_2/x_1)^{\rho}\right)^{1/\rho}$. As $\rho \to -\infty$, $(x_2/x_1)^{\rho} \to 0$ and $\alpha^{1/\rho} \to 1$, so $u \to x_1$. The same steps with $x_2 < x_1$ give $u \to x_2$. This is why the limit demand $M/(p_1 + p_2)$ contains no $\alpha$ or $\beta$. To reach $\min(\alpha x_1, \beta x_2)$, use the weights $\alpha^{\rho}$ and $\beta^{\rho}$, which give $\left((\alpha x_1)^{\rho} + (\beta x_2)^{\rho}\right)^{1/\rho}$. The limit $\rho \to 0$ has a similar subtlety. The function itself goes to infinity or to zero unless $\alpha + \beta = 1$, because $\alpha x_1^{\rho} + \beta x_2^{\rho} \to \alpha + \beta$. The rescaled function $\left((\alpha x_1^{\rho} + \beta x_2^{\rho})/(\alpha + \beta)\right)^{1/\rho}$ represents the same preferences and converges to $x_1^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}x_2^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}$. The case "$\rho = 0$" is shorthand for this limit, since $1/\rho$ is undefined at $\rho = 0$.

#### *(The Name)* A $1\%$ rise in the price ratio $p_1/p_2$ raises the quantity ratio $x_2^*/x_1^*$ by $1/(1-\rho)$ percent at every price.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The elasticity of substitution and the formula $\sigma = 1/(1-\rho)$ were not covered in lecture. They explain the name of the family.

The **elasticity of substitution** is $\sigma = d\ln(x_2^*/x_1^*)/d\ln(p_1/p_2)$, the percentage change in the quantity ratio per $1\%$ change in the price ratio. Taking logs of the ratio formula gives $\sigma = 1/(1-\rho)$, a number that does not depend on prices or on the bundle. That constancy gives the family its name. Perfect complements have $\sigma = 0$, Cobb-Douglas has $\sigma = 1$, and $\sigma \to \infty$ as $\rho \to 1$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Four Consumers and One Price Rise
>Four consumers each have $M = 12$ and CES utility with $\alpha = \beta = 1$. They differ only in $\rho$, which is $1/2$, $\rho \to 0$ (Cobb-Douglas), $-2$, and $1$. Prices start at $p_1 = 3$ and $p_2 = 2$. Then $p_2$ rises to $4$.
>
>>[!example]- For each consumer, does demand for good 1 rise, fall, or stay the same?
>>For $\rho = 1/2$ the goods are gross substitutes, so $x_1^*$ rises. The formula gives $12/(3[1 + (2/3)^{-1}]) = 1.60$ before and $12/(3[1 + (4/3)^{-1}]) \approx 2.29$ after.
>>
>>For the Cobb-Douglas consumer $x_1^* = M/(2p_1) = 2$ both times, since demand does not depend on $p_2$.
>>
>>For $\rho = -2$ the goods are gross complements, so $x_1^*$ falls. The exponents are $1/(\rho-1) = -1/3$ and $\rho/(\rho-1) = 2/3$. Demand goes from $12/(3[1 + (2/3)^{2/3}]) \approx 2.27$ to $12/(3[1 + (4/3)^{2/3}]) \approx 1.81$.
>>
>>For $\rho = 1$ the consumer has perfect substitutes with equal weights and buys only the cheaper good. At first good 2 is cheaper, so $x_1^* = 0$. After the rise good 1 is cheaper, so $x_1^* = 12/3 = 4$. Demand jumps because the price ratio crossed $\alpha/\beta = 1$.

>[!example] (Medium) Two Home Baristas
>Two home baristas each spend $M = 120$ a month on two coffee beans. Bean 1 costs $p_1 = 1$ per bag and bean 2 costs $p_2 = 2$. Both have CES utility with $\alpha = \beta = 1$. Barista S has $\rho = 1/2$ and treats the beans as close substitutes. Barista C has $\rho = -1$ and wants a balanced blend.
>
>>[!example]- Find each barista's bundle and the share of the budget spent on the cheaper bean.
>>For barista S the exponents are $1/(\rho-1) = -2$ and $\rho/(\rho-1) = -1$. Demand is $x_1^* = 120/(1 + 2^{-1}) = 80$ and $x_2^* = 120/(2[1 + (1/2)^{-1}]) = 20$. The budget share of bean 1 is $80/120 = 2/3$.
>>
>>For barista C the exponents are $-1/2$ and $1/2$. Demand is $x_1^* = 120/(1 + \sqrt{2}) \approx 49.71$ and $x_2^* = 120/(2[1 + \sqrt{1/2}]) \approx 35.15$. The budget share of bean 1 is about $41\%$.
>>
>>A Cobb-Douglas consumer with equal weights would spend exactly half on each bean. Barista C spends more than half the budget on the expensive bean. With $\rho < 0$ the consumer needs both goods, so a high price raises that good's budget share.

>[!example] (Hard) Gross Complements, Net Substitutes
>Barista C from the previous example ($\rho = -1$, $\alpha = \beta = 1$, $M = 120$) faces a rise in $p_2$ from $2$ to $4$, with $p_1 = 1$ unchanged.
>
>>[!example]- Find the new bundle and the Hicksian bundle at the old utility. Split the change in $x_1$ into substitution and income effects.
>>With $\alpha = \beta = 1$ and $\rho = -1$ the cost of one unit of utility is $P = (\sqrt{p_1} + \sqrt{p_2})^2$. At the old prices $P = (1 + \sqrt 2)^2 \approx 5.83$, so the old utility is $\bar u = 120/5.83 \approx 20.59$.
>>
>>At the new prices the Marshallian demands are $x_1^* = 120/(1 + \sqrt 4) = 40$ and $x_2^* = 120/(4[1 + \sqrt{1/4}]) = 20$. Demand for bean 1 fell from $49.71$ to $40$, so the beans are gross complements.
>>
>>The new cost of utility is $P = (1 + 2)^2 = 9$, so reaching $\bar u$ costs $e = 20.59 \times 9 \approx 185.30$. The Hicksian demands are $h_1 = \bar u\,(P/p_1)^{1/2} = 20.59 \times 3 \approx 61.77$ and $h_2 = \bar u\,(P/p_2)^{1/2} = 20.59 \times 1.5 \approx 30.88$. Their cost is $61.77 + 4 \times 30.88 \approx 185.30$, as it should be.
>>
>>The substitution effect on bean 1 is $61.77 - 49.71 = +12.06$. It is positive, so the beans are net substitutes. The income effect is $40 - 61.77 = -21.77$. The total is $-9.71$. The income effect is the larger one because bean 2 took about $59\%$ of the budget and its price doubled.
