>[!note] Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics
>Two systems are in **thermal contact** when energy can pass between them as [[2.4.2 Heat|heat]]. Two systems in thermal contact are in **thermal equilibrium** when no net energy flows between them. Their macroscopic properties, such as pressure, volume, and thermometer reading, then stay constant in time.
>
>The **zeroth law of thermodynamics** states that thermal equilibrium is transitive. Suppose system $A$ is in thermal equilibrium with system $C$, and system $B$ is also in thermal equilibrium with $C$. Then $A$ and $B$ are in thermal equilibrium with each other. In symbols,
>$$A \sim C \ \text{ and } \ B \sim C \implies A \sim B,$$
>where $X \sim Y$ means that no net energy flows between $X$ and $Y$ when they are placed in thermal contact.
>
>The law gives temperature its meaning. Temperature is the property that two systems share exactly when they are in thermal equilibrium, so that
>$$T_A = T_B \iff A \sim B.$$
>Two systems at different temperatures are not in equilibrium. When they touch, energy flows from the hotter system to the colder one. The flow stops when the two temperatures match.
>
>The law holds for systems of any size, material, or phase. It describes only the final equilibrium state and says nothing about how long the systems take to reach it. The [[2.5.2 First Law of Thermodynamics|first law]] accounts for the amount of energy exchanged, and the [[2.7.2 Second Law of Thermodynamics|second law]] sets the direction of the flow.

# Intuition

We often need to compare the temperatures of two objects that cannot touch. Suppose we want to compare two cups of coffee in different rooms. We cannot put the cups in contact, but we can put one thermometer in each cup in turn. The thermometer plays the role of system $C$. Suppose it shows the same reading in both cups. The zeroth law then guarantees that the cups would exchange no net energy if they touched. Every temperature measurement relies on this step.

The law is an experimental fact. Logic alone does not require it, because many relations are not transitive. Two people who each know a third person do not always know each other. Experiments show that thermal equilibrium never behaves this way. The law received its name late. Physicists recognized it as a separate principle after the first and second laws already had their names. It is logically more basic than both, so they numbered it zero. # Properties

#### *(Temperature as a Label)* Transitivity lets one number label every group of systems in mutual equilibrium.

Every system is in thermal equilibrium with itself, and $A \sim B$ implies $B \sim A$. Together with transitivity, these facts make thermal equilibrium an equivalence relation (a relation that sorts a collection into groups with no overlap). All systems inside one group are in equilibrium with each other. No system is in equilibrium with a member of another group. Temperature is the number attached to each group. The zeroth law guarantees that such a number exists. It does not say which number to use, and that choice belongs to the [[2.1.2 Temperature Scales|temperature scale]].

#### *(Thermometer Requirements)* A thermometer reports its own temperature, so the user must let it reach equilibrium with the object.

The reading equals the temperature of the object only after the net energy flow between the two has stopped. A reading taken too early lies between the starting temperature of the thermometer and the temperature of the object. The thermometer should also be small compared with the object. A large cold thermometer removes a noticeable amount of energy from a small cup of tea. The final reading is then correct for the cooled tea and too low for the tea as it was.

#### *(The Diaphragm Model)* Thermal contact means a wall that passes energy and nothing else.

Picture two boxes of gas separated by a thin, massless rubber sheet. No molecule crosses the sheet, so each box keeps its own particles, its own volume, and its own pressure. Molecules on either side do hit the sheet, and a fast molecule on the hot side that strikes it sets it moving toward the cold side, where it kicks a slow molecule. Energy crosses, particles do not. Thermal contact means exactly this. If the two sides start with different average energies per molecule, more energy moves from the hot side than comes back, and the difference shrinks until the two averages match. The wall then vibrates in balance, and the boxes are in thermal equilibrium. In symbols, the pair is isolated and the particle numbers $N_A$ and $N_B$ are fixed, so
$$E_A + E_B = \text{const}.$$
If $T_A > T_B$, energy flows from $A$ to $B$. Then $E_A/N_A$ falls, $T_A$ falls, and $T_B$ rises. If $T_A < T_B$, the flow runs from $B$ to $A$. If $T_A = T_B$, the flow from $A$ to $B$ equals the flow from $B$ to $A$, and nothing changes. A wall that lets nothing through, not even this vibration, is an insulating wall, and two systems separated by one never need to agree on a temperature.

#### *(Dynamic Balance)* Equilibrium stops the net flow of energy while the microscopic exchange continues.

According to the [[2.1.1 Atomic Theory of Matter|atomic theory]], particles at the boundary between two systems keep colliding and trading energy. In equilibrium, the energy passing in one direction equals the energy passing in the other direction on average. The word "net" in the definition of thermal equilibrium refers to this balance. The macroscopic properties stay constant because the two flows cancel.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) One Thermometer, Two Patients
>A nurse measures patient $A$ with a thermometer, waits for a steady reading, and records $37.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$. She then measures patient $B$ with the same thermometer and again records $37.0\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- What does the zeroth law let her conclude, and what does she assume about the thermometer?
>>The thermometer is system $C$. A steady reading means the thermometer has reached thermal equilibrium with the patient. The thermometer was in the same state in both measurements, so $A \sim C$ and $B \sim C$. The zeroth law gives $A \sim B$. The two patients would exchange no net energy if they were in thermal contact, and they have the same temperature. The nurse assumes that equal readings mean equal states of the thermometer. A thermometer with a repeatable property satisfies this assumption.

>[!example] (Easy) Metal Bench and Wooden Bench
>A metal bench and a wooden bench have stood in the same shaded park all night. In the morning the metal bench feels much colder to the hand.
>
>>[!example]- Are the two benches in thermal equilibrium with each other?
>>Yes. Each bench has been in thermal contact with the same air for hours, so each is in equilibrium with the air. The zeroth law then says that the benches are in equilibrium with each other, and their temperatures are equal. The hand is warmer than both benches, so energy leaves the hand in both cases. Metal carries energy away from the skin faster than wood does (see [[2.6.1 Thermal Conduction]]). The skin responds to the rate of energy loss, and the metal therefore feels colder at the same temperature.

>[!example] (Medium) What the Law Does Not Say
>System $A$ is not in thermal equilibrium with system $C$. System $B$ is also not in thermal equilibrium with $C$.
>
>>[!example]- Can we conclude whether $A$ and $B$ are in thermal equilibrium with each other?
>>No. The zeroth law draws a conclusion only from two equilibrium statements. Two cases show that both outcomes are possible. Suppose $A$ and $B$ are both at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $C$ is at $50\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Neither is in equilibrium with $C$, and $A \sim B$ holds. Now suppose $A$ is at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$, $B$ is at $80\ ^\circ\text{C}$, and $C$ is at $50\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Again neither is in equilibrium with $C$, and this time $A \sim B$ fails. We need a direct comparison of $A$ and $B$, or a thermometer reading for each.
