>[!note] Straddle
>A **long straddle** buys a European call and a European put on the same stock. Both have the same strike $K_0$ and the same expiry $T$. The strike is usually chosen at or near the current stock price $S_0$. Let $c$ and $p$ be the two premiums. The payoff at $T$ is
>$$\max(S_T - K_0, 0) + \max(K_0 - S_T, 0) = |S_T - K_0|.$$
>Above $K_0$ the call pays and the put expires worthless. Below $K_0$ the put pays and the call expires worthless. The straddle costs $c + p$ today. Ignoring interest, its profit at $T$ is $|S_T - K_0| - (c + p)$. It has two break-even prices, $K_0 - (c + p)$ and $K_0 + (c + p)$. The maximum loss is the whole premium $c + p$, at $S_T = K_0$. The gain has no upper limit when the stock rises. When the stock falls, the gain is at most $K_0 - (c + p)$, reached at $S_T = 0$. A straddle is a bet on a large move in either direction, and the buyer does not need to know the direction. A [[2.3.2d Strangle|strangle]] moves the put strike down and the call strike up. It costs less than a straddle and needs a larger move to profit.

# Intuition

Some events move a stock by a large amount in a direction nobody can predict, such as an earnings announcement or a court ruling. A call alone profits only from a rise, and a put alone profits only from a fall. Holding both profits from a large move either way. The price is two premiums, and the straddle loses money when the stock ends near $K_0$. To straddle something is to extend across both sides of it, as the call and the put do around $K_0$.

# Properties

#### *(Cost From Parity)* Put-call parity prices a straddle from the call alone, and at the forward price the call and the put cost the same.

Let $S$ be the stock price today, with no dividends before $T$. [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity|Put-call parity]] gives $p = c - S + K_0e^{-rT}$, so the straddle costs
$$c + p = 2c - (S - K_0e^{-rT}).$$
The bracket is the value of a forward contract with delivery price $K_0$. When $K_0 = Se^{rT}$, the forward price, the bracket is zero and $c = p$. A straddle struck at the forward price costs exactly two calls. The same identity builds a straddle from calls alone: buy two calls, sell one share short, and lend $K_0e^{-rT}$. At $T$ this pays $2\max(S_T - K_0, 0) - S_T + K_0 = |S_T - K_0|$.

#### *(Value Before Expiry)* Before expiry a straddle is worth at least $|S - K_0e^{-rT}|$, and the rest is time value that is gone by $T$.

The lower bounds in [[2.3.1b Option Price Bounds]] give $c \ge \max(S - K_0e^{-rT}, 0)$ and $p \ge \max(K_0e^{-rT} - S, 0)$. Adding them gives
$$c + p \ge |S - K_0e^{-rT}|.$$
The right side is the intrinsic value of the pair. When $S = K_0e^{-rT}$ it is zero, and the whole straddle price is time value (the part of the premium above intrinsic value). If the stock stays near $K_0$, the time value shrinks toward zero as $T$ approaches. The holder of an at-the-money straddle therefore loses value as days pass without a move. Held to expiry, the straddle profits only if the final move exceeds the premium.

#### *(Expected Move)* An at-the-money straddle costs about $0.8\,\sigma S\sqrt{T}$, so its price measures the size of the move the market expects.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>This approximation comes from the Black-Scholes-Merton formula and is not derived in the modules.

Let $\sigma$ be the volatility of the stock (the standard deviation of its return per year). Under [[2.3.3b Risk-Neutral Valuation|risk-neutral valuation]], the straddle price is $e^{-rT}\hat{\mathbb{E}}\big[|S_T - K_0|\big]$, the discounted expected size of the move. For a strike at the forward price, the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]] gives approximately
$$c + p \approx \sqrt{2/\pi}\;\sigma S\sqrt{T} \approx 0.8\,\sigma S\sqrt{T}.$$
Here $\sigma\sqrt{T}$ is the standard deviation of the return over the life of the options. The factor $\sqrt{2/\pi}$ is the mean of $|Z|$ for a standard normal $Z$. Take a stock at $\$40$ with $\sigma = 35\%$ and three months to expiry. The approximation gives $\$5.59$, and the exact formula with $K_0 = \$40$ and $r = 4\%$ gives $\$5.56$. The approximation is best when $\sigma\sqrt{T}$ is small and $K_0$ is close to the forward price.

#### *(Short Straddle)* Selling a straddle collects $c + p$ and profits when the stock stays near $K_0$, with no limit on the loss.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules cover the long straddle only.

A **short straddle** writes the call and the put. Its profit at $T$ is $(c + p) - |S_T - K_0|$. The seller profits whenever $S_T$ ends between the two break-even prices and keeps the full premium only at $S_T = K_0$. A large rise creates a loss with no upper limit. Both legs are written options, so the seller needs a margin account. A [[2.3.2e Butterfly Spread|butterfly spread]] takes the same view on a small move and caps the loss.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Profit From a Straddle
>A stock trades at $\$40$. A three-month European call with strike $\$40$ costs $\$2.98$, and the matching put costs $\$2.58$. A trader buys the straddle.
>
>>[!example]- Find the cost, the break-even prices, and the profit at expiry if the stock ends at $\$30$, $\$41$, or $\$50$.
>>The straddle costs $2.98 + 2.58 = \$5.56$. The break-even prices are $40 - 5.56 = \$34.44$ and $40 + 5.56 = \$45.56$.
>>
>>At $S_T = 30$ the put pays $\$10$, and the profit is $10 - 5.56 = \$4.44$. At $S_T = 41$ the call pays $\$1$, and the profit is $1 - 5.56 = -\$4.56$. At $S_T = 50$ the call pays $\$10$, and the profit is again $\$4.44$.

>[!example] (Medium) A Straddle Before Earnings
>A stock trades at $\$50$ and pays no dividends. It reports earnings in two weeks. On its last four reports it moved by about $8\%$ each time. A one-month European call with strike $\$50$ costs $\$2.69$. The risk-free rate is $5\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Price the matching put, find the break-even prices of the straddle, and decide whether it pays if the stock again moves by $8\%$.
>>The discounted strike is $50e^{-0.05/12} = \$49.79$. Parity gives the put price
>>$$p = c - S + Ke^{-rT} = 2.69 - 50 + 49.79 = \$2.48.$$
>>The straddle costs $2.69 + 2.48 = \$5.17$. The break-even prices are $\$44.83$ and $\$55.17$. The stock must move by $5.17/50 = 10.34\%$ in either direction for the trade to break even.
>>
>>A move of $8\%$ takes the stock to $\$54$ or $\$46$. The straddle then pays $\$4.00$, and the profit is $4.00 - 5.17 = -\$1.17$. The straddle loses money on a move of the usual size. The option prices already assume a move larger than $8\%$.

>[!example] (Hard) Buying a Straddle Through Its Synthetic Copy
>A stock trades at $\$40$ and pays no dividends. A three-month European call with strike $\$40$ is quoted at $\$2.98$, and the matching put at $\$2.70$. The risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding. Short selling is allowed.
>
>>[!example]- Find the cheapest way to hold the payoff $|S_T - 40|$, check that the two ways pay the same at expiry, and find the saving.
>>Buying the call and the put directly costs $2.98 + 2.70 = \$5.68$.
>>
>>The synthetic copy buys two calls, sells one share short, and lends $40e^{-0.04 \times 0.25} = \$39.60$. Its cost is
>>$$2 \times 2.98 - 40 + 39.60 = \$5.56.$$
>>At expiry the loan returns $\$40$ and the trader buys back the share at $S_T$. At $S_T = 30$ the calls expire, and the position pays $-30 + 40 = \$10$. At $S_T = 40$ it pays $\$0$. At $S_T = 50$ the calls pay $\$20$, and the position pays $20 - 50 + 40 = \$10$. These match $|S_T - 40|$ in every case, and the general check is in the first property.
>>
>>The synthetic copy saves $5.68 - 5.56 = \$0.12$. The saving equals the gap between the put quote and its parity value, $2.98 - 40 + 39.60 = \$2.58$. The put is $\$0.12$ too expensive. A trader with no view could also sell the put and buy its synthetic copy to lock in the same $\$0.12$.
