>[!note] Typical Sets
>A **typical set** is an exponentially smaller subset of the space of all $n$-length sequences $\mathcal{X}^n$ (where $|\mathcal{X}^n|=2^{n\log_{2}|\mathcal{X}|}$), which captures most of the probability mass of the source as $n$ grows large. Formally, the $\epsilon$-typical set $A_{\epsilon}^{(n)} \subset \mathcal{X}^n$ is the set of $n$-length sequences $X^{n} = X_{1}, \ldots, X_{n}$, such that each sequence has a uniform probability of approximately $2^{\mathbb{E}[\log_{2}p_{X}(X^n)]}=2^{-nH(x)}$:
>$$A_{\epsilon}^{(n)} = \{ X^{n} \in \mathcal{X}^n : 2^{-n(H(X) + \epsilon)} \leq p_{X^n}(x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}) \leq 2^{-n(H(X) - \epsilon)} \},$$
>where $|A_{\epsilon}^{(n)}| \approx 2^{nH(X)}$.
>
>As $n$ grows large, sequences in the (much smaller) typical set $A_{\epsilon}^{(n)}$ contain virtually all the probability mass of $\mathcal{X}^n$. Mathematically, this means that $\lim_{n \to \infty}\mathbb{P}(A_{\epsilon}^{(n)}) = 1$. This property of typical sets is known as **asymptotic equipartition**, and is the LLN-equivalent analogue in information theory. Mathematically,
>$$-\frac{1}{n}\log_{2}p_{X^n}(X_{1}, \ldots, X_{n}) \to H(X) \quad \text{in probability},$$
>meaning that the per-symbol self-information of a sequence converges to the entropy.

# Examples

>[!example] Biased Coin Flips
>You flip $100$ coins with probability $1/4$ of coming up heads.
>>[!example]-  What is the size of the sample space? 
>>We know that each coin can either land on $H$ or $T$. For 100 coins, this becomes $|\Omega| = 2^{100}$.
>
>>[!example]- What is the size of the typical set, and how do the two sizes compare?
>>We can calculate the entropy of each coin as $$H(X) = 0.25 \log 4+0.75\log\frac{4}{3}=0.811 \text{ bits per coinflip.}$$
>>Therefore, the size of the typical set is $\approx 2^{81}$, or $\frac{2^{81}}{2^{100}} = \frac{1}{2^{19}}$ of the size of the sample space.
