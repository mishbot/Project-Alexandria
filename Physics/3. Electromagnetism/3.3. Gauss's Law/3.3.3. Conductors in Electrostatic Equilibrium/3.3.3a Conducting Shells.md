>[!note] Conducting Shells
>A **conducting shell** is a hollow piece of metal. Take a thick spherical shell with inner radius $a$ and outer radius $b$. A point charge $q$ sits at the center of the cavity. The shell itself carries a net charge $Q_s$, which may be zero. The shell's charge sits on its two surfaces:
>- The inner surface carries $Q_{\text{in}} = -q$. A Gaussian surface inside the metal has zero flux, so it encloses zero charge, and $q + Q_{\text{in}} = 0$.
>- The outer surface carries $Q_{\text{out}} = q + Q_s$. This follows from [[3.1.2. Conservation of Charge|conservation of charge]], since $Q_{\text{in}} + Q_{\text{out}} = Q_s$.
>
>A neutral shell has $Q_{\text{in}} = -q$ and $Q_{\text{out}} = +q$. With $q$ at the center, both surface charges spread evenly. [[3.3.2a Spherical Symmetry|Spherical symmetry]] then gives the field in the three regions:
>$$E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} \quad (r < a), \qquad E = 0 \quad (a < r < b), \qquad E = \frac{q + Q_s}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} \quad (r > b).$$
>A positive $E$ points outward. The field jumps at $r = a$ and at $r = b$, where the surface charges sit. Each jump equals $|\sigma|/\varepsilon_0$ for the charge on that surface, as for any [[3.3.2a Spherical Symmetry|charged shell]].
>
>When $q$ sits off center, the totals $Q_{\text{in}} = -q$ and $Q_{\text{out}} = q + Q_s$ stay the same, because neither step used symmetry. Other features change or stay as follows:
>- The inner surface charge becomes uneven, densest on the side nearer $q$.
>- With no other charges nearby, the charge on a spherical outer surface stays even. The field outside is then unchanged.
>- Gauss's law no longer gives the field in the cavity, because the symmetry is broken.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture worked only a neutral shell. It also did not state that the outer surface charge stays even for an off-center charge. Both follow Giancoli chapter 22.

# Intuition

Every field line that leaves $q$ must end on the inner wall, since the metal has no field inside. The lines end on negative charge gathered there. The number of lines ending there equals the number leaving $q$, so the inner wall carries $-q$. A neutral shell that moves $-q$ to its inner wall leaves $+q$ behind. That charge cannot stay inside the metal, so it moves to the outer wall. From outside, the shell then looks like a charge $q$ spread evenly over a sphere.

Moving $q$ toward one side of the cavity shortens the lines on that side. They crowd together there, so more negative charge gathers on that part of the wall. The total on the inner wall stays $-q$.

The animation puts a charge $+q$ in the cavity of a neutral metal shell. The inner wall gathers $-q$ and the outer wall gathers $+q$. Moving the charge off center changes the inner charge but not the outer charge.

# Derivation

Each step uses only two facts, the zero field in the metal and conservation of charge. Draw a closed surface that lies entirely inside the metal and goes around the cavity. Its shape does not matter. The field is zero on it, so Gauss's law reads
$$\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A = 0 = \frac{q + Q_{\text{in}}}{\varepsilon_0},$$
which gives $Q_{\text{in}} = -q$.

The outer charge needs a different tool. A Gaussian surface outside the shell encloses $q + Q_{\text{in}} + Q_{\text{out}}$. The field there is unknown, so the flux is unknown, and the law gives no new equation. Conservation of charge supplies it instead. The shell's own charge is fixed at $Q_s$, so $Q_{\text{out}} = Q_s - Q_{\text{in}} = q + Q_s$.

With $q$ at the center, the whole arrangement is spherically symmetric. A Gaussian sphere of radius $r < a$ encloses only $q$, which gives $E = q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$. A sphere of radius $r > b$ encloses $q + Q_{\text{in}} + Q_{\text{out}} = q + Q_s$.

# Properties

#### *(Uneven Inner Charge)* The inner surface charge is densest on the part of the wall nearest $q$.

The [[3.3.3. Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium|surface rule]] ties the density on the inner wall to the total field right next to it, $|\sigma| = \varepsilon_0 E$. With $q$ at the center, the total field at the inner wall has one value, $q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^2)$, all around. With $q$ near one side, the total field at the near wall is stronger, so the charge there is denser. The far wall gets less. The field in the cavity is the sum of the field of $q$ and the field of this uneven layer.

#### *(Even Outer Charge)* With no other charges nearby, the charge on a spherical outer surface stays even wherever $q$ sits.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not discuss the outer surface for an off-center charge. Giancoli chapter 22 treats charge in a conductor's cavity. The full proof uses a uniqueness theorem beyond that chapter.

The charge $q$ and the inner layer have zero net charge together. The inner layer arranges itself so that the two make zero field in the metal. A uniqueness theorem, stating that conductor charges have a single equilibrium arrangement, shows this field also vanishes beyond the cavity. The outer layer therefore feels no field from inside the shell. With no other charges nearby, it spreads as on a lone metal sphere, evenly. The field outside the shell is $(q + Q_s)/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$ wherever $q$ sits. A charge placed near the shell, or an outer surface that is not a sphere, makes the outer charge uneven.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Charge at the Center
>A neutral metal shell has inner radius $8.0\ \text{cm}$ and outer radius $10\ \text{cm}$. A charge of $+5.0\ \text{nC}$ sits at its center.
>
>>[!example]- Find the charge density on each surface, and the field $4.0$, $9.0$, and $20\ \text{cm}$ from the center.
>>The inner surface carries $-5.0\ \text{nC}$ and the outer surface $+5.0\ \text{nC}$. Their densities are
>>$$\sigma_{\text{in}} = \frac{-5.0 \times 10^{-9}}{4\pi (0.080)^2} = -6.2 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{C/m}^2, \qquad \sigma_{\text{out}} = \frac{5.0 \times 10^{-9}}{4\pi (0.10)^2} = 4.0 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{C/m}^2.$$
>>At $4.0\ \text{cm}$, in the cavity, $E = (8.99 \times 10^{9})(5.0 \times 10^{-9})/(0.040)^2 = 2.8 \times 10^{4}\ \text{N/C}$, outward. At $9.0\ \text{cm}$, in the metal, $E = 0$. At $20\ \text{cm}$, $E = (8.99 \times 10^{9})(5.0 \times 10^{-9})/(0.20)^2 = 1.1 \times 10^{3}\ \text{N/C}$, outward.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture worked only a neutral shell. The next example gives the shell its own net charge, as in Giancoli chapter 22.

>[!example] (Medium) A Charged Shell
>A metal shell with inner radius $10\ \text{cm}$ and outer radius $15\ \text{cm}$ carries a net charge of $+7.0\ \text{nC}$. A charge of $-3.0\ \text{nC}$ sits at its center.
>
>>[!example]- Find the charge on each surface, and the field $5.0\ \text{cm}$ and $25\ \text{cm}$ from the center.
>>The inner surface carries $Q_{\text{in}} = -q = +3.0\ \text{nC}$. The outer surface carries $Q_{\text{out}} = Q_s - Q_{\text{in}} = 7.0 - 3.0 = +4.0\ \text{nC}$. At $5.0\ \text{cm}$ only the central charge is enclosed, so
>>$$E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{-3.0 \times 10^{-9}}{(0.050)^2} = -1.08 \times 10^{4}\ \text{N/C},$$
>>pointing inward. At $25\ \text{cm}$ the enclosed charge is $-3.0 + 3.0 + 4.0 = +4.0\ \text{nC}$, so
>>$$E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{4.0 \times 10^{-9}}{(0.25)^2} = 575\ \text{N/C},$$
>>pointing outward. Outside, the field is that of the shell's net charge plus the central charge.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The next example uses a shell with its own net charge of $-10\ \text{nC}$. It also uses the even outer charge for an off-center charge. Lecture covered neither.

>[!example] (Hard) An Off-Center Charge
>A metal shell has inner radius $6.0\ \text{cm}$, outer radius $9.0\ \text{cm}$, and net charge $-10\ \text{nC}$. A charge of $+4.0\ \text{nC}$ sits in the cavity, $2.0\ \text{cm}$ from the center. No other charges are nearby.
>
>>[!example]- Find the charge on each surface and the field $30\ \text{cm}$ from the center. Is the field $1.0\ \text{cm}$ from the charge, inside the cavity, equal to $q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$?
>>The inner surface carries $-4.0\ \text{nC}$, densest on the side nearer the charge. The outer surface carries $-10 - (-4.0) = -6.0\ \text{nC}$, spread evenly. Outside the shell, the field is that of $-6.0\ \text{nC}$ at the center:
>>$$E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{-6.0 \times 10^{-9}}{(0.30)^2} = -599\ \text{N/C},$$
>>pointing toward the center. Inside the cavity, the field of $q$ adds to the field of the uneven inner layer. The field of $q$ alone is $q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2) = 3.6 \times 10^{5}\ \text{N/C}$ at $1.0\ \text{cm}$. The inner layer adds a field that Gauss's law cannot find, so the answer is no. Close to $q$, its own field is much larger than the layer's. The formula is a fair approximation there.
