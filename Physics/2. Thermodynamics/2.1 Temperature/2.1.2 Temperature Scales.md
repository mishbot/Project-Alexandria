>[!note] Temperature Scales
>**Temperature** is the quantity a thermometer measures. It tells how hot or cold an object is. A **temperature scale** assigns a number to each temperature. To build a scale, we choose two reproducible reference temperatures, called fixed points. We give each fixed point a number and divide the interval between them into equal steps.
>
>The **Celsius scale** sets the freezing point of water at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and the boiling point at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Both values refer to a pressure of one atmosphere. The **Fahrenheit scale** sets the same two points at $32\ ^\circ\text{F}$ and $212\ ^\circ\text{F}$. The $100$ Celsius degrees between the fixed points span $180$ Fahrenheit degrees, so one Celsius degree equals $\tfrac{9}{5}$ Fahrenheit degrees. The two readings of one temperature are related by
>$$T_F = \tfrac{9}{5}\,T_C + 32, \qquad T_C = \tfrac{5}{9}\,(T_F - 32),$$
>where $T_C$ is the reading in degrees Celsius and $T_F$ is the reading in degrees Fahrenheit.
>
>The **Kelvin scale** is the SI scale, and the gas laws and the laws of thermodynamics use it. It keeps the size of the Celsius degree and places its zero at [[2.2.1 Absolute Temperature|absolute zero]], the lowest possible temperature. A Kelvin reading follows from a Celsius reading by a shift,
>$$T = T_C + 273.15,$$
>where $T$ is in kelvins, written $\text{K}$ with no degree sign. The table lists four reference temperatures on all three scales.
>
>| Temperature | Celsius | Fahrenheit | Kelvin |
>|---|---|---|---|
>| Water boils (1 atm) | $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ | $212\ ^\circ\text{F}$ | $373.15\ \text{K}$ |
>| Water freezes (1 atm) | $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ | $32\ ^\circ\text{F}$ | $273.15\ \text{K}$ |
>| Celsius and Fahrenheit readings agree | $-40\ ^\circ\text{C}$ | $-40\ ^\circ\text{F}$ | $233.15\ \text{K}$ |
>| Absolute zero | $-273.15\ ^\circ\text{C}$ | $-459.67\ ^\circ\text{F}$ | $0\ \text{K}$ |
>
>A **thermometer** is any device with a property that changes with temperature in a repeatable way. Liquid-in-glass thermometers use the [[2.1.4 Thermal Expansion|thermal expansion]] of mercury or alcohol. Other thermometers use the bending of a bimetallic strip or the electrical [[3.6.3. Resistivity|resistance]] of a metal. The constant-volume gas thermometer uses the pressure of a gas held at a fixed volume.

# Intuition

The sense of touch is a poor thermometer. A metal railing feels colder than a wooden bench at the same temperature. The skin responds to how fast energy leaves the hand, and metal removes energy faster. Physics needs an instrument that gives the same number to anyone who uses it. A thermometer supplies that number, and a scale says what the number means.

Every scale rests on two free choices. The first choice is where to put the zero. The second choice is how large one degree is. Celsius and Kelvin use the same degree and different zeros, so the conversion between them is a shift. Fahrenheit differs from Celsius in both choices. The conversion therefore needs a multiplication for the degree size and an addition for the zero. The diagram places the three scales side by side, with equal temperatures at equal heights.

# Properties

#### *(Temperature Differences)* A temperature difference converts with the scale factor alone.

Subtracting two readings cancels the additive constant in each conversion formula. A difference therefore obeys $\Delta T_F = \tfrac{9}{5}\,\Delta T_C$ and $\Delta T = \Delta T_C$. A rise of $10$ Celsius degrees equals a rise of $18$ Fahrenheit degrees and a rise of $10\ \text{K}$. Applying the full formula to a difference gives $50\ ^\circ\text{F}$, which is a common error. Some textbooks write a difference as $\text{C}^\circ$ and a reading as $^\circ\text{C}$ to keep the two apart. Formulas for [[2.1.4 Thermal Expansion|thermal expansion]] and [[2.4.3 Specific Heat|specific heat]] contain $\Delta T$, so they accept Celsius degrees or kelvins without change.

#### *(Kelvin for Ratios)* Any formula that multiplies or divides by a temperature needs the Kelvin reading.

Only the Kelvin scale puts its zero at the lowest possible temperature, so only Kelvin readings form meaningful ratios. An object at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is not twice as hot as an object at $10\ ^\circ\text{C}$. In kelvins the ratio is $293.15 / 283.15 \approx 1.04$. The [[2.2.3 Ideal Gas Law|ideal gas law]] and the efficiency of a [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot engine]] contain $T$ itself, and both give wrong results with Celsius readings.

#### *(Calibration)* A thermometer reading comes from linear interpolation between the two fixed points, and this is the operational definition of temperature.

Let $X$ be the measured property, such as the length of a mercury column. Let $X_0$ and $X_{100}$ be its values at the freezing and boiling points of water. The Celsius reading for a measured value $X$ is
$$T_C = 100 \times \frac{X - X_0}{X_{100} - X_0},$$
so the reading is the fraction of the way from $X_0$ to $X_{100}$, scaled to $100$ degrees. The formula assumes that $X$ changes by equal amounts for equal temperature changes. To calibrate, put the device in an ice bath and mark $0$. Then put it in boiling water, mark $100$, and divide the space between into equal steps. The sketch shows the two marks. Thermometers built on different properties agree at the fixed points and can differ by a few tenths of a degree between them. The constant-volume gas thermometer settles the disagreement, because all gases at low density read the same, and it defines the [[2.2.1 Absolute Temperature|absolute temperature]] scale.

# Examples

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not use Fahrenheit conversions. This example follows Giancoli section 17.2.

>[!example] (Easy) A Reading and a Difference
>A weather report gives a morning low of $59\ ^\circ\text{F}$ and an afternoon high of $86\ ^\circ\text{F}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find both temperatures in degrees Celsius and in kelvins. Then find the rise during the day on all three scales.
>>The conversion formula gives the two Celsius readings,
>>$$T_C = \tfrac{5}{9}(59 - 32) = 15\ ^\circ\text{C}, \qquad T_C = \tfrac{5}{9}(86 - 32) = 30\ ^\circ\text{C}.$$
>>Adding $273.15$ gives $288.15\ \text{K}$ and $303.15\ \text{K}$. The rise is $86 - 59 = 27$ Fahrenheit degrees. A difference converts with the factor alone, so the rise is $\tfrac{5}{9} \times 27 = 15$ Celsius degrees, which is also $15\ \text{K}$. Subtracting the converted readings gives the same result, $30 - 15 = 15$.

>[!example] (Medium) An Unmarked Thermometer
>The mercury column of an unmarked thermometer is $4.0\ \text{cm}$ long in melting ice and $24.0\ \text{cm}$ long in boiling water at one atmosphere. In a cup of tea the column is $16.5\ \text{cm}$ long.
>
>>[!example]- What is the temperature of the tea?
>>The column grows by $24.0 - 4.0 = 20.0\ \text{cm}$ over $100$ Celsius degrees. In the tea it has grown by $16.5 - 4.0 = 12.5\ \text{cm}$ from its length at the freezing point. Linear interpolation gives
>>$$T_C = 100 \times \frac{12.5}{20.0} = 62.5\ ^\circ\text{C}.$$
>>The result assumes that the mercury column lengthens by the same amount for every degree.
