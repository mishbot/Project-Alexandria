>[!note] Expectation (LOTUS)
>The **expectation** of a random variable represents its probability-weighted average value. The generalized expectation function can be given by the **Law of the Unconscious Statistician (LOTUS)**:
>- The expectation of a discrete random variable $X=g(Y)$ with a range $\mathcal{X}$, where $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, is generally defined as,
>$$\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum^{}_{x \in \mathcal{X}}x \cdot p_{X}(x)=\sum^{}_{x \in \mathcal{X}}g(y) \cdot p_{X}(g(y)).$$
>
>- Similarly, the expectation of a continuous random variable $X=g(Y)$ is defined as
>$$\mathbb{E}(X)=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}x \cdot f_{X}(x)dx=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}g(y) \cdot f_{X}(x)dy.$$
# Intuition
The expectation can be seen as the average or mean of a probability distribution. It is the most expected value given all probabilities.

# Properties
#### (*Linearity*) Any $X,Y$ satisfy $\mathbb{E}[\alpha X+\beta Y+\gamma]=\alpha \mathbb{E}(X)+\beta \mathbb{E}(Y)+\gamma.$
The linearity of expectation can be proved by taking $\mathbb{P}[X=a]=\sum^{}_{\upomega \in \Upomega}\mathbb{P}[\upomega]$ (sum to one rule). Then taking the definition of a random variable, we get:
$$
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}[X] &= \sum^{}_{a \in \mathscr{A}}a \times \mathbb{P}[X=a] \\ 
&= \sum^{}_{\upomega \in \Upomega} X(\upomega) \times \mathbb{P}[\upomega]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$$
Then, for random variables $X$ and $Y$,
$$
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}[X+Y] &= \sum^{}_{\upomega \in \Upomega}(X+Y)(\upomega) \times \mathbb{P}[\upomega] \\ 
&= \sum^{}_{\upomega \in \Upomega} (X(\upomega)+Y(\upomega)) \times \mathbb{P}[\upomega] \\
&= \sum^{}_{\upomega \in \Upomega}X(\upomega) \times \mathbb{P}[\upomega] + \sum^{}_{\upomega \in \Upomega}Y(\upomega) \times \mathbb{P}[\upomega] \\
&= \mathbb{E}[X]+\mathbb{E}[Y]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$$
Why does the probability $\mathbb{P}[\upomega]$ not change with the amount of variables? Well, because we simplify each event $X=a$ into $\upomega \in \Upomega$. All of the above stems from this simple fact, since by the definition of the random variable, $\set{\upomega \in \Upomega : X(\upomega) = a}$ and $\set{(a,\mathbb{P}[X=a]):a \in \mathscr{A}}$. From there, we replace $a$ with $X(\upomega)$, and therefore $\mathbb{P}[X=a]$ with $\mathbb{P}[\upomega]$.
#### (*Tail Formula*) Any $X \geq 0$ satisfies $\mathbb{E}[X]=\begin{dcases} \sum^{\infty}_{k=1}\mathbb{P}[X \geq k], \\ \int^{\infty}_{0}\mathbb{P}(X \geq k)dk.\end{dcases}$
For a random variable $X$ that takes values $i \in \set{0, 1, 2,...}$, each with probability $p_i$,
$$
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}[X] &= (0 \times p_0)+(1 \times p_1)+(2 \times p_2)+...+((i-1) \times p_{i-1})+(i \times p_i) \\
&= (p_1+p_2+...+p_{i-1}+p_i)+(p_2+...+p_{i-1}+p_i)+...+(p_{i-1}+p_i)+p_i \\
&= \mathbb{P}[X \geq 1]+\mathbb{P}[X \geq 2]+...+\mathbb{P}[X \geq i-1]+\mathbb{P}[X \geq i] \\
&= \sum^{\infty}_{i=1}\mathbb{P}[X \geq i].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$$


#### (*Independence*) If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, then $\mathbb{E}[XY]= \mathbb{E}[X]\cdot \mathbb{E}[Y]$.
#### (*Conditionality*) $\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y=y] = \begin{dcases}\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}}x \cdot p_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y), \\ \int^{\infty}_{-\infty}x \cdot f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y)dx.\end{dcases}$
#### (*Iterated/Total Expectation*)  $\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]]=\begin{dcases}\sum\limits_{y \in \mathcal{Y}}p_{Y}(y)\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y=y], \\ \int^{\infty}_{-\infty}f_{Y}(y)\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y]dy.\end{dcases}$

#### (*Wald's Identity*) If $X=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}$, then $\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}] \cdot \mathbb{E}[Y]$.

#### (*Jensen's Inequality*) Any convex $\varphi: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfies $\mathscr{\varphi}(\mathbb{E}[X]) \leq \mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]$.

# Examples
>[!example] (Expectation, Indicator Variables) Connecting Ropes
>You have $n$ identical lengths of rope, each with two loose ends. You toss all ropes into a bag, swirl them around, then–without looking–pull out two ends and tie them together.
>>[!example]- What is the probability that the two ends you just tied belong to the _same_ rope?
>>Before grabbing any rope, we have $2n$ possible ends to grab. After grabbing the first end, we grab another one of the $2n-1$ ends. This gives us $2n(2n-1)$ possible outcomes for grabbing two random ends.
>>
>>Consider the possibility of grabbing the same rope. Before grabbing any end, we can blindly pick any one of the $2n$ ropes. However, after we grab our first end, there is exactly $1$ way of grabbing the other end of the same rope. Therefore, there are exactly $2n$ ways of grabbing the same rope. This makes intuitive sense: you can grab either ends of the same rope in the beginning, and then grab the second end. Therefore, we have:
>>$$\mathbb{P}(\text{Same rope in first attempt})=\frac{2n}{2n(2n-1)}=\frac{1}{2n-1}.$$
>
>>[!example]- If you continue this process until all ends are connected, what is the expected total number of times you ever tie the two ends of the same rope together?
>>Consider the following indicator variable:
>>$$I_{i}=\begin{cases}
1 & \text{the two ends of rope } i \text{ are paired}, \\
0 & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}$$
>>where $\mathbb{P}(I_{i}=1)=\dfrac{1}{2n-1}$ for all $i$. Then, $N=\sum^{n}_{i=1}I_{i}$ is the number of self-loops after all ends have been tied. As such,
>>$$\mathbb{E}(N)=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\mathbb{E}(I_{i})=\frac{n}{2n-1}.$$
>
>>[!example]- What is the expected number of total loops after there are no loose ends left?
>>We start with $2n$ loose ends and immediately tie 2 ends, yielding $2n-1$ loose ends. We proceed until there are no loose ends left. Let $\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]$ represent the number of total loops given $n$ ropes.
>>

>[!example] (Iterated Expectation) Dice Game
>You roll a dice. For each roll, you are paid the face value. If a roll gives 4, 5, or 6, you can roll the dice again. Once you get 1, 2, or 3, the game stops.
>>[!example]- What is the expected payoff of this game?
>>Let $X_{n}$ be your cumulative expected payoff in round $n$, and let $C_{i-1} \sim \text{Ber}(1/2)$ be an indicator variable showing the outcome of your last throw (i.e., whether you rolled 4, 5, 6 or not). Then,
>>$$\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}(X)&=\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{E}(X \mid C)) \\
&=\frac{1}{2} \cdot \mathbb{E}(X \mid C=0) + \frac{1}{2} \cdot \mathbb{E}(X \mid C=1) \\
&= \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1+2+3}{3}+\frac{1}{2} \cdot \left(\frac{4+5+6}{3}+\mathbb{E}(X)\right) \\
&= 7.
\end{align}$$
>> We add $\mathbb{E}(X)$ to our payoff on the right hand side, since you continue playing if you roll 4, 5, or 6 for an indefinite amount of time.

>[!example] Ace Hunt
>Ace Hunt is a solo “table game” played with a freshly shuffled 52-card deck placed face-down in the center. You (the dealer) turn over cards one at a time into a single community pile, pausing only when the very first Ace appears; at that moment the hand ends and the total number of cards revealed is recorded. The person whose guess is closest to the recorded number of total cards wins.
>>[!example]- What is the probability that you will need to turn over exactly $k$ cards before revealing the first Ace?
>>Let $X_{n}$ denote the even that the $n$-th card turned over is an Ace. Then, 
>>$$\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k+1} \;\middle|\; \bigcap_{i=1}^{k} X_{i} = 0\right)=\frac{4}{52-k}.$$
>
>>[!example]- What is the expected number of cards you must expose to see the first Ace?
>>Denote $X$ as the number of cards turned over before the first ace. Then, $X=1+\sum^{48}_{i=1}X_{i}$ (since there are at most 48 non-Ace cards drawn before the first ace, where
>>$$X_{i} = \begin{cases}
1 & \text{i-th card is not an Ace} \\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}$$


>[!example] Coupon Collector
>Let there be $n$ distinct coupon types, labeled $1,2,\dots,n$.  You collect coupons from cereal boxes one at a time; on each trial you receive exactly one coupon, and each type is always equally likely.
>>[!example]- If you want to collect a complete set of coupons with at least one of each type, how many boxes on average are needed to complete the set?
>>Let $X_{i}$ represent the number of additional (marginal) draws needed to collect the $i$-th coupon type after $i-1$ types have been collected. Then, $X=\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}$ is the total number of coupons needed for all $n$ coupon types to be collected.
>>
>>Given that $i-1$ coupons are collected, a new coupon will be a different type with probability $1-\frac{i-1}{n}$. Since each $X_{i}$ is a first-success measure, $X_{i} \sim \text{Geom}\left( \dfrac{n-i+1}{n} \right)$. As such,
>>$$\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}(X) &= \sum^{n}_{i=1} \frac{1}{p} \\
&= \sum^{n}_{i=1} \frac{n}{n-i+1} \\
&= n\left( \frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n-1}+\ldots+\frac{1}{2}+1 \right) \\
&=nH_{n}
\end{align}$$
>
>>[!example]- If you have collected $k$ coupons, what is the expected number of distinct coupon types?
>>Let $X=\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}$ represent the present coupon types, such that $X_{i}$ indicates whether the $i$-th coupon type is present. The probability of a coupon type that is NOT the $i$-th type is exactly $\dfrac{n-1}{n}$, and therefore $\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=0)=\left(\dfrac{n-1}{n}\right)^k$ indicates that in the sequence of $k$ draws, the $i$-th coupon does not appear once. We can express $X$ as a sum of $X_{i} \sim \text{Ber}\left(1-\left[\dfrac{n-1}{n}\right]^k\right)$, such that
>>$$\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}(X) &= \sum^{n}_{i=1}\mathbb{E}(X_{i}) \\
&=\sum^{n}_{i=1} \left(1-\left[\dfrac{n-1}{n}\right]^k\right) \\
&= n\left(1-\left[\dfrac{n-1}{n}\right]^k\right).
\end{align}$$
