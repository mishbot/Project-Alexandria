>[!note] Student's t Distribution
>A continuous random variable $T \in \mathbb{R}$ follows the **Student's** $t$ **distribution** with $\nu > 0$ degrees of freedom, written $T \sim t_\nu$, if it can be expressed as
>$$T=\frac{Z}{\sqrt{U/\nu}},$$
>where $Z\sim \mathcal N(0,1)$ is [[2.2c.3 Gaussian Distribution|normally distributed]], $U\sim \mathcal{X}^2_\nu$ is [[2.2c.7 Chi-Square Distribution|Chi-Square distributed]], and $Z\perp U$.
>
>Equivalently, $T$ is the distribution of a standardized normal random variable when the variance is estimated from data rather than known. For small $\nu$, $t_\nu$ has heavier tails than $\mathcal N(0,1)$, such that as $\nu\to\infty$, $t_\nu \to \mathcal N(0,1)$.
>
>Its [[2.2c.1 Probability Density Function|probability density function]] is
>$$f_T(t)=\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)}\left(1+\frac{t^2}{\nu}\right)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}},\qquad t\in\mathbb R.$$
>
>The cumulative distribution function of $t_\nu$ has no closed-form expression in general, but can be written in terms of the regularized incomplete [[2.2c.9 Beta Distribution|Beta function]] $I_x(a,b)$ as
>$$F_T(t) = 1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\,I_{\frac{\nu}{\nu+t^2}}\!\left(\tfrac{\nu}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\right).$$
>
>A key characteristic of $t_\nu$ is **robustness to outliers** relative to the normal due to heavier tails, especially when $\nu$ is small. The tails decay polynomially as $|t|^{-(\nu+1)}$, in contrast to the exponential decay of the Gaussian.
>
>Special cases:
>- If $\nu = 1$, then $T$ follows the **Cauchy distribution**, which has no finite mean or variance.
>- If $\nu \to \infty$, then $t_\nu \to \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, and in practice $t_\nu$ is nearly indistinguishable from the standard normal for $\nu \gtrsim 30$.
>
>>[!warning] Standard t-statistic
>>If $X_1,\dots,X_n \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ with unknown $\sigma^2$, then
>>$$\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1},$$
>>where $S^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar X)^2$. This is the foundation of the **one-sample t-test** and the construction of **confidence intervals** for the mean when the population variance is unknown.

# Intuition
The Student's $t$ distribution arises from a simple question: what happens when you standardize a normal random variable but have to **estimate the standard deviation** from data? If $\sigma$ were known, the standardized variable $\frac{\bar X - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}$ would be exactly $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$. But replacing $\sigma$ with the sample standard deviation $S$ introduces extra randomness in the denominator, which fattens the tails—extreme values become more probable because $S$ can be unusually small.

With few observations ($\nu$ small), $S$ is a noisy estimate and the tails are substantially heavier. As $\nu$ grows, $S$ becomes a reliable estimate of $\sigma$, and $t_\nu$ collapses to $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$. This is why the $t$-distribution is central to inference with **small samples**: it correctly accounts for the uncertainty in estimating the spread.

# Properties
#### (*Mean*) $\mathbb{E}[T \sim t_\nu] = 0$ for $\nu > 1$. (Undefined for $\nu \leq 1$.)
#### (*Variance*) $\text{Var}[T \sim t_\nu] = \dfrac{\nu}{\nu - 2}$ for $\nu > 2$. (Infinite for $1 < \nu \leq 2$; undefined for $\nu \leq 1$.)
As $\nu$ grows, the variance decreases toward $1$, recovering the standard normal variance. For small $\nu$, the inflated variance reflects the additional uncertainty from estimating $\sigma^2$.

#### (*Symmetry*) The density $f_T(t)$ is symmetric about $0$, so $T$ and $-T$ are identically distributed and all odd moments vanish (when they exist).
#### (*Kurtosis*) $\text{Kurt}[T \sim t_\nu] = \dfrac{6}{\nu - 4}$ for $\nu > 4$. (Infinite for $2 < \nu \leq 4$.)
The excess kurtosis is always positive (leptokurtic), confirming heavier tails than the Gaussian. As $\nu \to \infty$, the kurtosis tends to $0$.

#### (*Scaling*) If $T \sim t_\nu$, then $T^2 \sim F(1, \nu)$, where $F(1,\nu)$ is the F-distribution with $1$ and $\nu$ degrees of freedom.
#### (*Sum*) The sum of independent $t$-distributed random variables does **not** follow a $t$-distribution. This is unlike the [[2.2c.3 Gaussian Distribution|Gaussian]], which is closed under addition.
#### (*Gaussian Limit*) For any fixed $t$, $f_{t_\nu}(t) \to f_{\mathcal{N}(0,1)}(t)$ as $\nu \to \infty$, since $U/\nu \xrightarrow{p} 1$ by the [[3.2 Laws of Large Numbers|law of large numbers]], so $T = Z/\sqrt{U/\nu} \xrightarrow{d} Z$.

# Examples
>[!example] Confidence Interval for the Mean
>A researcher measures the reaction time (in ms) of $n = 8$ participants: $\{312, 298, 325, 340, 305, 290, 318, 330\}$. Assume the reaction times are normally distributed with unknown mean $\mu$ and unknown variance $\sigma^2$.
>>[!example]- Construct a $95\%$ confidence interval for $\mu$.
>>First, compute the sample statistics:
>>$$\bar X = \frac{1}{8}\sum_{i=1}^{8}X_i = 314.75, \qquad S^2 = \frac{1}{7}\sum_{i=1}^{8}(X_i - \bar X)^2 = 295.07, \qquad S \approx 17.18.$$
>>Since $\sigma$ is unknown, we use the $t$-distribution with $\nu = n - 1 = 7$ degrees of freedom. The $95\%$ confidence interval is
>>$$\bar X \pm t_{7,\,0.025}\cdot\frac{S}{\sqrt{n}} = 314.75 \pm 2.365 \cdot \frac{17.18}{\sqrt{8}} = 314.75 \pm 14.37,$$
>>giving the interval $[300.38,\; 329.12]$. Had we incorrectly used $z_{0.025} = 1.96$ (the normal critical value), the interval would have been $[302.84, 326.66]$—narrower, and thus overly confident for such a small sample.

>[!example] One-Sample t-Test
>A coffee machine is calibrated to dispense $\mu_0 = 250$ mL per cup. A quality inspector draws $n = 12$ cups and measures a sample mean of $\bar X = 243$ mL with sample standard deviation $S = 10$ mL.
>>[!example]- At significance level $\alpha = 0.05$, is there evidence that the machine is under-dispensing?
>>We test $H_0: \mu = 250$ against $H_1: \mu < 250$ (one-sided). The test statistic is
>>$$t = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{S/\sqrt{n}} = \frac{243 - 250}{10/\sqrt{12}} = \frac{-7}{2.887} \approx -2.425.$$
>>Under $H_0$, $t \sim t_{11}$. The critical value for a one-sided test at $\alpha = 0.05$ with $11$ degrees of freedom is $t_{11,\,0.05} \approx -1.796$. Since $-2.425 < -1.796$, we reject $H_0$. There is statistically significant evidence at the $5\%$ level that the machine is dispensing less than $250$ mL.
