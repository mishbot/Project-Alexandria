>[!note] Electric Dipole Potential
>An [[3.2.6. Electric Dipole|electric dipole]] has $+q$ at $z = +d/2$ and $-q$ at $z = -d/2$. Its dipole moment $\vec p = q\vec d$ points along $+z$, with size $p = qd$. A point P sits a distance $r$ from the center, at an angle $\theta$ from $\vec p$. The law of cosines gives its distances to the two charges:
>$$r_\pm = \sqrt{r^2 + \frac{d^2}{4} \mp r d\cos\theta}.$$
>Here $r_+$ is the distance to $+q$ and $r_-$ the distance to $-q$. With the zero at infinity, the two source charges make the exact potential
>$$V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{r_+} - \frac{1}{r_-}\right).$$
>Far from the dipole, $r \gg d$, it becomes
>$$V \approx \frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r^2} = \frac{\vec p\cdot\hat r}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r^2},$$
>where $\hat r$ is the unit vector from the center toward P. Its features:
>- $V > 0$ on the side of $+q$, where $\theta < 90^\circ$, and $V < 0$ on the side of $-q$.
>- $V = 0$ everywhere on the bisecting plane, $\theta = 90^\circ$, at every distance.
>- $V$ falls as $1/r^2$, one power faster than a point charge, because the net charge is zero.
>
>The far-field form uses the **binomial approximation** from the [[6.3a Binomial Theorem|binomial theorem]]. For any power $n$ and any $|u| \ll 1$,
>$$(1 + u)^n \approx 1 + nu.$$
>The approximation must keep every term of first order in $d/r$. Dropping both $d^2/4$ and $rd\cos\theta$ next to $r^2$ makes $r_+ = r_-$. That gives $V = 0$, which is wrong.

# Intuition

Far from the dipole, the separation $d$ is small compared with the distance $r$. The two potentials nearly cancel, because one is positive and the other negative. What remains comes from the small difference in their distances to P. That difference is about $d\cos\theta$, the length of the dipole projected onto the line toward P. On the bisecting plane, P is equally far from both charges, and the cancellation is exact.

The potential is positive on the side of the positive charge, since that charge is closer. It is small, because the cancellation is nearly complete. It falls as $1/r^2$, faster than the $1/r$ of a single charge. The dipole's field falls faster again, as $1/r^3$, as found on the bisector in [[3.2.6. Electric Dipole]].

Point P moves around the dipole at a fixed distance $r = 2d$. The graph traces the exact $V$ against $\theta$. A dashed curve shows the far-field form $p\cos\theta/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$ for comparison.

# Derivation

Take the triangle formed by the center, $+q$, and P. Two of its sides are $r$ and $d/2$, with the angle $\theta$ between them. The third side is $r_+$, and the law of cosines gives
$$r_+^2 = r^2 + \left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^2 - 2r\,\frac{d}{2}\cos\theta = r^2 + \frac{d^2}{4} - rd\cos\theta.$$
For $-q$, the angle at the center is $\pi - \theta$, and $\cos(\pi - \theta) = -\cos\theta$. That flips the sign of the last term in $r_-^2$. For $\theta$ between $0$ and $\pi/2$, the result has $r_- > r_+$, as a sketch requires.

Far away, drop $d^2/4$, which is second order in $d$. Keep $rd\cos\theta$, which is first order. Factoring out $r^2$ gives
$$\frac{1}{r_\pm} \approx \frac{1}{r}\left(1 \mp \frac{d}{r}\cos\theta\right)^{-1/2}.$$
Here $u = \mp (d/r)\cos\theta$ and $n = -1/2$. Since $|\cos\theta| \le 1$ and $d \ll r$, the size of $u$ is much less than $1$. The binomial approximation then gives
$$\frac{1}{r_\pm} \approx \frac{1}{r}\left(1 \pm \frac{d}{2r}\cos\theta\right).$$
In the difference $1/r_+ - 1/r_-$, the terms $1/r$ cancel and the two corrections add:
$$V \approx \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{1}{r}\cdot\frac{d}{r}\cos\theta = \frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r^2}.$$

# Properties

#### *(Keep the First Order)* The leading terms cancel, so the whole answer comes from the first-order correction.

Each point-charge potential is about $q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$. Their difference is smaller by a factor of about $d/r$. Keeping only the leading term of each loses the difference completely. The rule is to keep terms up to the first order in $d/r$, since that order survives the cancellation. The $d^2/4$ term is second order. Dropping it changes the result by a fraction of order $(d/r)^2$.

#### *(How Far Is Far)* On the axis, at three times the separation, the far-field formula is already within $3\%$.

On the axis, the exact potential is
$$V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{r - d/2} - \frac{1}{r + d/2}\right) = \frac{qd}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,(r^2 - d^2/4)}.$$
The far-field formula drops $d^2/4$ from the denominator. At $r = 3d$ it comes out $1/36 = 2.8\%$ low. At $r = 10d$ it is $1/400 = 0.25\%$ low. Off the axis the error changes sign. At $r = 3d$ the formula is $2.5\%$ high at $60^\circ$ and $4.0\%$ high at $80^\circ$. Close to the bisector it is about $4.2\%$ high, although both values there are near zero. A molecule has $d$ below a nanometer, so the formula holds a few nanometers away.

#### *(Any Orientation)* The form $\vec p\cdot\hat r$ works for a dipole pointing in any direction.

The angle $\theta$ is always measured from $\vec p$ to the line from the center to P. When $\vec p$ does not lie along an axis, the dot product finds $p\cos\theta$ from components. With P at position $\vec r$ from the dipole's center, $V \approx \vec p\cdot\vec r/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3)$. This needs only $\vec p$ and $\vec r$ in components, with no angle drawn.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Potential Around a Small Dipole
>Charges of $\pm 3.0\ \text{nC}$ sit $1.0\ \text{mm}$ apart.
>
>>[!example]- Find the potential $10\ \text{cm}$ from the center at $\theta = 0^\circ$, $90^\circ$, and $120^\circ$.
>>The dipole moment is $p = (3.0 \times 10^{-9})(1.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 3.0 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{C}\cdot\text{m}$. Here $r = 100d$, so the far-field formula applies. At $\theta = 0^\circ$,
>>$$V = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(3.0 \times 10^{-12})\cos 0^\circ}{(0.10)^2} = 2.7\ \text{V}.$$
>>At $\theta = 90^\circ$, $\cos\theta = 0$, so $V = 0$. At $\theta = 120^\circ$, $\cos\theta = -0.5$, so $V = -1.3\ \text{V}$.

>[!example] (Medium) Exact Versus Approximate
>Charges of $\pm 5.0\ \text{nC}$ sit $2.0\ \text{cm}$ apart. Point P is $6.0\ \text{cm}$ from the center, at $\theta = 60^\circ$.
>
>>[!example]- Compare the exact potential at P with the far-field formula.
>>The law of cosines gives the two distances:
>>$$r_+ = \sqrt{0.0036 + 0.0001 - 0.0006} = 0.0557\ \text{m}, \qquad r_- = \sqrt{0.0036 + 0.0001 + 0.0006} = 0.0656\ \text{m}.$$
>>With $q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 44.95\ \text{V}\cdot\text{m}$, the exact potential is
>>$$V = 44.95\,(17.96 - 15.25) = 122\ \text{V}.$$
>>The far-field formula, with $p = 1.0 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{C}\cdot\text{m}$, gives
>>$$V \approx \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(1.0 \times 10^{-10})(0.50)}{(0.060)^2} = 125\ \text{V}.$$
>>At three times the separation, the approximation is $2.5\%$ high.

>[!example] (Hard) An Ion Beside a Water Molecule
>A water molecule has $p = 6.2 \times 10^{-30}\ \text{C}\cdot\text{m}$. A sodium ion of charge $+e$ sits $0.50\ \text{nm}$ from the molecule's center, on the dipole axis. A molecule at temperature $T$ has a random thermal energy of order $k_B T$, where $k_B = 1.381 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}$ is the Boltzmann constant. At $T = 300\ \text{K}$, $k_B T = 4.14 \times 10^{-21}\ \text{J}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the ion's potential energy when the molecule's negative end faces it. Compare the result with $k_B T$.
>>Charges of $\pm e$ with this moment would sit $p/e = 0.039\ \text{nm}$ apart. The ion is $13$ times farther away, so the far-field formula applies. With the negative end toward the ion, the ion sits at $\theta = 180^\circ$:
>>$$V = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(6.2 \times 10^{-30})\cos 180^\circ}{(0.50 \times 10^{-9})^2} = -0.223\ \text{V}.$$
>>The ion's potential energy is
>>$$U = eV = (1.602 \times 10^{-19})(-0.223) = -3.6 \times 10^{-20}\ \text{J}.$$
>>This is about $9$ times $k_B T$. With the molecule turned the other way, $U = +3.6 \times 10^{-20}\ \text{J}$. Turning it around costs $7.1 \times 10^{-20}\ \text{J}$, about $17\,k_B T$. Thermal collisions rarely supply that much, so nearby water molecules keep their negative ends toward the ion.
