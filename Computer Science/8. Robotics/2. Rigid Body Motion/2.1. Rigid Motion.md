> [!note] Rigid Motion
> A **rigid body motion** in three-dimensional space is a transformation that preserves distances and angles — a rotation followed by a translation. The collection of all such transformations forms the **Special Euclidean Group** $SE(3)$, whose elements are pairs $(R,p)$ with $R \in SO(3)$ ([[1.2. Special Orthonormal Groups|special orthonormal group]]) and $p \in \mathbb{R}^3$. Embedded into $4 \times 4$ matrices via **homogeneous coordinates**, every rigid motion is
>$$g = \begin{bmatrix} R & p\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \in SE(3).$$
>Frames are tracked with subscripts: $g_{ab} \in SE(3)$ is the **pose of frame $b$ expressed in frame $a$** — equivalently, the transformation that converts $b$-coordinates to $a$-coordinates. With this convention, composition stacks adjacent indices and the inverse swaps them:
>$$g_{ab}\,g_{bc} = g_{ac} = \begin{bmatrix} R_{ab}R_{bc} & R_{ab}\,p_{bc} + p_{ab} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad g_{ab}^{-1} = g_{ba} = \begin{bmatrix} R_{ab}^T & -R_{ab}^T\,p_{ab} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad g_{aa} = I_4.$$
>
>Homogeneous coordinates distinguish **points** from **free vectors**. A point $p\in\mathbb{R}^3$ lifts to $\bar p = (p, 1)^T$; a free vector $v\in\mathbb{R}^3$ lifts to $\bar v = (v, 0)^T$. The extra coordinate encodes whether translation acts:
>$$g\,\bar p = \begin{bmatrix} Rp + p_g \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad g\,\bar v = \begin{bmatrix} Rv \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
>(where $p_g$ is the translation part of $g$). Points are translated and rotated; free vectors are only rotated.
>
>$SE(3)$ is a six-dimensional Lie group (three rotation + three translation parameters). Its Lie algebra $\mathfrak{se}(3)$ consists of $4\times 4$ matrices
>$$\hat{\xi} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\omega} & v \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \xi = \begin{bmatrix} v \\ \omega \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6,$$
>called [[2.2. Twists and Screws|twists]]. The **exponential map** sends a unit twist $\xi_u$ ($\|\omega\| = 1$) and scalar $\theta$ to the finite rigid motion
>$$e^{\hat{\xi}_u \theta} = \begin{bmatrix} e^{\hat{\omega}\theta} & A(\omega, \theta)\,v \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} R & p \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix},$$
>where $e^{\hat{\omega}\theta}$ is [[1.4. Rodrigues' Formula|Rodrigues' formula]] and
>$$A(\omega, \theta) = I\theta + (1 - \cos\theta)\hat{\omega} + (\theta - \sin\theta)\hat{\omega}^2.$$
>Pure translation ($\omega = 0$) is the degenerate case $e^{\hat{\xi}_u \theta} = \begin{bmatrix} I & v\theta \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. The inverse log map solves $(\omega, \theta) = \log(R)$ (see [[1.5. Exponential Coordinates]]) then $v = A(\omega, \theta)^{-1} p$.
>
>The **adjoint representation** $\mathrm{Ad}_g : \mathbb{R}^6 \to \mathbb{R}^6$ transforms twist coordinates between frames:
>$$\mathrm{Ad}_g = \begin{bmatrix} R & \hat{p}R \\ 0 & R \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \xi' = \mathrm{Ad}_g\,\xi.$$
>
>These ingredients — the matrix form, the composition/inverse formulas, the twist exponential, and the adjoint — are the algebraic toolkit every downstream robotics topic (forward kinematics, Jacobians, dynamics, control) is built on.

# Intuition
$SE(3)$ is the natural mathematical home for rigid body poses. Embedding the affine transformation $(R, p)$ into a $4\times4$ matrix via homogeneous coordinates converts composition of rigid motions into matrix multiplication and inversion into a simple matrix inverse. This linearization trick is what makes the product-of-exponentials formula in [[2.1. Forward Kinematics|forward kinematics]] possible.

The distinction between points ($\bar{p} = [p; 1]$) and vectors ($\bar{v} = [v; 0]$) in homogeneous coordinates is not just notational—it encodes the physics that translation moves points but not free directions. A force direction, for instance, should not change when the origin is shifted, and the $0$ in the fourth entry ensures this automatically.

# Properties

## Group Operations

#### (*Composition Mechanism*) The translation of $g_{ab}\,g_{bc}$ is $R_{ab}\,p_{bc} + p_{ab}$, not $p_{ab} + p_{bc}$.

Following the chain $a \leftarrow b \leftarrow c$: a point known in $c$-coordinates is first translated by $p_{bc}$ to reach $b$-coordinates, then rotated by $R_{ab}$ and translated by $p_{ab}$ to land in $a$-coordinates. The translation $p_{bc}$ lives in frame $b$, so it must be rotated through $R_{ab}$ before being added to $p_{ab}$ — they have to be in the same frame to add. The "rotation of the downstream translation" is what makes rigid-motion composition non-abelian and is the source of the **adjacent-index cancellation rule**: $g_{ab}g_{bc} = g_{ac}$ only because the inner $b$ indices match.

#### (*Inverse Mechanism*) The inverse translation is $-R_{ab}^T\,p_{ab}$, not $-p_{ab}$.

Undoing $(R_{ab}, p_{ab})$ means undoing the *final* state, which was reached by rotating first and then translating. To return home you translate back by $-p_{ab}$ — but $p_{ab}$ is expressed in frame $a$ while the "undo translation" must be applied *before* the rotation, in frame $b$. Rotating $-p_{ab}$ into frame $b$ via $R_{ab}^T = R_{ba}$ gives the correct $-R_{ab}^T p_{ab} = p_{ba}$. The result satisfies the index identity $g_{ab}^{-1} = g_{ba}$. A common bug in tabletop simulators is using $-p$ in place of $-R^T p$ and watching objects drift on rotation.

## The Lie Algebra $\mathfrak{se}(3)$

#### (*Twist Exponential Mechanism*) $A(\omega, \theta)$ integrates the time-varying rotation into the translation.

For a scalar ODE $\dot{x} = ax + b$, the solution is $x(t) = e^{at}x(0) + a^{-1}(e^{at} - 1)b$. The $SE(3)$ exponential is the matrix generalization: the translation component accumulates the linear velocity $v$ rotated through every intermediate angle from $0$ to $\theta$, giving $\int_0^\theta e^{\hat{\omega} s} v\, ds = A(\omega, \theta)\,v$. The three terms in $A = I\theta + (1-\cos\theta)\hat{\omega} + (\theta - \sin\theta)\hat{\omega}^2$ come from integrating Rodrigues' formula term-by-term. Pure rotation about an axis through the origin ($v = 0$) gives zero translation; rotation about an off-origin axis ($v = -\omega \times q$) induces the familiar circular arc.

#### (*Non-Unit Twist*) For a non-unit twist, factor out $\|\omega\|$ and absorb it into $\theta$.

If $\|\omega\| \neq 1$, let $\tilde{\omega} = \omega / \|\omega\|$, $\tilde{\theta} = \theta\|\omega\|$, $\tilde{v} = v / \|\omega\|$, and apply the unit-twist formula with $(\tilde{\omega}, \tilde{\theta}, \tilde{v})$. This rescaling matters whenever twists come from velocity measurements rather than constructed unit screws.

#### (*SE(3) Log Map Recipe*) Extract $(\omega, \theta, v)$ from $g = (R, p)$ in two steps: SO(3) log on $R$ first, then $v = A(\omega, \theta)^{-1}p$.

**Step 1 — recover $(\omega, \theta)$ from $R$ alone**, using the [[1.5. Exponential Coordinates|SO(3) log map]]:
$$\theta = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{tr}(R) - 1}{2}\right), \qquad \hat{\omega} = \frac{1}{2\sin\theta}(R - R^T).$$
The trace formula uses $\mathrm{tr}(R) = 1 + 2\cos\theta$, a direct corollary of [[1.4. Rodrigues' Formula|Rodrigues' formula]]. The axis comes from the antisymmetric part $R - R^T = 2\sin\theta\,\hat{\omega}$. Two edge cases: $R = I$ gives $\theta = 0$ with $\omega$ undefined (any axis works for a zero-angle rotation); $\mathrm{tr}(R) = -1$ gives $\theta = \pi$ where $\sin\theta = 0$ kills the denominator — instead read off $\omega$ from any nonzero column of $R + I = 2\omega\omega^T$ and normalize (sign is ambiguous since $(\omega, \pi) \sim (-\omega, \pi)$).

**Step 2 — recover $v$ from $p$** using the inverse of the integration matrix:
$$v = A(\omega, \theta)^{-1}\,p, \qquad A(\omega, \theta)^{-1} = \frac{1}{\theta}I - \frac{1}{2}\hat{\omega} + \left(\frac{1}{\theta} - \frac{1}{2}\cot\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\hat{\omega}^2.$$
The singularity at $\theta = 0$ is handled by the pure-translation degenerate case: $R = I$ means $\omega = 0$, and the rigid motion is a pure shift, so $v = p$ with $\theta = \|p\|$ and $\hat{\omega}$ pointing along $p$.

Note that $v$ **cannot be recovered from $R$ alone**. The rotational data $(\omega, \theta)$ is encoded in $R$, but the same axis-angle pair is compatible with infinitely many translations $p \in \mathbb{R}^3$ — each pairing produces a different $v$. Step 2 explicitly mixes $R$ (through $A(\omega, \theta)^{-1}$) with $p$ to undo the rotational coupling that the forward exponential introduced.

**Frame of the extracted twist.** In isolation, the recipe is frame-ambiguous: at the identity, spatial and body twists coincide ($\mathrm{Ad}_I = I$), so the same $\xi$ exponentiates to $g$ whether interpreted as a constant spatial or constant body screw. The frame is fixed by *what you do with the result*. With the current pose $g_{sc}$ and target $g_{sd}$ both expressed in the spatial frame $s$, the index-matched composition gives $g_{sc}^{-1}\,g_{sd} = g_{cs}\,g_{sd} = g_{cd}$ — the relative motion expressed *in the current body frame*. So $\log(g_{cd})$ produces a body twist of $g_{sc}$. Reversing the order gives $g_{sd}\,g_{sc}^{-1} = g_{sd}\,g_{cs}$ — which does not collapse into a single index pair and instead represents the *spatial* version of the same screw. The two are related by the [[5.1. Rigid Body Velocity#The Adjoint Map|adjoint]]: $\xi_s = \mathrm{Ad}_{g_{sc}}\,\xi_b$.

#### (*Log Map in IK*) $\log(g_{sc}^{-1}\,g_{sd}) = \log(g_{cd}) \in \mathfrak{se}(3)$ is the body twist that drives the current pose to the target in unit time.

Given current pose $g_{sc}$ and target pose $g_{sd}$, the pose error $g_{cd} = g_{sc}^{-1}\,g_{sd}$ lives in $SE(3)$, and its log is the 6-vector $(v, \omega)$ fed into the Jacobian update $\dot{q} = J^{-1}\,\xi$ in [[5.4. Jacobian Inverse Kinematics|Jacobian IK]]. Near $\theta = 0$ the cotangent term in $A^{-1}$ becomes ill-conditioned numerically; production code uses a Taylor series expansion of $A^{-1}$ around $\theta = 0$ instead of the closed form.

## Adjoint Representation

#### (*Frame-Change Mechanism*) The off-diagonal block $\hat{p}R$ encodes the lever-arm coupling between rotation and translation under a frame change.

When a twist is reexpressed in a frame shifted by $p$ and rotated by $R$, the angular part $\omega$ rotates cleanly into $R\omega$ — angular velocity is independent of origin. The linear part $v$ picks up an extra term $\hat{p}(R\omega) = p \times (R\omega)$ because shifting the origin by $p$ changes the linear velocity of the moving point by exactly the cross-product with the angular velocity. The block $\hat{p}R$ is "rotate $\omega$ first, then take the cross product with $p$" — exactly the rigid-body velocity transport law (see [[5.1. Rigid Body Velocity#The Adjoint Map|adjoint]]). This is why pure rotation in one frame can look like rotation-plus-translation in another.

#### (*Algebraic Properties*) $\mathrm{Ad}$ is a group homomorphism: $\mathrm{Ad}_{g_{ab}\,g_{bc}} = \mathrm{Ad}_{g_{ab}}\mathrm{Ad}_{g_{bc}}$, $\mathrm{Ad}_{g_{ab}^{-1}} = \mathrm{Ad}_{g_{ba}} = (\mathrm{Ad}_{g_{ab}})^{-1}$, $\det\mathrm{Ad}_g = 1$.

Composition lifts: changing twist frames along a chain $a \leftarrow b \leftarrow c$ is the same as changing once by the composed transform $g_{ac}$. The inverse equals $\mathrm{Ad}_{g_{ba}} = \begin{bmatrix} R_{ab}^T & -R_{ab}^T\hat{p}_{ab} \\ 0 & R_{ab}^T \end{bmatrix}$ — useful when converting body twists ($\xi_b$, in the body's own frame) to spatial twists ($\xi_s$, in the world frame) and back via $\xi_s = \mathrm{Ad}_{g_{sb}}\xi_b$. The unit determinant means twist coordinates change frames without volume distortion in $\mathbb{R}^6$, which matters for the symplectic structure of rigid-body dynamics.

# Examples
>[!example] Composing Two Rigid Motions
>Let $g_{ab}$: rotate $90°$ about $z$, translate by $(1, 0, 0)$. Let $g_{bc}$: translate by $(0, 1, 0)$ (no rotation).
>>[!example]- Composition $g_{ac} = g_{ab}\,g_{bc}$
>>$g_{ab} = \begin{bmatrix} R_z(\pi/2) & e_1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $g_{bc} = \begin{bmatrix} I & e_2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
>>
>>$g_{ac} = g_{ab}\,g_{bc} = \begin{bmatrix} R_z(\pi/2)\cdot I & R_z(\pi/2)\,e_2 + e_1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
>>
>>Compute the rotated translation: $R_z(\pi/2)\,e_2 = R_z(\pi/2)(0,1,0)^T = (-1,0,0)^T$. So $p_{ac} = (-1,0,0)^T + (1,0,0)^T = (0,0,0)^T$.
>>
>>Result: $g_{ac} = \begin{bmatrix} R_z(\pi/2) & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ — a pure rotation. The translation of $g_{bc}$ along $b$'s $y$-axis got rotated by $g_{ab}$ into $a$'s $-x$ direction, canceling $g_{ab}$'s translation along $a$'s $+x$. The same vector lives in different frames before and after the rotation, which is exactly what the $R_{ab}\,p_{bc}$ term tracks.
