>[!note] Backpropagation Through Time
>**Backpropagation through time** (BPTT) trains an [[5.3.1. RNNs|RNN]] by unrolling it over the $T$ steps of a sequence. The unrolled network is a deep feedforward network with the same weights in every step. Ordinary [[2.3. Backpropagation|backpropagation]] runs on it. In a stacked RNN the gradients flow down through the layers and backward through time.
>
>Write the vanilla RNN as $\mathbf h_t = \sigma(\mathbf a_t)$ with pre-activation $\mathbf a_t = \mathbf W\mathbf h_{t-1} + \mathbf B\mathbf u_t + \mathbf b_h$. Here $\mathbf h_t, \mathbf a_t, \mathbf b_h \in \mathbb R^d$, $\mathbf u_t \in \mathbb R^m$, $\mathbf W \in \mathbb R^{d \times d}$ and $\mathbf B \in \mathbb R^{d \times m}$. The loss is $L = \sum_{t=1}^T \ell_t$, or $\ell_T$ alone for a many-to-one task. Gradients with respect to vectors are row vectors, and $\boldsymbol\delta_t = \partial L/\partial\mathbf a_t \in \mathbb R^{1 \times d}$. Each shared weight collects one term per step:
>$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf W} = \sum_{t=1}^T \boldsymbol\delta_t^T\mathbf h_{t-1}^T, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf B} = \sum_{t=1}^T \boldsymbol\delta_t^T\mathbf u_t^T, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf b_h} = \sum_{t=1}^T \boldsymbol\delta_t^T, \qquad \boldsymbol\delta_t = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf h_t}\,\mathrm{diag}\big(\sigma'(\mathbf a_t)\big).$$
>The Jacobian of one step is $\mathbf J_t = \partial\mathbf h_t/\partial\mathbf h_{t-1} = \mathrm{diag}\big(\sigma'(\mathbf a_t)\big)\,\mathbf W \in \mathbb R^{d \times d}$. A loss at step $t$ reaches an earlier state $\mathbf h_s$ through a product of Jacobians:
>$$\frac{\partial \ell_t}{\partial \mathbf h_s} = \frac{\partial \ell_t}{\partial \mathbf h_t}\,\mathbf J_t\mathbf J_{t-1}\cdots\mathbf J_{s+1}.$$
>The backward pass gets all of these from the recursion $\partial L/\partial\mathbf h_t = \partial\ell_t/\partial\mathbf h_t + (\partial L/\partial\mathbf h_{t+1})\,\mathbf J_{t+1}$, which starts at $t = T$.
>
>The product has $t - s$ factors, so its size changes geometrically with the distance:
>
>- **Vanishing gradients**: if $\|\mathbf J_k\|_2 \le \gamma < 1$ at every step, the gradient shrinks at least as fast as $\gamma^{t-s}$. With $\gamma = 0.9$, $50$ steps multiply it by at most $0.9^{50} \approx 0.005$.
>- **Exploding gradients**: in a linear RNN, $\mathbf J_k = \mathbf W$ and the product is $\mathbf W^{t-s}$. If the spectral radius $\rho(\mathbf W) = \max_i |\lambda_i(\mathbf W)|$ exceeds $1$, almost every gradient grows like $\rho^{t-s}$. With $\rho = 1.1$, $50$ steps multiply it by about $117$. If $\rho(\mathbf W) < 1$, it vanishes.
>- **tanh**: the derivative $1 - \tanh^2(a)$ lies in $(0, 1]$, so $\|\mathbf J_t\|_2 \le \|\mathbf W\|_2$. Saturated units shrink the gradient further. The sigmoid has $\sigma' \le 1/4$.
>- **ReLU**: the derivative is $0$ or $1$, so active units pass $\mathbf W$ through unchanged. No factor below $1$ limits a growing product, and the state itself can grow without bound.
>
>A stable forward pass, in which old inputs fade, therefore gives an exponentially decaying backward pass. This is why RNNs find long-range dependencies hard to learn.
>
>The forward pass takes $T$ sequential steps of $O(d^2 + dm)$ work each, and the backward pass costs about twice as much. BPTT stores every $\mathbf h_t$, so memory grows as $O(Td)$ per sequence and layer. A nonlinear RNN cannot run its steps in parallel. A linear recurrence $\mathbf h_t = \mathbf A_t\mathbf h_{t-1} + \mathbf b_t$ can, with a **parallel scan** of depth $O(\log T)$.

>The lecture derives the Jacobian product and the vanishing case. The exploding case and the comparison of tanh and ReLU come from the homework. The cost counts and the parallel scan come from the discussion section. The norm bounds follow Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio (2013), and the scan operator follows Blelloch (1990).

# Intuition

To train an RNN, we need to know how a change to $\mathbf W$ changes the losses at later steps. Unrolled, the loop becomes a chain of $T$ copies of the same layer. Each copy uses $\mathbf W$ once, and the gradient for $\mathbf W$ is the sum of the gradients of all copies. To reach a state $k$ steps back, the gradient passes through $k$ [[Jacobians]]. If each Jacobian multiplies the gradient by $0.9$, then $50$ of them multiply it by about $0.005$. The figure plots the factor $\rho^k$ on a log scale.

In a linear RNN, $\partial\mathbf h_t/\partial\mathbf h_s = \mathbf W^{t-s}$. The same power of $\mathbf W$ sets how much $\mathbf u_s$ still affects $\mathbf h_t$. If old inputs fade from the state, their gradients fade at the same rate. Long memory with usable gradients needs $\rho(\mathbf W)$ close to $1$. A value slightly below $1$ still vanishes over long spans, and a value slightly above $1$ explodes.

# Properties

#### *(Short Paths Dominate)* Long-range terms make up a tiny share of the total gradient, so gradient descent tends to fit short-range patterns first.

The gradient for $\mathbf W$ is a double sum over a loss step $t$ and an earlier step $s$. Each term contains the factor $\partial\ell_t/\partial\mathbf h_s$, which passes through $t - s$ Jacobians. With $\|\mathbf J_k\|_2 \le 0.9$, this factor has norm at most $0.9^{50} \approx 0.005$ times $\|\partial\ell_t/\partial\mathbf h_t\|$ when $t - s = 50$. The total gradient can still be large, and short-range terms make up almost all of it. The term for inputs $50$ steps apart can be smaller than the noise in a minibatch gradient. A loss at every step adds more terms to the double sum. Most of the new terms are short-range as well.

#### *(Eigenvalues Versus Norms)* The spectral radius decides the long-run size of $\mathbf W^k$, while the operator norm bounds every single step.

For any square matrix, $\|\mathbf W^k\| \to 0$ as $k \to \infty$ exactly when $\rho(\mathbf W) < 1$. The powers of a matrix far from symmetric can still grow over the first few steps. The matrix $\mathbf W = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 4 \\ 0 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}$ has $\rho = 0.5$ but $\|\mathbf W\|_2 \approx 4.06$. Its powers have norms $4.06$, $4.02$, $3.01$, $2.00$ and $1.25$ for $k = 1$ to $5$. They fall to $0.078$ at $k = 10$. The condition $\|\mathbf W\|_2 < 1$ rules out such growth, because $\|\mathbf W^k\|_2 \le \|\mathbf W\|_2^k$ ([[4.9b Operator Norm]]). For a symmetric $\mathbf W$ the two quantities agree, $\|\mathbf W\|_2 = \rho(\mathbf W)$.

#### *(Tanh Versus ReLU)* A tanh RNN keeps its state bounded and its gradients tend to vanish, while a ReLU RNN can explode in both passes.

With tanh, every entry of $\mathbf h_t$ stays in $(-1, 1)$ for any weights. Large pre-activations push units toward $\pm 1$, where the derivative $1 - h^2$ is near $0$. A recurrent weight above $1$ can then still give a Jacobian well below $1$. With ReLU, $\mathbf J_t = \mathbf D_t\mathbf W$, where $\mathbf D_t$ is diagonal with a $1$ for each active unit and $0$ otherwise. Active units pass the gradient through $\mathbf W$ without shrinking it. If $\mathbf W$ stretches vectors of active units, the state and the gradient both grow geometrically. Inactive units block the gradient completely. See [[3. Activation Functions]] for the derivatives.

#### *(Cost and Memory)* BPTT keeps every state from the forward pass, so memory grows with $T$ and long sequences are split into blocks.

The backward recursion needs $\mathbf h_{t-1}$ and $\sigma'(\mathbf a_t)$ at every step. For tanh, $\sigma'(\mathbf a_t) = 1 - \mathbf h_t \odot \mathbf h_t$, so storing the states is enough. A batch of $N$ sequences through $n_\ell$ layers stores $N n_\ell T d$ numbers. The steps also cannot run in parallel. Step $t$ needs $\mathbf h_{t-1}$, so a GPU must finish each step before it starts the next. In a stacked RNN, the cells $\mathbf h_t^{(\ell)}$ with the same value of $t + \ell$ do not depend on each other. The stack therefore needs $T + n_\ell - 1$ sequential rounds instead of $T n_\ell$. Traces that do not fit in memory are split into blocks of consecutive steps. The learned Kalman filter of [[5.3.1. RNNs]] trains this way.

#### *(Parallel Scan)* A linear recurrence can be computed in $O(\log T)$ parallel rounds, because composing affine steps is associative.

Each step of $\mathbf h_t = \mathbf A_t\mathbf h_{t-1} + \mathbf b_t$ is the affine map $\mathbf h \mapsto \mathbf A_t\mathbf h + \mathbf b_t$, stored as the pair $(\mathbf A_t, \mathbf b_t)$. Applying step $s$ and then step $t$ gives the pair
$$(\mathbf A_s, \mathbf b_s) \bullet (\mathbf A_t, \mathbf b_t) = (\mathbf A_t\mathbf A_s,\ \mathbf A_t\mathbf b_s + \mathbf b_t).$$
This operation is associative, so any grouping of the steps gives the same result. With $\mathbf h_0 = \mathbf 0$, $\mathbf h_t$ is the second entry of the combined pair for steps $1$ to $t$. A prefix scan computes all $T$ combined pairs. The Hillis-Steele scan takes $\lceil \log_2 T \rceil$ rounds and $O(T \log T)$ work. The Blelloch scan takes about $2\log_2 T$ rounds and $O(T)$ work. With a dense $\mathbf A_t$, each combine is a $d \times d$ matrix product that costs $O(d^3)$. Scans are therefore practical mainly when $\mathbf A_t$ is diagonal. The backward pass of a linear recurrence is another linear recurrence, run in reverse. It can use a scan too. A nonlinear $\sigma$ breaks the method, because compositions of maps $\mathbf h \mapsto \sigma(\mathbf W\mathbf h + \mathbf c)$ do not keep a fixed small form.

#### *(Gradient Clipping)* Rescaling any gradient whose norm exceeds a threshold $c$ prevents one exploding gradient from producing a huge update.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Gradient clipping follows Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio (2013) and Goodfellow et al., *Deep Learning*, §10.11.1.

Clipping by norm replaces the gradient $\mathbf g$ of all parameters with
$$\mathbf g \leftarrow \frac{c}{\|\mathbf g\|}\,\mathbf g \quad \text{if } \|\mathbf g\| > c.$$
The direction stays the same, and the step length is at most $\eta c$ for learning rate $\eta$. Where the loss rises sharply, one exploding gradient can otherwise move the parameters far from where training was. Clipping does nothing for vanishing gradients. Those need changes to the architecture, such as the gated cells in [[5.3.1. RNNs]].

#### *(Truncated BPTT)* Backpropagating through only the last $k$ steps caps memory at $O(kd)$ and ignores longer gradient paths.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Truncated BPTT goes back to Williams and Peng (1990).

Truncated BPTT splits a long sequence into chunks of $k$ steps. The forward pass carries the hidden state across chunk boundaries, so the state still holds older information. The backward pass stops at the start of each chunk and treats the incoming state as a constant. Each chunk gives one parameter update, so training on a long stream updates more often. Gradient paths longer than $k$ steps are cut. The network then gets no direct gradient for dependencies that span more than $k$ steps.

# Derivation

We want the gradient of $L$ with respect to the shared matrix $\mathbf W$. Give each step its own copy $\mathbf W_t$, and set every copy equal to $\mathbf W$. The loss depends on $\mathbf W$ only through the copies, so the multivariable chain rule gives
$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf W} = \sum_{t=1}^T \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf W_t}.$$
The Easy example below checks this rule against direct differentiation on a scalar model. Copy $\mathbf W_t$ enters only through $\mathbf a_t = \mathbf W_t\mathbf h_{t-1} + \mathbf B\mathbf u_t + \mathbf b_h$. Entry $(i, j)$ of $\mathbf W_t$ changes $a_t[i]$ at rate $h_{t-1}[j]$, so $\partial L/\partial\mathbf W_t = \boldsymbol\delta_t^T\mathbf h_{t-1}^T$. Since $\sigma$ acts entrywise, $\boldsymbol\delta_t = (\partial L/\partial\mathbf h_t)\,\mathrm{diag}(\sigma'(\mathbf a_t))$. The state $\mathbf h_t$ affects $L$ through $\ell_t$ and through $\mathbf h_{t+1}$, which gives the backward recursion
$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf h_t} = \frac{\partial \ell_t}{\partial \mathbf h_t} + \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf h_{t+1}}\,\mathbf J_{t+1}, \qquad \mathbf J_{t+1} = \mathrm{diag}\big(\sigma'(\mathbf a_{t+1})\big)\,\mathbf W.$$
Unrolling it gives $\partial L/\partial\mathbf h_t = \sum_{t' \ge t} (\partial\ell_{t'}/\partial\mathbf h_{t'})\,\mathbf J_{t'}\cdots\mathbf J_{t+1}$, with the empty product for $t' = t$ equal to $\mathbf I$. Each term is the Jacobian product from the callout. Summing $\boldsymbol\delta_t^T\mathbf h_{t-1}^T$ over $t$ gives the formula for $\partial L/\partial\mathbf W$. The same steps with $\mathbf u_t$ or $1$ in place of $\mathbf h_{t-1}$ give the gradients for $\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf b_h$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Gradient of a Room-Temperature Model
>A room's temperature deviation from its setpoint follows $h_t = \alpha h_{t-1} + \beta u_t$, with $h_0 = 0$. The room keeps the fraction $\alpha = 0.5$ of its deviation at each step, and the heater adds $\beta u_t$ with $\beta = 2$. The heater commands are $u_1 = 1$, $u_2 = 2$ and $u_3 = -1$. The loss $L = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{3} h_t^2$ asks for zero deviation at every step.
>
>>[!example]- Compute $\partial L/\partial\alpha$ and $\partial L/\partial\beta$ by BPTT, and check them by expanding each $h_t$.
>>The forward pass gives $h_1 = 2$, $h_2 = 0.5 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 2 = 5$ and $h_3 = 0.5 \cdot 5 - 2 = 0.5$. Write $g_t = \partial L/\partial h_t$. Backward, each step adds its own loss gradient $h_t$ to the gradient carried back through the Jacobian $\alpha = 0.5$:
>>$$g_3 = h_3 = 0.5, \qquad g_2 = h_2 + \alpha g_3 = 5.25, \qquad g_1 = h_1 + \alpha g_2 = 4.625.$$
>>Step $t$ adds $g_t\,h_{t-1}$ to $\partial L/\partial\alpha$ and $g_t\,u_t$ to $\partial L/\partial\beta$:
>>$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \alpha} = 4.625 \cdot 0 + 5.25 \cdot 2 + 0.5 \cdot 5 = 13, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \beta} = 4.625 \cdot 1 + 5.25 \cdot 2 + 0.5 \cdot (-1) = 14.625.$$
>>Expanding gives $h_1 = \beta u_1$, $h_2 = \alpha\beta u_1 + \beta u_2$ and $h_3 = \alpha^2\beta u_1 + \alpha\beta u_2 + \beta u_3$. Then
>>$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \alpha} = h_2\,\beta u_1 + h_3\,(2\alpha\beta u_1 + \beta u_2) = 10 + 3 = 13,$$
>>$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \beta} = h_1 u_1 + h_2\,(\alpha u_1 + u_2) + h_3\,(\alpha^2 u_1 + \alpha u_2 + u_3) = 2 + 12.5 + 0.125 = 14.625.$$
>>Both methods agree. The loss at step 2 contributes most of each gradient, because $h_2 = 5$ is the largest deviation.

>[!example] (Medium) Same Weight, Two Activations
>A one-unit RNN computes $h_t = \sigma(1.5\,h_{t-1} + u_t)$ with $h_0 = 0$ and a constant input $u_t = 1$. Compare $\sigma = \tanh$ with $\sigma = \mathrm{ReLU}$.
>
>>[!example]- Follow the state and the gradient factor $\partial h_{20}/\partial h_0$ for each activation.
>>With tanh, the first states are $0.762$, $0.973$ and $0.985$. The state then settles at the fixed point $h^* \approx 0.986$ of $h^* = \tanh(1.5h^* + 1)$. Each Jacobian there is $1.5\,(1 - h^{*2}) \approx 0.042$. The product of the $20$ Jacobians is $\partial h_{20}/\partial h_0 \approx 7 \times 10^{-27}$. The gradient vanishes even though the weight exceeds $1$, because the unit is saturated. With ReLU, every pre-activation is positive, so $h_t = 1.5\,h_{t-1} + 1 = 2(1.5^t - 1)$. The state reaches $h_{20} \approx 6649$, and every Jacobian equals $1.5$. The gradient factor is $1.5^{20} \approx 3325$, so the forward pass and the backward pass both explode.

>[!example] (Medium) Memory Budget for Speech
>A speech model stacks $n_\ell = 3$ RNN layers with $d = 1024$ units. It reads $m = 80$ audio features per frame at $100$ frames per second. One batch holds $16$ clips of $30$ seconds, in 32-bit floats.
>
>>[!example]- How much memory do the stored states need? How many multiply-adds does a training step take, and how many sequential rounds does the forward pass need?
>>Each clip has $T = 3000$ frames. BPTT stores $3 \cdot 16 \cdot 3000 \cdot 1024 \approx 1.47 \times 10^8$ numbers at $4$ bytes each, about $0.59$ GB. Per frame and clip, layer $1$ needs $1024^2 + 1024 \cdot 80 = 1{,}130{,}496$ multiply-adds. Each upper layer needs $2 \cdot 1024^2 = 2{,}097{,}152$, so the total per frame is $5{,}324{,}800$. The forward pass over the batch takes $5{,}324{,}800 \cdot 48{,}000 \approx 2.6 \times 10^{11}$ multiply-adds. With the backward pass at twice that, a training step takes about $7.7 \times 10^{11}$. The grid of $3 \times 3000$ cells needs $T + n_\ell - 1 = 3002$ sequential rounds, however many processors are available. A linear recurrence of the same length with a diagonal $\mathbf A_t$ would need $\lceil \log_2 3000 \rceil = 12$ rounds per layer with a parallel scan.

>[!example] (Hard) A Parallel Scan by Hand
>A scalar linear recurrence $h_t = 0.5\,h_{t-1} + b_t$ has $h_0 = 0$ and inputs $(b_1, b_2, b_3, b_4) = (4, 2, 6, 2)$. Each step is the pair $(0.5, b_t)$, and pairs combine by $(a_s, b_s) \bullet (a_t, b_t) = (a_t a_s,\ a_t b_s + b_t)$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute every $h_t$ sequentially, then with a two-round Hillis-Steele scan.
>>Sequentially, $h_1 = 4$, $h_2 = 0.5 \cdot 4 + 2 = 4$, $h_3 = 0.5 \cdot 4 + 6 = 8$ and $h_4 = 0.5 \cdot 8 + 2 = 6$. These are four dependent steps. In round $1$ of the scan, each position $t \ge 2$ combines with position $t - 1$, all at once. The four positions then hold $(0.5, 4)$, $(0.25, 4)$, $(0.25, 7)$ and $(0.25, 5)$. In round $2$, each position $t \ge 3$ combines with the round-1 result two places back. Position $3$ gets $(0.5, 4) \bullet (0.25, 7) = (0.125, 8)$, and position $4$ gets $(0.25, 4) \bullet (0.25, 5) = (0.0625, 6)$. The second entries are now $4, 4, 8, 6$, the same states as before. Two rounds equal $\log_2 4$. At $T = 1024$ the scan needs $10$ rounds instead of $1024$ steps. The first entries $0.5^t$ show how much $h_0$ still affects $h_t$.
