>[!note] Early Exercise
>**Early exercise** is the exercise of an American option before its expiry $T$. Let $C$ and $P$ be the prices of an American call and put with strike $K$, and $c$ and $p$ the prices of the matching European options. Let $S$ be the stock price today and $r > 0$ the continuously compounded risk-free rate. On a stock with no dividends, an American call is never exercised early. Exercise pays $S - K$, while the [[2.3.1b Option Price Bounds|lower bound]] makes the live call worth at least $S - Ke^{-rT}$, which is more. Hence $C = c$.
>
>An American put may be exercised early. Write the value of the put, if it is held, as its intrinsic value $Ke^{-rT} - S$ plus a time value $\text{TV}$. Exercise pays $K - S$, so the holder exercises when
>$$K(1 - e^{-rT}) > \text{TV}.$$
>The left side is the present value of the interest earned by receiving $K$ now instead of at $T$. The right side is the value the holder gives up by ending the option. The right to exercise early usually makes $P$ larger than $p$.
>
>Dividends change both answers. Let $D$ be a dividend with ex-dividend date $t$, the first day the stock trades without the right to $D$, where $0 < t < T$. Let $\text{PV}(D)$ be the present value of all dividends before $T$. Immediately before $t$, a call is worth exercising when the first condition below holds. A put is worth exercising early when the second holds:
>$$D > K(1 - e^{-r(T-t)}) + \text{TV}, \qquad K(1 - e^{-rT}) > \text{PV}(D) + \text{TV}.$$
>Larger dividends make early exercise more likely for a call and less likely for a put. In a [[2.3.3c Binomial Tree|binomial tree]], each node compares the exercise value with the value of holding and keeps the larger. These conditions are guides. The time value depends on later exercise choices, so they do not give the exact exercise rule.

# Intuition

Early exercise trades the option's remaining choice for an earlier cash flow. For a call, exercise means paying $K$ sooner, which costs interest. It also ends the call holder's right to decline the purchase if the stock falls below $K$. With no dividends, early exercise of a call has costs and no benefit. For a put, exercise means receiving $K$ sooner, which earns interest. The cost is the lost chance that the stock rises back above $K$, when the holder would prefer not to sell. When the stock is far below $K$, that chance is small and the interest is worth more.

# Properties

#### *(Call Without Dividends)* Selling an American call always beats exercising it, so the early exercise right is worth nothing.

A holder who no longer wants the call should sell it. The sale brings at least $S - Ke^{-rT}$, and its gap above the exercise value $S - K$ has two parts. Exercise pays $K$ now instead of at $T$, which loses interest worth $K(1 - e^{-rT})$ today. Exercise also ends the time value, which protects the holder against a fall below $K$. An investor who wants to own the share at $T$ can keep the call and buy a bond that pays $K$ at $T$. That pays $\max(S_T, K)$, never less than the share. Since $C = c$, any model for the European call also prices the American call on a stock with no dividends.

#### *(Put Trade-Off)* An American put is exercised early when it is deep enough in the money that the interest on $K$ outweighs the time value.

>[!warning] Rough Conditions
>The time value is part of the option price, and it depends on when the holder will exercise later. So these conditions do not give the exercise boundary in closed form. A precise rule for optimal exercise needs a pricing model and more advanced methods.

The interest term $K(1 - e^{-rT}) = e^{-rT} \cdot K(e^{rT} - 1)$ is the present value of the interest on $K$ over the life of the put. It grows with $r$, $T$, and $K$. The time value shrinks as the put moves deeper into the money, because a rise back above $K$ becomes unlikely. At $S = 0$ the stock stays at zero, the time value is zero, and exercise is certain. So there is a critical price $S^*$ below which the holder exercises. With $r = 0$ the interest term is zero, and an American put on a stock with no dividends is no longer worth exercising early.

#### *(Dividends and Calls)* A dividend can make early exercise of a call worthwhile, but only immediately before an ex-dividend date.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules assume that the call is exercised immediately before the ex-dividend date. The argument that no other time is better goes beyond the modules.

On the ex-dividend date the share price drops by about the dividend $D$. The call holder does not receive $D$, so the call loses value with the drop. Exercising immediately before the ex-dividend date $t$ gives the holder the share and its dividend. Let $S_{t^+}$ be the price immediately after the date. Exercise pays $S_t - K = S_{t^+} + D - K$, and holding is worth $S_{t^+} - Ke^{-r(T-t)} + \text{TV}$. Their difference gives the call condition in the callout. Exercise at any other time is worse than waiting until immediately before the next ex-dividend date. Waiting delays the payment of $K$ and misses no dividend. After the last dividend before $T$, the call is never exercised early.

#### *(Dividends and Puts)* Dividends make early exercise of a put less likely, because the stock will fall by the dividends before $T$.

The stock price today includes the dividends still to be paid, so the stock is expected to drop by them before $T$. If the put is held, its intrinsic value uses $S - \text{PV}(D)$ in place of $S$, and the put is worth $Ke^{-rT} - (S - \text{PV}(D)) + \text{TV}$. Exercise pays $K - S$. Comparing the two gives the put condition in the callout. Think of a holder who owns both the share and the put. Exercise delivers the share now, so the holder loses the dividends it would have paid. If $\text{PV}(D) \ge K(1 - e^{-rT})$, the condition cannot hold, because the time value is never negative. The holder then does not exercise before the dividends are paid.

#### *(Tree Check)* In a binomial tree, the American value at each node is the larger of the exercise value and the value of holding.

Work backward from expiry. At a node with stock price $S_t$, the American put value is
$$\max\Big(K - S_t,\; e^{-r\Delta t}\big[\hat p\, f_u + (1 - \hat p)\, f_d\big]\Big),$$
where $f_u$ and $f_d$ are the American values at the next up and down nodes and $\Delta t$ is the step length. In each step the stock price is multiplied by $u$ (up) or $d$ (down). Here $\hat p = (e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)$ is the risk-neutral probability of an up move, the probability under which the stock earns the risk-free rate on average ([[2.3.3b Risk-Neutral Valuation]]). It has a hat so it is not confused with the put price $p$. The second term is the value of holding. An American call uses the same formula with exercise value $S_t - K$. Trees with many steps give accurate prices. With $S = 50$, $K = 52$, $r = 6\%$, $T = 1$, a volatility of $30\%$, and 1,000 steps, the European put is worth $\$5.40$ and the American put $\$5.82$. The difference of $\$0.42$ is the early exercise premium, the value of the right to exercise early.

# Derivation

We want the exercise conditions in the callout. In each case we compare the cash from exercise now with the value of holding the option.

For a call on a stock with no dividends, exercise pays $S - K$. A held call is worth $C \ge c \ge S - Ke^{-rT}$ by [[2.3.1b Option Price Bounds]]. With $r > 0$, $Ke^{-rT} < K$, so $S - Ke^{-rT} > S - K$. Holding (or selling) always beats exercise, so the early exercise right has no value and $C = c$.

For a put on a stock with no dividends, exercise pays $K - S$. A held put is worth $Ke^{-rT} - S + \text{TV}$. Exercise wins when
$$K - S > Ke^{-rT} - S + \text{TV}.$$
Cancel $S$ and collect the $K$ terms to get $K(1 - e^{-rT}) > \text{TV}$.

For a call immediately before an ex-dividend date $t$, exercise pays $S_{t^+} + D - K$. Holding is worth $S_{t^+} - Ke^{-r(T-t)} + \text{TV}$. Exercise wins when $S_{t^+} + D - K > S_{t^+} - Ke^{-r(T-t)} + \text{TV}$. Cancel $S_{t^+}$ to get $D > K(1 - e^{-r(T-t)}) + \text{TV}$.

For a put on a stock with dividends, exercise pays $K - S$. Holding is worth $Ke^{-rT} - (S - \text{PV}(D)) + \text{TV}$. Exercise wins when $K - S > Ke^{-rT} - S + \text{PV}(D) + \text{TV}$, that is, $K(1 - e^{-rT}) > \text{PV}(D) + \text{TV}$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Deep In-the-Money Put
>An American put on a stock with no dividends has strike $\$50$ and one year to expiry. The risk-free rate is $6\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A pricing model values the put, if held to expiry, at $\$17.414$ when the stock trades at $\$30$ and at $\$6.596$ when it trades at $\$45$.
>
>>[!example]- For each stock price, compare the interest on the strike with the time value, and decide whether exercising now beats holding to expiry.
>>The interest term is $K(1 - e^{-rT}) = 50(1 - e^{-0.06}) = \$2.912$. The discounted strike is $50e^{-0.06} = \$47.088$.
>>
>>At $S = \$30$, the intrinsic value is $47.088 - 30 = \$17.088$, so the time value is $17.414 - 17.088 = \$0.326$. The interest of $\$2.912$ is larger, so exercise wins. Exercise pays $\$20.00$, which is $\$2.59$ more than the $\$17.41$ from holding. That gain equals $2.912 - 0.326 = 2.586$.
>>
>>At $S = \$45$, the intrinsic value is $47.088 - 45 = \$2.088$, so the time value is $6.596 - 2.088 = \$4.508$. That exceeds the interest of $\$2.912$, so the holder keeps the put. Exercise would pay only $\$5.00$, which is $\$1.60$ less than holding. The stock is only $\$5$ below $K$, so a rise above $\$50$ is still possible, and the time value is large.

>[!example] (Medium) A Call Before the Dividend
>A stock trades at $\$70$ immediately before it goes ex-dividend. An American call on it has strike $\$60$ and three months to expiry after the ex-dividend date. No other dividend is paid before expiry. The risk-free rate is $5\%$ per year with continuous compounding. After the date no dividend remains, so the call is worth the European price, which a pricing model puts at $\$8.852$ if the stock opens at $\$67.50$ and $\$10.720$ if it opens at $\$69.60$.
>
>>[!example]- Decide whether to exercise immediately before the ex-dividend date when the dividend is $\$2.50$, and again when it is $\$0.40$.
>>The interest lost on the strike is $K(1 - e^{-r(T-t)}) = 60(1 - e^{-0.05 \times 0.25}) = \$0.745$. The discounted strike is $60e^{-0.0125} = \$59.255$.
>>
>>With $D = \$2.50$, the stock opens at $\$67.50$. The intrinsic value after the date is $67.50 - 59.255 = \$8.245$, so the time value is $8.852 - 8.245 = \$0.607$. The threshold is $0.745 + 0.607 = \$1.352$. The dividend of $\$2.50$ exceeds it, so the holder exercises. As a check, exercise pays $70 - 60 = \$10.00$, which beats the $\$8.85$ call held through the drop by $\$1.15 = 2.50 - 1.35$.
>>
>>With $D = \$0.40$, the stock opens at $\$69.60$. The intrinsic value is $69.60 - 59.255 = \$10.345$, so the time value is $10.720 - 10.345 = \$0.375$. The threshold is $0.745 + 0.375 = \$1.120$. The dividend of $\$0.40$ falls short, so the holder keeps the call. Holding is worth $\$10.72$, which beats the $\$10.00$ from exercise by $\$0.72 = 1.12 - 0.40$.

>[!example] (Hard) Two-Step Tree for an American Put
>A stock trades at $\$80$ and pays no dividends. A one-year put has strike $\$84$. Use a two-step binomial tree with steps of six months, $u = 1.15$, and $d = 0.85$. The risk-free rate is $6\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Price the European put and the American put, find the node where early exercise wins, and check that node against the interest condition.
>>The risk-neutral probability of an up move is $\hat p = (e^{0.03} - 0.85)/(1.15 - 0.85) = 0.6015$, and the discount factor per step is $e^{-0.03} = 0.9704$. The stock prices are $\$92.00$ and $\$68.00$ after one step, and $\$105.80$, $\$78.20$, and $\$57.80$ at expiry. The put pays $\$0$, $\$5.80$, and $\$26.20$ at those final nodes.
>>
>>At the up node, holding is worth $0.9704 \times (0.6015 \times 0 + 0.3985 \times 5.80) = \$2.2429$. Exercise would pay $84 - 92 < 0$, so the holder keeps the put. At the down node, holding is worth $0.9704 \times (0.6015 \times 5.80 + 0.3985 \times 26.20) = \$13.52$. Exercise pays $84 - 68 = \$16.00$, so the holder exercises.
>>
>>The European put cannot be exercised there, so it is worth $0.9704 \times (0.6015 \times 2.2429 + 0.3985 \times 13.52) = \$6.54$ today. The American put uses $\$16.00$ at the down node and is worth $0.9704 \times (0.6015 \times 2.2429 + 0.3985 \times 16.00) = \$7.50$. Exercise today would pay only $\$4.00$, so the holder waits. The early exercise premium is $7.50 - 6.54 = \$0.96$.
