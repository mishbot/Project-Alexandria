>[!note] Principal-Protected Notes
>A **principal-protected note** (PPN) is a zero-coupon bond that returns the principal, plus calls or binary options on an index. The investor pays the notional $L$ at time $0$ and receives at least $L$ at maturity $T$. The issuer buys the bond and spends the rest of $L$ on options. The options can take two forms.
>- With calls, the issuer buys at-the-money calls on the normalized index $S_T/S_0$. The note pays $L$ plus a share of the index's rise.
>- With binaries, the issuer buys cash binaries. The note pays $L$ plus a fixed bonus if the index ends at or above its start, and $L$ otherwise.
>
>With calls, the note pays at $T$
>$$L + pL\max\Big(\frac{S_T}{S_0} - 1,\ 0\Big), \qquad p = \frac{1 - e^{-rT}}{c}.$$
>The **participation rate** $p$ is the share of the index's rise that the investor receives. Here $r$ is the continuously compounded risk-free rate. The price of the at-the-money call per unit of notional is $c$. A PPN is the mirror image of a [[2.3.6.3e Reverse Convertible Notes|reverse convertible note]]. A PPN spends the bond's interest on options it buys. A reverse convertible sells an option to raise its coupon.

# Intuition

Some investors want a share of the stock market's gains but cannot accept a loss of principal. A bank deposit protects the principal and pays only interest. A principal-protected note gives the investor options in place of the interest. The issuer sets aside enough money to repay the principal and buys options with the rest. The note works best when rates are high, since the interest then buys many options.

# Properties

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules name principal-protected notes as a use of options and binaries. The bond-plus-options construction and the participation rate follow Hull's treatment of these notes. The comparisons and variants in the later properties follow from that construction.

#### *(Principal Protection)* A principal-protected note holds a zero-coupon bond and spends the rest on options, so its upside depends on rates.

An investor pays $L$ at time $0$. The issuer buys a zero-coupon bond that pays $L$ at $T$, which costs $Le^{-rT}$ ([[1.2c Present Value]]). The remaining $L(1 - e^{-rT})$ buys options on the index. With at-the-money calls on the normalized index, each worth $c$, the budget per unit of notional buys $(1 - e^{-rT})/c$ calls. That number is the participation rate $p$. A version with binaries spends the same budget on cash binaries ([[2.3.6.1b Binary Options]]). It pays a fixed bonus if the index ends at or above its start. The investor gives up the interest on $L$ and, for an index that pays dividends, the dividends. Issuer fees come out of the same budget. The investor also holds the issuer's promise to repay $L$, so an issuer default can cost principal.

#### *(Rates and Volatility)* The participation rate rises with the interest rate and falls with volatility.

The rate enters $p = (1 - e^{-rT})/c$ twice. A higher $r$ makes the bond cheaper, so the budget $1 - e^{-rT}$ grows. A higher $r$ also raises the call's price $c$, since a call's rho is positive ([[2.3.4c Vega and Rho]]). The budget grows much faster, so $p$ rises with $r$. Volatility enters only through $c$. A higher $\sigma$ makes each call more expensive, so the budget buys fewer calls. A higher dividend yield lowers $c$ and raises $p$, but the note does not pass the dividends on. The table uses a five-year note with $q = 2\%$.

| | $\sigma = 18\%$ | $\sigma = 25\%$ |
|---|---|---|
| $r = 1\%$ | $c = 0.12594$, $p = 38.7\%$ | $c = 0.18190$, $p = 26.8\%$ |
| $r = 4\%$ | $c = 0.18466$, $p = 98.2\%$ | $c = 0.23572$, $p = 76.9\%$ |

From $r = 1\%$ to $r = 4\%$, the budget per unit of notional grows from $0.0488$ to $0.1813$, almost four times. The call's price grows by less than half. In a low-rate market an issuer can raise $p$ in two ways. It can cap the upside, or it can protect less than the full principal.

#### *(Calls or a Binary)* The calls version pays more than the binary version only after a rise larger than $c/b$.

Let $g = S_T/S_0 - 1$ be the index's rise. The binary version buys unit cash binaries that pay \$1 when $S_T \ge S_0$. Each costs $b = e^{-rT}N(d_2)$, with $d_2$ at strike $S_0$. So the budget buys a bonus $G = L(1 - e^{-rT})/b$. The calls version pays $pLg$ after a rise, with $pL = L(1 - e^{-rT})/c$. The two notes pay the same when $pLg = G$. The budget cancels, so the break-even rise is
$$g^* = \frac{G}{pL} = \frac{c}{b}.$$
Here $c$ and $b$ are the unit call and the unit cash binary, both at the money. Below $g^*$ the binary note pays more, and above it the calls note pays more. Both pay $L$ after a fall. In the (Medium) example, $c = 0.18466$ and $b = 0.42478$, so $g^* = 43.5\%$. An investor who expects a modest rise should prefer the binary version.

#### *(Partial Protection and Caps)* Protecting less than the full principal, or capping the gain, raises the participation rate.

An issuer can change two terms to buy more upside. Write $c(K)$ for the unit call at strike $K$ on the normalized index, so that $c = c(1)$.
- With a protection level $k < 1$, the bond repays only $kL$ and costs $kLe^{-rT}$. The budget grows to $L(1 - ke^{-rT})$, so $p = (1 - ke^{-rT})/c$. The note pays $kL + pL\max(S_T/S_0 - 1, 0)$, so a fall can cost up to $(1 - k)L$.
- With a cap $\kappa$ on the rise, the issuer also sells a call at strike $1 + \kappa$. The options form a [[2.3.2a Vertical Spread|bull call spread]], so $p = (1 - e^{-rT})/\big(c(1) - c(1 + \kappa)\big)$. The note pays at most $L + pL\kappa$.

Each change gives up something the full note keeps. Lower protection gives up part of the floor. A cap gives up the largest gains. The (Hard) example compares the three designs in one market.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Payments on a Principal-Protected Note
>An investor buys a three-year principal-protected note on a stock index for \$50,000. The note repays \$50,000 at maturity plus $70\%$ of the index's rise. The index pays no dividends. Ignore issuer default.
>
>>[!example]- Find the note's payment if the index ends $24\%$ up or $15\%$ down. Compare it with holding the index.
>>The note pays $L + pL\max(S_T/S_0 - 1, 0)$ with $L = 50{,}000$ and $p = 0.70$. After a rise of $24\%$, it pays $50{,}000 + 0.70 \times 50{,}000 \times 0.24 = \$58{,}400$. An investor who held the index would have $50{,}000 \times 1.24 = \$62{,}000$. The note gives up \$3,600 of the \$12,000 gain.
>>
>>After a fall of $15\%$, the note pays \$50,000. The index would be worth $50{,}000 \times 0.85 = \$42{,}500$. The note avoids a loss of \$7,500. Its return over three years is $16.8\%$ in the first case and $0$ in the second. A bank deposit would have paid interest in both cases.

>[!example] (Medium) Building a Principal-Protected Note
>An investor puts \$10,000 into a five-year note on a stock index. The issuer promises to repay \$10,000 at maturity plus a share of the index's rise. The risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding. The index has a dividend yield of $2\%$ and a volatility of $18\%$. Ignore fees and issuer default.
>
>>[!example]- Find the participation rate in at-the-money calls and the payments if the index ends $30\%$ up or $20\%$ down. Then find the bonus that a binary version could pay, and the participation rate if the rate were $1\%$.
>>The zero-coupon bond costs $10{,}000e^{-0.04 \times 5} = \$8{,}187.31$. That leaves an option budget of \$1,812.69.
>>
>>Price a call on the normalized index with spot $1$, strike $1$ and $\tau = 5$. Then
>>$$d_1 = \frac{(0.04 - 0.02 + 0.0162) \times 5}{0.18\sqrt{5}} = \frac{0.181}{0.40249} = 0.44970, \qquad d_2 = 0.44970 - 0.40249 = 0.04721.$$
>>With $N(d_1) = 0.67354$ and $N(d_2) = 0.51883$, the unit call costs
>>$$c = e^{-0.1}(0.67354) - e^{-0.2}(0.51883) = 0.60944 - 0.42478 = 0.18466.$$
>>The participation rate is $p = 1{,}812.69/(10{,}000 \times 0.18466) = 98.16\%$.
>>
>>If the index ends $30\%$ up, the note pays $10{,}000 + 0.981626 \times 10{,}000 \times 0.30 = \$12{,}944.88$. If it ends $20\%$ down, the note pays \$10,000. An investor who held the index with dividends reinvested would have $10{,}000 \times 1.30 \times e^{0.1} = \$14{,}367.22$ or $10{,}000 \times 0.80 \times e^{0.1} = \$8{,}841.37$. The note gives up the dividends and the interest in exchange for the floor.
>>
>>A unit cash binary that pays \$1 if the index ends at or above its start costs $e^{-0.2}N(d_2) = 0.42478$. The budget buys a bonus of about $1{,}812.69/0.42478 = \$4{,}267$. So the binary note pays \$14,267 after any rise and \$10,000 otherwise. The calls note pays more only after a rise above $4{,}267/9{,}816 = 43.5\%$.
>>
>>At $r = 1\%$ the bond costs $10{,}000e^{-0.05} = \$9{,}512.29$, so the budget falls to \$487.71. The unit call now costs $0.12594$, and the participation rate drops to $38.7\%$. Low rates leave little money for the options.

>[!example] (Hard) Trading Protection for Participation
>An issuer designs a five-year note on an index for \$20,000. The risk-free rate is $3.5\%$ and the dividend yield is $1.5\%$, both continuously compounded. The index volatility is $20\%$. The issuer compares three designs. Design A protects the full \$20,000 and buys at-the-money calls. Design B protects $90\%$, or \$18,000, and buys at-the-money calls. Design C protects the full \$20,000 and caps the rise at $50\%$. Ignore fees and issuer default.
>
>>[!example]- Find each design's participation rate and its payments if the index ends $20\%$ down, $30\%$ up or $70\%$ up. Then find where design B overtakes design A, and where design A overtakes design C.
>>The unit at-the-money call has $d_1 = (0.035 - 0.015 + 0.02) \times 5/(0.20\sqrt{5}) = 0.2/0.44721 = 0.44721$ and $d_2 = 0$. With $N(d_1) = 0.67264$,
>>$$c(1) = e^{-0.075}(0.67264) - e^{-0.175}(0.5) = 0.62404 - 0.41973 = 0.20431.$$
>>At strike $1.5$, $\ln(1/1.5) = -0.40547$. So $d_1 = (-0.40547 + 0.2)/0.44721 = -0.45943$ and $d_2 = -0.90665$. With $N(d_1) = 0.32296$ and $N(d_2) = 0.18230$,
>>$$c(1.5) = e^{-0.075}(0.32296) - 1.5e^{-0.175}(0.18230) = 0.29963 - 0.22955 = 0.07008.$$
>>
>>The full bond costs $20{,}000e^{-0.175} = \$16{,}789.14$, which leaves \$3,210.86. The $90\%$ bond costs \$15,110.23, which leaves \$4,889.77. The call spread costs $0.20431 - 0.07008 = 0.13423$. The participation rates are
>>$$p_A = \frac{3{,}210.86}{20{,}000 \times 0.20431} = 78.6\%, \qquad p_B = \frac{4{,}889.77}{20{,}000 \times 0.20431} = 119.7\%, \qquad p_C = \frac{3{,}210.86}{20{,}000 \times 0.13423} = 119.6\%.$$
>>
>>The table lists the payments.
>>
>>| Index at $T$ | Design A | Design B | Design C |
>>|---|---|---|---|
>>| $20\%$ down | \$20,000.00 | \$18,000.00 | \$20,000.00 |
>>| $30\%$ up | \$24,714.72 | \$25,179.99 | \$27,176.25 |
>>| $70\%$ up | \$31,001.02 | \$34,753.30 | \$31,960.42 |
>>
>>Design B pays \$2,000 less after any fall. It gains $(p_B - p_A)L$ more per unit of rise, so it overtakes A after a rise of $0.10/(1.1967 - 0.7858) = 24.3\%$. Design C pays at most $p_C \times 50\% = 59.8\%$ of the notional. Design A reaches that amount when $0.7858g = 0.598$, so A overtakes C after a rise of $76.1\%$. Design C pays the most for every rise up to $50\%$. Design B overtakes it after a rise of $58.3\%$. Design A has neither a cap nor a loss of principal. It pays for both with the lowest participation rate.
