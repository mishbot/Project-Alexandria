>[!note] One-Step Binomial Model
>The **one-step binomial model** prices a derivative when the stock can take only two values at expiry. Today the stock price is $S_0$. After one period of length $T$ it is either $S_u = uS_0$ or $S_d = dS_0$, with $d < u$. A derivative on the stock, such as a call, pays $f_u$ in the up state and $f_d$ in the down state. The risk-free rate is $r$ with continuous compounding. The price of the derivative today is
>$$f = e^{-rT}\big[p f_u + (1-p) f_d\big], \qquad p = \frac{e^{rT} - d}{u - d}.$$
>The weight $p$ depends only on $u$, $d$, $r$, and $T$. The real chance of an up move never enters the price.
>
>Three arguments give the same $f$. First, a writer who sells the option and holds $\Delta$ shares has a riskless position when
>$$\Delta = \frac{f_u - f_d}{S_u - S_d}.$$
>Here $\Delta$, the delta, is the number of shares held per option. A riskless position must earn $r$, and that fixes $f$. Second, $\Delta$ shares plus a cash amount $A$ invested at $r$ replicate the option, with
>$$A = e^{-rT}\,\frac{S_u f_d - S_d f_u}{S_u - S_d}, \qquad f = \Delta S_0 + A.$$
>A negative $A$ means borrowing. Third, any market price other than $f$ allows an [[4.2.2a Arbitrage|arbitrage]].
>
>The model assumes no transaction costs or taxes, free [[4.2.1. Short Selling|short selling]], and borrowing and lending at $r$. It also needs $d < e^{rT} < u$, or else the stock and the bond alone allow an arbitrage. [[2.3.3b Risk-Neutral Valuation]] reads $p$ as a probability, and [[2.3.3c Binomial Tree]] chains many single steps together.

# Intuition

A call pays $\max(S_T - K, 0)$, which is not a straight line in $S_T$. No fixed holding of shares and cash matches that payoff at every possible stock price. If the stock can end at only two prices, a match is always possible. The two states give two equations. The number of shares and the amount of cash give two unknowns.

# Properties

#### *(Delta as a Slope)* The delta is the change in the option value divided by the change in the stock price across the two states.

Going from the down state to the up state, the stock gains $S_u - S_d$ and the option gains $f_u - f_d$. Their ratio $\Delta$ is the slope of the line through the two end points $(S_d, f_d)$ and $(S_u, f_u)$. A holding of $\Delta$ shares gains or loses as much as one option, so the two cancel in a hedge. As the two prices $S_u$ and $S_d$ move closer together, the ratio approaches the derivative $\partial f/\partial S$. That limit is the delta of continuous-time hedging in [[2.3.3d Delta Hedging]].

#### *(Signs of Delta and Cash)* A call is replicated by buying shares with borrowed money, and a put by short selling shares and lending the proceeds.

Take a strike with $S_d \le K \le S_u$. For a call, $f_u = S_u - K$ and $f_d = 0$. Then $\Delta = (S_u - K)/(S_u - S_d)$ lies between $0$ and $1$, and $A = -e^{-rT} S_d f_u/(S_u - S_d)$ is negative. For a put, $f_u = 0$ and $f_d = K - S_d$. Then $\Delta$ lies between $-1$ and $0$, and $A$ is positive. Subtracting the put portfolio from the call portfolio leaves $\Delta_{\text{call}} - \Delta_{\text{put}} = 1$ share and $A_{\text{call}} - A_{\text{put}} = -Ke^{-rT}$ in cash. That is one share and a loan of $Ke^{-rT}$, the forward position in [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity]].

#### *(Arbitrage at the Wrong Price)* If the option trades at any price other than $f$, a trader can lock in the difference today with no risk.

Suppose the market price is $f_1 > f$. Write the option at $f_1$ and buy the replicating portfolio for $f$. The portfolio pays exactly what the option owes in both states, so the trader keeps $f_1 - f$. Suppose instead the market price is $f_2 < f$. Buy the option, short $\Delta$ shares, and lend the net cash $\Delta S_0 - f_2$. At $T$ the position owes the fixed amount $\Delta S_u - f_u = \Delta S_d - f_d$, whose value today is $\Delta S_0 - f$. The loan returns more than that, and the trader keeps $(f - f_2)e^{rT}$ at $T$.

#### *(No Real-World Probability)* The chance of an up move does not appear anywhere in the price.

The hedge removes all risk, so the derivation never weighs the two states by how likely they are. Two traders who disagree about the chance of an up move still agree on $f$, as long as they agree on $S_u$, $S_d$, and $r$. Only the sizes of the moves and the risk-free rate matter. So two stocks with different expected returns have the same option prices when $u$, $d$, and $r$ match. [[2.3.3b Risk-Neutral Valuation]] develops this point.

# Derivation

We want to derive $\Delta$, $A$, and the formula for $f$.

The first argument uses a hedged portfolio. The writer is short one option and long $\Delta$ shares. At $T$ the position is worth $\Delta S_u - f_u$ in the up state and $\Delta S_d - f_d$ in the down state. Setting the two values equal removes all risk:
$$\Delta S_u - f_u = \Delta S_d - f_d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta = \frac{f_u - f_d}{S_u - S_d}.$$
This is the hedge ratio in the callout. A riskless position must earn the risk-free rate, or else the trader could borrow at $r$ to buy it, or sell it and lend at $r$. So its value today is the discounted value at $T$, $\Delta S_0 - f = e^{-rT}(\Delta S_u - f_u)$. Solving for $f$ gives the price.

The second argument uses replication. Hold $\Delta$ shares and invest $A$ in cash, which grows to $Ae^{rT}$. The portfolio matches the option when
$$\Delta S_u + Ae^{rT} = f_u, \qquad \Delta S_d + Ae^{rT} = f_d.$$
These are two linear equations in the unknowns $\Delta$ and $A$. Subtracting them gives the same $\Delta$ as before. Substituting back gives
$$Ae^{rT} = f_u - \frac{f_u - f_d}{S_u - S_d}\,S_u = \frac{S_u f_d - S_d f_u}{S_u - S_d}.$$
Multiplying by $e^{-rT}$ gives $A$. By the [[4.2.2a Arbitrage|law of one price]], the option costs what the portfolio costs, $f = \Delta S_0 + A$. Comparing with the hedge argument, $A = -e^{-rT}(\Delta S_u - f_u)$, so both arguments give the same $f$.

For the general formula, substitute $S_u = uS_0$ and $S_d = dS_0$. The factor $S_0$ cancels from both terms:
$$f = \frac{f_u - f_d}{u - d} + e^{-rT}\,\frac{u f_d - d f_u}{u - d} = e^{-rT}\left[\frac{e^{rT} - d}{u - d}\,f_u + \frac{u - e^{rT}}{u - d}\,f_d\right].$$
The second step collects the terms in $f_u$ and in $f_d$. The two weights add up to $1$, and the first one is $p$. This gives $f = e^{-rT}[p f_u + (1 - p) f_d]$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Pricing a Call With a Hedge
>A stock trades at $\$80$. In three months it will be either $\$90$ or $\$72$. A three-month European call has strike $\$82$. The risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Find the price of the call by building a riskless hedge.
>>The call pays $f_u = 90 - 82 = \$8$ in the up state and $f_d = 0$ in the down state. The hedge ratio is
>>$$\Delta = \frac{8 - 0}{90 - 72} = 0.4444.$$
>>A writer who is short one call and long $0.4444$ shares holds a position worth $0.4444 \times 90 - 8 = \$32$ in the up state and $0.4444 \times 72 - 0 = \$32$ in the down state. The value is $\$32$ either way. Discounted for three months, it is worth $32e^{-0.04 \times 0.25} = \$31.682$ today. The shares cost $\tfrac{8}{18} \times 80 = \$35.556$, so
>>$$f = 35.556 - 31.682 = \$3.87.$$
>>As a check, $u = 1.125$, $d = 0.9$, and $p = (e^{0.01} - 0.9)/0.225 = 0.4891$. The formula gives $f = e^{-0.01} \times 0.4891 \times 8 = \$3.87$.

>[!example] (Medium) Exploiting a Mispriced Call
>Take the stock and call of the (Easy) example, with fair price $\$3.87$. A dealer quotes the call at $\$4.50$.
>
>>[!example]- Build a trade on $100$ calls that earns a riskless profit today, and find what a quote of $\$3.00$ would allow instead.
>>The call is too expensive, so write $100$ calls and receive $\$450$. Replicate them: buy $44.444$ shares for $\$3{,}555.56$ and borrow $\$3{,}168.16$, which is the present value of $\$3{,}200$. The cash flow today is
>>$$450 - 3{,}555.56 + 3{,}168.16 = +\$62.60.$$
>>At expiry, in the up state the shares are worth $\$4{,}000$ and the calls cost $\$800$. In the down state the shares are worth $\$3{,}200$ and the calls expire. Either way $\$3{,}200$ is left, which repays the loan exactly. The profit is $100 \times (4.50 - 3.874) = \$62.60$, collected today.
>>
>>At a quote of $\$3.00$ the call is too cheap. Buy $100$ calls for $\$300$, short $44.444$ shares for $\$3{,}555.56$, and lend the net $\$3{,}255.56$ for three months. The loan returns $\$3{,}288.27$. Closing the short position net of the call payoffs costs $\$3{,}200$ in both states. The trader keeps $\$88.27$ at expiry, worth $\$87.40$ today, which is $100 \times (3.874 - 3.00)$.

>[!example] (Hard) Replicating a Put
>Take the stock of the (Easy) example. A three-month European put has strike $\$82$.
>
>>[!example]- Price the put by replication, check the price with the formula in $p$, and show that the call and put portfolios together satisfy put-call parity.
>>The put pays $f_u = 0$ in the up state and $f_d = 82 - 72 = \$10$ in the down state. The replicating portfolio holds
>>$$\Delta = \frac{0 - 10}{90 - 72} = -0.5556, \qquad A = e^{-0.01}\,\frac{90 \times 10 - 72 \times 0}{90 - 72} = 50e^{-0.01} = \$49.50.$$
>>So short $0.5556$ shares and lend $\$49.50$. At expiry the loan pays $\$50$. In the up state the short shares cost $\tfrac{10}{18} \times 90 = \$50$, leaving $0$. In the down state they cost $\$40$, leaving $\$10$. The portfolio matches the put. Its cost today is $-\tfrac{10}{18} \times 80 + 49.50 = -44.44 + 49.50 = \$5.06$.
>>
>>With $p = 0.4891$, the formula gives $f = e^{-0.01} \times (1 - 0.4891) \times 10 = \$5.06$, the same price.
>>
>>For the call of the (Easy) example, $\Delta = 0.4444$ and $A = -\$31.68$. The call portfolio minus the put portfolio holds $0.4444 + 0.5556 = 1$ share and $-31.68 - 49.50 = -\$81.18$ in cash. That cash is $-82e^{-0.01}$, a loan of the present value of the strike. So $c - p = 3.874 - 5.058 = -\$1.18$, which equals $S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = 80 - 81.18$.
