>[!note] Otto Cycle
>The **Otto cycle** is the idealized cycle of a gasoline engine. It has four legs, starting from the state $A$ at the smallest volume $V_A$ and the highest pressure $P_A$. The first is an [[2.5.3d Adiabatic Process|adiabatic]] expansion $A \to B$ to the largest volume $V_B$, the power stroke. The second is an [[2.5.3a Isovolumetric Process|isovolumetric]] cooling $B \to C$ at $V_B$, the exhaust. The third is an adiabatic compression $C \to D$ back to $V_A$, the compression stroke. The fourth is an isovolumetric heating $D \to A$ at $V_A$, the ignition. Heat flows only on the two vertical legs, because the adiabats have $Q = 0$. With $d$ accessible degrees of freedom,
>$$Q_H = \tfrac{d}{2}\, V_A\, (P_A - P_D), \qquad Q_L = \tfrac{d}{2}\, V_B\, (P_B - P_C),$$
>both positive because $P_A > P_D$ and $P_B > P_C$. The two adiabats relate the pressures through $PV^{\gamma} = \text{const}$, and the efficiency comes out as
>$$e = 1 - \left( \frac{V_A}{V_B} \right)^{\gamma - 1} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\,\gamma - 1}}, \qquad r = \frac{V_B}{V_A},$$
>where $r$ is the **compression ratio** of the engine and $\gamma = 1 + 2/d$. The efficiency depends on the compression ratio and on nothing else. For air with $\gamma = 1.40$, a compression ratio of $8$ gives $e = 0.56$ and a ratio of $10$ gives $0.60$. Real engines reach about two thirds of these values, because the strokes are not adiabatic and the gas is not ideal.

# Intuition

In a four-stroke engine the piston draws in a mixture of air and fuel and compresses it. The spark ignites it, the hot gas pushes the piston down, and the exhaust valve lets the spent gas out. On the diagram the compression stroke is the lower adiabat $C \to D$: fast, so no heat escapes, and the gas warms as it is squeezed. Ignition is the vertical leg $D \to A$: the fuel burns so quickly that the piston has not moved, so the volume is fixed while the pressure and temperature jump. The power stroke is the upper adiabat $A \to B$: the hot gas expands and does the work that drives the car. The exhaust is the vertical leg $B \to C$: the pressure drops at fixed volume as the hot gas is replaced by cool fresh mixture. The cycle is a loop, and the area inside it is the work per cycle.

# Properties

#### *(Compression Ratio Decides Everything)* Two Otto engines with the same $r$ and the same gas have the same ideal efficiency, whatever their size or fuel.

The pressures $P_A$ and $P_D$ cancel out of the efficiency. More fuel per cycle raises $P_A$, gives a larger $Q_H$, and gives proportionally more work, but the same fraction of the heat is wasted. The only way to raise the ideal efficiency is to raise $r$, which is why engine designers push compression ratios up. The limit is set by the fuel: compress a gasoline mixture too far and it ignites on its own during the compression stroke, which is knocking. High-octane fuel resists this and allows a higher $r$.

#### *(Temperatures Along the Cycle)* The four temperatures follow from $TV^{\gamma - 1} = \text{const}$ on the adiabats.

The compression stroke raises the temperature by the factor $r^{\gamma - 1}$, and the power stroke lowers it by the same factor, so $T_D / T_C = T_A / T_B = r^{\gamma - 1}$. The efficiency can therefore be written as $1 - T_C / T_D = 1 - T_B / T_A$. It is less than $1 - T_C / T_A$, the value built from the cycle's extreme temperatures, and the [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot engine]] note shows that $1 - T_C / T_A$ is the most any cycle between those temperatures can reach. The Otto cycle takes in heat over a range of temperatures from $T_D$ up to $T_A$ and rejects it over a range from $T_B$ down to $T_C$. That spread is why it falls short. The same spread makes the Otto cycle irreversible, as the [[2.7.7 Entropy Statement of the Second Law|entropy statement]] shows at the end of the section.

#### *(Compute the Heats)* The work is easiest to find as $Q_H - Q_L$, not as the sum of the works on the four legs.

The vertical legs do no work, so the work is the difference of the two adiabatic works, each of which needs the endpoint pressures. The heats need the same pressures and give the efficiency at the same time. Finding $Q_H$ and $Q_L$ first, and using the adiabatic relation to connect the unknown pressures to the known ones, is the standard procedure for any cycle.

#### *(Diesel Differs)* A diesel engine ignites by compression and burns at nearly constant pressure, so its cycle replaces the vertical ignition leg with a horizontal one.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover the diesel cycle. It mentioned only that a diesel engine ignites its fuel by compression, with no spark plug. This property is standard textbook material.

The compression ratio of a diesel is high, near $20$, because there is no fuel in the cylinder during compression to ignite early. The fuel is injected at the top and burns as the piston starts down. The ideal diesel cycle has a lower efficiency than an Otto cycle with the same $r$, but a diesel runs at an $r$ that a gasoline engine cannot reach, and in practice it wins.

# Derivation

We want the efficiency in terms of the volumes alone. Heat flows only on the two isovolumetric legs, and on such a leg $Q = \tfrac{d}{2} V \Delta P$. On $D \to A$ at volume $V_A$ the pressure rises, so $Q_H = \tfrac{d}{2} V_A (P_A - P_D)$. On $B \to C$ at volume $V_B$ the pressure falls, and the heat out is $Q_L = \tfrac{d}{2} V_B (P_B - P_C)$. The efficiency is
$$e = 1 - \frac{Q_L}{Q_H} = 1 - \frac{V_B\,(P_B - P_C)}{V_A\,(P_A - P_D)}.$$
The problem gives $V_A$, $V_B$, and typically $P_A$ and $P_D$ or the temperatures, and $P_B$ and $P_C$ have to come from the fact that the four states lie on one cycle. $A$ and $B$ are joined by an adiabat, so $P_A V_A^{\gamma} = P_B V_B^{\gamma}$ and $P_B = P_A (V_A / V_B)^{\gamma}$. Likewise $C$ and $D$ are joined by an adiabat, so $P_C = P_D (V_A / V_B)^{\gamma}$. Subtracting,
$$P_B - P_C = (P_A - P_D) \left( \frac{V_A}{V_B} \right)^{\gamma}.$$
The pressure difference $P_A - P_D$ now cancels between numerator and denominator,
$$e = 1 - \frac{V_B}{V_A} \left( \frac{V_A}{V_B} \right)^{\gamma} = 1 - \left( \frac{V_A}{V_B} \right)^{\gamma - 1},$$
the headline result. The work per cycle is $W = Q_H - Q_L = \tfrac{d}{2}(P_A - P_D)\left[ V_A - V_B (V_A/V_B)^{\gamma} \right]$, which is $e\, Q_H$ as it must be.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Efficiency from the Compression Ratio
>A gasoline engine has a compression ratio of $8.0$ and runs on air, $\gamma = 1.40$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the ideal efficiency, and the gain from raising the ratio to $10$.
>>The efficiency depends only on $r$,
>>$$e = 1 - \frac{1}{8.0^{0.40}} = 1 - 0.435 = 0.56.$$
>>At $r = 10$ the value is $1 - 10^{-0.40} = 0.60$. Raising the compression ratio by a quarter gains four points of efficiency. Real engines of this type deliver $25$ to $35$ percent, because friction, heat loss to the cylinder walls, and incomplete combustion all cost work that the ideal cycle ignores.

>[!example] (Hard) An Otto Cycle in Numbers
>A cylinder draws in $0.800\ \text{L}$ of air at $1.00\ \text{atm}$ and $300\ \text{K}$ (state $C$), compresses it adiabatically to $0.100\ \text{L}$ (state $D$), and the ignition adds $800\ \text{J}$ of heat at fixed volume (state $A$). Take $\gamma = 1.40$ and $d = 5$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the pressure and temperature at each vertex, the heat rejected, the work, and the efficiency.
>>The compression ratio is $r = 8.00$. Along the adiabat $C \to D$,
>>$$P_D = P_C\, r^{\gamma} = (1.00\ \text{atm})(8.00)^{1.40} = 18.4\ \text{atm}, \qquad T_D = T_C\, r^{\gamma - 1} = (300)(8.00)^{0.40} = 689\ \text{K}.$$
>>The gas has $Nk = P_C V_C / T_C = (101\,325)(0.800 \times 10^{-3})/300 = 0.270\ \text{J/K}$. Ignition at fixed volume raises the temperature by $\Delta T = Q_H / (\tfrac{5}{2} Nk) = 800 / (2.5 \times 0.270) = 1184\ \text{K}$, so $T_A = 1873\ \text{K}$ and, at fixed volume, $P_A = P_D\, T_A / T_D = 50.0\ \text{atm}$. Along the power stroke $A \to B$ the factors reverse: $T_B = T_A / r^{\gamma - 1} = 815\ \text{K}$ and $P_B = P_A / r^{\gamma} = 2.72\ \text{atm}$. The exhaust leg $B \to C$ at fixed volume rejects
>>$$Q_L = \tfrac{5}{2}\, Nk\, (T_B - T_C) = (2.5)(0.270)(515) = 348\ \text{J}.$$
>>Then $W = Q_H - Q_L = 452\ \text{J}$ and $e = 452/800 = 0.565$, which equals $1 - 8.00^{-0.40}$ as the formula requires. The peak pressure of $50\ \text{atm}$ and peak temperature of $1900\ \text{K}$ are typical of a real engine at the top of the power stroke.
