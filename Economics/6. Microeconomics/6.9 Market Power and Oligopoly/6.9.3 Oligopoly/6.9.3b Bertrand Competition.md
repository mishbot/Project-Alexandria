>[!note] Bertrand Competition
>In **Bertrand competition**, firms first choose prices at the same time and then produce the quantity the market demands at those prices. Two firms sell an identical good at the same constant marginal cost $c$. Market demand $Y(p)$ is decreasing and positive at prices near $c$. Consumers buy only from the cheaper firm and split equally at a tie, so firm $i$'s profit is
>$$\pi_i(p_i, p_{-i}) = \begin{cases} (p_i - c)\,Y(p_i) & \text{if } p_i < p_{-i}, \\ (p_i - c)\,Y(p_i)/2 & \text{if } p_i = p_{-i}, \\ 0 & \text{if } p_i > p_{-i}. \end{cases}$$
>- The unique [[2.2c Nash Equilibrium|Nash equilibrium]] is $p_1^* = p_2^* = c$. Each firm sells $Y(c)/2$ and earns zero profit.
>- The result is **marginal-cost pricing**: two firms are enough to give the perfectly competitive outcome. Because a duopoly earns no profit at all, the result is called the **Bertrand paradox**.
>- With linear inverse demand $p = a - bY$, the outcome is $p = c$ and $Y = (a-c)/b$, with no deadweight loss.
>- To show that a profile is an equilibrium, show that no firm has a profitable deviation. To show that a profile is not an equilibrium, exhibit one firm with one profitable deviation.
>- At the equilibrium each firm is indifferent among all prices $p_i \ge c$. The equilibrium holds because no deviation is *strictly* profitable.
>- Bertrand is the duopoly model with prices chosen at the same time. [[6.9.3a Cournot Competition|Cournot]] firms choose quantities and keep the markup $p - c = (a-c)/3$. The number of firms is the same in both models, so the markup gap comes from the choice variable alone.

# Intuition

Two shops sell the same laptop model. A buyer checks both prices and buys from the cheaper shop. If both shops charge $\$900$ and the laptop costs $\$800$, each shop sells to half the buyers. A shop that cuts its price to $\$899$ gives up one dollar per laptop and gains the other half of the market. That nearly doubles its profit, so it cuts. The rival then cuts below $\$899$. The cuts stop only at $\$800$, where a further cut means selling at a loss. Price wars among PC makers follow this pattern.

The game is a [[2.1 Strategic Form Game|game in strategic form]]. The players are the two firms, the strategies are prices $p_i \ge 0$, and the payoffs are the profits $\pi_i$. A profile $(p_1^*, p_2^*)$ is a Nash equilibrium if $\pi_i(p_i^*, p_{-i}^*) \ge \pi_i(p_i, p_{-i}^*)$ for every price $p_i$ and each firm $i$. No firm can raise its profit by changing its own price while the other price stays fixed. The figure shows firm 1's profit as a function of $p_1$ when $c = 5$, $Y(p) = 20 - p$, and $p_2 = 9$. Profit rises along $(p_1 - c)\,Y(p_1)$ up to $p_2$, halves at the tie, and drops to zero above $p_2$. The best point lies slightly below $p_2$.

# Properties

#### *(Undercutting Doubles Sales)* A tiny price cut below the rival's price takes the whole market, so profit jumps at the rival's price.

Moving from the tie at $p_{-i}$ to $p_{-i} - \varepsilon$ changes firm $i$'s sales from $Y(p_{-i})/2$ to $Y(p_{-i} - \varepsilon)$ and its margin by only $\varepsilon$. For small $\varepsilon$ this nearly doubles profit whenever $p_{-i} > c$. The same jump drives cases 1 and 2 of the uniqueness proof in the Derivation.

#### *(Two Firms Are Enough)* Price competition with identical goods and equal costs removes all market power with only two firms.

In a [[6.9.3a Cournot Competition|Cournot]] duopoly each firm's output lowers the price on its own units, and this keeps the price above $c$. In Bertrand a firm can take the whole market with a tiny price cut. No markup survives that threat. The model predicts the same outcome as [[6.7.5 Profit Maximization and Supply|perfect competition]], with price $c$ and no deadweight loss, although only two firms are in the market.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture did not derive best responses. It asked whether the result is realistic and pointed to price wars among PC makers, but the reasons in the last property were not covered.

#### *(Best Responses)* When the rival prices above cost but not above the monopoly price, firm $i$ has no best response.

Let $p_M$ be the [[6.9.1 Monopoly|monopoly]] price, where $(p - c)\,Y(p)$ peaks, and assume this profit rises with $p$ below $p_M$. If $c < p_{-i} \le p_M$, firm $i$ wants the highest price strictly below $p_{-i}$, and no such price exists on a continuous price scale. If $p_{-i} > p_M$, the best response is $p_M$. If $p_{-i} = c$, every $p_i \ge c$ earns zero and is a best response. If $p_{-i} < c$, every $p_i > p_{-i}$ is a best response, since it avoids selling at a loss. The equilibrium $p_1 = p_2 = c$ is the one profile where each price is a best response to the other.

#### *(When the Paradox Fails)* Prices stay above marginal cost when products differ, costs differ, capacity is limited, or the firms meet repeatedly.

With differentiated products, a small price cut steals only some buyers, so undercutting gains less. With different costs, the low-cost firm sets a price at about the rival's cost and earns a profit (Hard example). With limited capacity, the undercutting firm cannot serve the whole market, and the rival keeps the leftover buyers at a higher price. With repeated play, firms can hold prices up by threatening a price war after any cut ([[4.1 Infinitely Repeated Game]]).

# Derivation

We want to show that $p_1 = p_2 = c$ is a Nash equilibrium and that no other profile is. First, the equilibrium. At $p_1 = p_2 = c$ firm 1 earns $(c - c)\,Y(c)/2 = 0$. A higher price $p_1 > c$ loses every buyer and earns $0$. A lower price $p_1 < c$ wins every buyer at a loss, $(p_1 - c)\,Y(p_1) < 0$. No deviation is strictly profitable, and the argument for firm 2 is the same.

Second, uniqueness. Label the firms so that $p_1 \ge p_2$. Every other profile falls into one of five cases, and in each one some firm has a profitable deviation. The figure maps the cases in the $(p_1, p_2)$ plane.

- Case 1, $p_1 > p_2 > c$. Firm 1 sells nothing. It deviates to a price slightly below $p_2$ and above $c$, and earns a positive profit.
- Case 2, $p_1 = p_2 > c$. Each firm earns $(p_1 - c)\,Y(p_1)/2$. Firm 1 deviates to $p_1 - \varepsilon$ and earns nearly twice as much.
- Case 3, $p_1 > c \ge p_2$. Firm 2 sells at $p_2 \le c$ and earns $(p_2 - c)\,Y(p_2) \le 0$. It deviates to a price between $c$ and $p_1$ and earns a positive profit.
- Case 4, $c > p_1 \ge p_2$. Firm 2 sells below cost and makes a loss. It deviates to any price above $p_1$ and earns $0$.
- Case 5, $p_1 = c > p_2$. Firm 2 makes a loss. It deviates to $c$ and earns $0$.

Only case 6, $p_1 = c = p_2$, remains, so the equilibrium is unique. If a deviation price in cases 1 to 3 is so high that demand is zero, the firm picks a price between $c$ and the choke price instead, where demand is still positive, and earns a positive profit.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Three Candidate Profiles
>Two Bertrand duopolists have marginal cost $c$ and the profits in the callout.
>
>>[!example]- For each profile, decide whether it is a Nash equilibrium. If not, name a profitable deviation for firm 1. (a) $p_1 = p_2 = c + 1$. (b) $p_1 = p_2 = c - 1$. (c) $p_1 = p_2 = c$.
>>(a) Not an equilibrium. Each firm earns $Y(c+1)/2$. Firm 1 can charge slightly less than $c + 1$, take the whole market, and earn almost $Y(c+1)$.
>>
>>(b) Not an equilibrium. Each firm earns $-Y(c-1)/2 < 0$. Firm 1 can charge a higher price, for example $c$, sell nothing, and earn $0$.
>>
>>(c) An equilibrium. Firm 1 earns $0$. A higher price still earns $0$, and a lower price earns a loss, so no deviation is strictly profitable.

>[!example] (Medium) Five Broken Profiles
>Two gas stations on one corner have marginal cost $c = 5$ per gallon, and the corner's demand is $Y(p) = 20 - p$.
>
>>[!example]- For each profile, find both stations' profits and one profitable deviation: (a) $(12, 9)$, (b) $(9, 9)$, (c) $(12, 5)$, (d) $(4, 3)$, (e) $(5, 3)$, where the first number is $p_1$.
>>(a) Case 1. Station 2 earns $(9 - 5)(11) = 44$ and station 1 earns $0$. Station 1 moves to $8.99$ and earns $3.99 \cdot 11.01 = 43.93$.
>>
>>(b) Case 2. Each earns $44/2 = 22$. Station 1 moves to $8.99$ and earns $43.93$.
>>
>>(c) Case 3. Station 2 earns $0 \cdot 15 = 0$. It moves to $11.99$ and earns $6.99 \cdot 8.01 = 55.99$. Its monopoly price is $12.5$, so pricing slightly below $12$ is its best option.
>>
>>(d) Case 4. Station 2 sells $17$ gallons at a loss of $2$ each and earns $-34$. It moves to any price above $4$, sells nothing, and earns $0$.
>>
>>(e) Case 5. Station 2 again earns $-34$. It moves to $5$, ties with station 1 at cost, and earns $0$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture assumed equal costs. The Hard example drops that assumption.

>[!example] (Hard) A Station With Lower Costs
>Station 1 signs a cheaper supply contract, so $c_1 = 4$, while $c_2 = 6$. Demand is $Y(p) = 20 - p$, and prices are set in whole cents.
>
>>[!example]- (a) Show that $p_1 = 5.99$, $p_2 = 6.00$ is a Nash equilibrium. (b) Why does station 1 not charge its monopoly price? (c) How much of the cost advantage reaches consumers?
>>(a) Station 1 sells $14.01$ gallons and earns $(5.99 - 4)(14.01) = 27.88$. Matching at $6.00$ halves its sales and earns $(2)(14)/2 = 14$. Any price above $6.00$ earns $0$. A lower price earns less, since $(p - 4)(20 - p)$ rises with $p$ up to $12$. Station 2 earns $0$. To sell anything it must charge $5.99$ or less, below its cost of $6$, which earns a loss. Neither station gains by deviating.
>>
>>(b) Station 1's monopoly price is $(20 + 4)/2 = 12$, with profit $64$. At $12$ station 2 would undercut. Station 2's cost caps station 1's price.
>>
>>(c) Consumers pay about $6$, the cost of the less efficient station, and buy $14$ gallons. The cost advantage of $2$ per gallon stays with station 1 as profit. With equal costs of $4$, the price would be $4$. On the cent grid, $(6.00, 6.01)$ is also an equilibrium. Other grid equilibria exist only if station 2 posts a price below its own cost.
