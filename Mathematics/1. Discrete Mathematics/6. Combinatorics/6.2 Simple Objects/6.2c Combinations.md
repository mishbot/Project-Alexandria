>[!note] Combination
>A **combination** is an **unordered list** of elements from a set where the elements in the set are **not replaced**. Formally, the total number  of combinations of $k \leq n$ elements that can be made from a set of $n$ elements is expressed as
>$$N=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}=\binom{n}{k},$$
>thereby not only "cutting off" the $n-k$ unused arrangements, but accounting for the $k!$ ways of [[6.2b Permutations|permuting]] every sequence of selected elements, since order does not matter here. This is a direct consequence of the division principle.
>
>Combination counting is used to count the number of ways to choose a subset of items without regard to order (and without repetition), for instance, selecting three students from a class of ten.

# Intuition
We know from the previous note that given a "constrained" sequence size $k \leq n$, and a set of $n$ elements, there are $\dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!}$ ways to construct the sequence without repeating elements, where order does matter. 

However, in combinations, the **order does not matter**. This means that sequences like $(1, 2, 3)$ and $(3, 2, 1)$, though different as permutations, represent the **same group** when order is irrelevant.

As such, we must account for all permutations that represent the **same combination**. Since each group of $k$ distinct elements can be permuted (i.e., rearranged) in $k \cdot (k-1) \cdot \ldots \cdot 2 \cdot 1=k!$ different ways, we divide by $k!$ to eliminate overcounting directly from the division principle and obtain $\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$. 
# Examples
>[!example] Dice Throws
>We are playing Threes Away, and throw the 3 dice one by one. 
>>[!example]- How many throws are in strictly increasing order?
>>Since each dice has 6 faces, and we are choosing 3 distinct elements, the total number of throws in strictly increasing order is
>>$$\binom{6}{3}=20.$$
>
>>[!example]- How many throws are in strictly decreasing order?
>>By symmetry, the answer is also $\binom{6}{3}=20$.
>
>>[!example]- How many throws are not in strict order?
>>First note that the total number of outcomes is $6^3=216$. Since this is a complement of the subset of sequences which are in strict order, the answer is
>>$$216-(20+20)=176.$$
>
>>[!example]- How many throws have two of the same value?
>>Since we are counting two of the same value, we have one free choice which can take on all 6 values, and one face which can take on the other 5 values. Since 2 of the 3 values are equal, our answer is 
>>$$6 \cdot 5 \cdot \binom{3}{2} = 90.$$
>>To think about why, consider 4 to be the repeating value, and 2 as the singleton value:
>>- If you choose positions 1 and 2 to be the 4’s, the outcome is $(4, 4, 2)$.
>>- If you choose positions 1 and 3, it’s $(4, 2, 4)$.
>>- If you choose positions 2 and 3, it’s $(2, 4, 4)$.
>>
>>Since there are 3 different ways to pick a pair of positions out of 3, that’s $\binom{3}{2} = 3$. In other words, “3 choose 2” just counts the number of ways to place the two identical dice among the three spots.
>>
>>As a side note, you might be tempted to answer $6 \cdot 1 \cdot 5 = 30$. However, this only accounts for one of the 3 cases we outlined above.
