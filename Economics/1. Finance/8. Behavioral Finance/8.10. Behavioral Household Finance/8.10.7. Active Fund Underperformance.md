>[!note] Active Fund Underperformance
>The **active fund underperformance puzzle** is the empirical fact that the average actively-managed equity mutual fund delivers *negative* after-fee alpha relative to a passive benchmark, while households continue to allocate the majority of equity mutual-fund assets to active rather than passive products. The puzzle has two pieces that must be held together: a **benchmark** that says what after-fee returns *should* be, and an **empirical result** that says what they actually *are*.
>
>**The rational benchmark (Berk and Green, $2004$).** In a frictionless world where (i) fund managers possess genuine skill, (ii) there are decreasing returns to scale — a \$$10$ billion fund cannot deploy its alpha-generating strategy as efficiently as a \$$100$ million one — and (iii) investors are fully rational and Bayesian, flows chase past performance until each fund has been scaled up to the point where its *after-fee* alpha is exactly **zero**. Skilled managers still produce gross alpha, but they capture all of it in fees; the marginal investor is indifferent between the active fund and the passive benchmark. The Berk-Green equilibrium is the rational null hypothesis against which all active-fund evidence is measured. It does *not* predict that active managers are untalented — it predicts that talent accrues to managers, not investors.
>
>**The empirical result.** The after-fee alpha of the average actively-managed equity fund is not zero, it is **negative** — on the order of $-1\%$ to $-2\%$ per year across the three classic studies:
>- **Gruber ($1996$)** — first formally documents that the average active equity fund underperforms its benchmark after fees, using the full post-$1984$ CRSP mutual-fund database.
>- **French ($2008$)** — calculates the aggregate "cost of active investing" (fees, trading costs, and tax drag) and finds that active investors collectively pay about $0.67\%$ of aggregate market value per year relative to a passive benchmark.
>- **Fama and French ($2010$)** — apply a bootstrap test to the cross-section of active-fund alphas and find that after fees, the distribution of $t$-statistics is shifted left of the rational prediction: the top funds look roughly consistent with luck, and the bottom funds are worse than luck.
>
>These findings violate Berk-Green: if the Berk-Green mechanism were running, average after-fee alpha would be zero, not $-1$-to-$2\%$. So either the model's assumptions fail or investors are systematically allocating to dominated products.
>
>**Channel decomposition (Del Guercio and Reuter, $2013$).** The underperformance is *not* uniform across active funds — it is concentrated in **broker-sold** funds, i.e. funds distributed through intermediaries (brokers, insurance agents, financial advisors) who receive commissions. Funds sold **direct-to-investor** (e.g. Vanguard, Fidelity Select, T. Rowe Price no-load funds) show roughly zero or slightly positive after-fee alpha, consistent with Berk-Green. This decomposition reframes the puzzle: it is not that households *prefer* losing money to active managers. It is that households who make independent choices behave roughly rationally, while households who delegate to a commissioned intermediary are sold into dominated products. The underperformance puzzle is therefore a **distribution-channel problem**, not a direct household-cognition problem.
>
>**Why the puzzle matters.** Active funds hold more than half of U.S. equity mutual-fund assets. If Del Guercio-Reuter is right, the welfare loss from broker-channel misallocation is large — on the order of tens of billions of dollars per year of household wealth transferred to intermediaries. It also reshapes the policy debate: the remedy is not "nudge households to pick passively" but "change broker compensation structures" — which is the motivation for the SEC's Regulation Best Interest and similar fiduciary rules.

# Intuition

The Berk-Green model begins from an observation that most discussions of active management miss: genuine skill should be *scarce*, and scarcity should produce rents. A truly skilled stock-picker whose trades generate $2\%$ of alpha on a \$$50$ million portfolio will attract inflows, because rational investors see the track record and pile in. As assets grow, the strategy becomes harder to execute — prices move against the manager's orders, liquidity dries up in small-cap names, decreasing returns to scale bite. At some fund size, the gross alpha has shrunk exactly to the level of fees, and any further inflow would push after-fee alpha negative, so flows stop. In equilibrium, every skilled manager has been scaled up to the point where investors are indifferent, and every unskilled manager has been allowed to die of underperformance. Talented managers end up rich; investors end up at zero alpha. Berk-Green is, in this sense, a *rational* story about why active funds don't beat passive ones — but it is also a story that predicts *zero* underperformance, not *negative* underperformance.

The empirical data sit a full $1$–$2$ percentage points below that zero line. Somebody is receiving dominated products — either because they don't know better, or because someone else is choosing for them. Del Guercio and Reuter's channel decomposition is the crucial piece: it shows the losers are disproportionately concentrated among investors who delegate the choice to a broker. The broker earns commissions on high-fee active funds but nothing (or little) on low-cost index funds, so the broker's incentive is to recommend the active option regardless of its expected return. The household trusts the broker, does not separately verify, and ends up holding the dominated product. Direct-channel investors face no such conflict and their allocations do not underperform.

# Properties

#### (*Berk-Green Equilibrium*) Flows absorb skill until after-fee alpha is zero.

Berk and Green ($2004$) assume managers have persistent skill and costs are convex in fund size. A rational Bayesian investor observes past returns, updates her belief about skill, and allocates capital until the marginal fund offers the same expected return as the passive benchmark. At the fund level, the skilled manager's gross alpha decays as assets grow because large trades are harder to execute; at the equilibrium fund size, gross alpha *equals* fees, so after-fee alpha is exactly zero. The model generates flow-performance sensitivity ("money flows to past winners"), persistence in gross returns ("skill is real"), and zero persistence in net returns ("rents go to managers, not investors") — all three of which are broadly consistent with the data *at the top* of the active-fund distribution. The model is falsified at the *average*: the data show negative net alpha, not zero.

#### (*Magnitude*) Average after-fee alpha is negative $1$–$2\%$ per year.

Gruber ($1996$) documents that from $1985$–$1994$, the average active equity fund underperformed the market by about $65$ basis points per year after fees. French ($2008$) widens the lens: combining fees, trading costs, and the tax inefficiency of actively managed funds, the aggregate "cost of active investing" is roughly $67$ basis points per year of *aggregate market capitalization*, or several tens of billions of dollars annually in the U.S. alone. Fama and French ($2010$) bootstrap the cross-sectional distribution of alpha $t$-statistics and find that after fees, the distribution is shifted systematically leftward of what pure luck would produce — there are fewer high-alpha funds than chance, and more low-alpha funds than chance. The three studies triangulate the same conclusion with different methods.

#### (*Broker Channel Concentration*) Del Guercio-Reuter decompose the underperformance by distribution channel.

Del Guercio and Reuter ($2013$) classify the U.S. active-fund universe into **broker-sold** and **direct-sold** and compute alphas separately. Broker-sold funds — those distributed through intermediaries that collect front-load or ongoing commissions — account for essentially *all* of the documented underperformance; direct-sold funds (no-load funds marketed directly to end investors) earn roughly zero after-fee alpha, consistent with Berk-Green. The cleaner the channel, the closer the data get to the rational benchmark. This splits the puzzle into a solved piece (direct investors are fine) and an unsolved piece (broker-sold is dominated), and pushes the explanation toward incentives in the distribution channel rather than cognitive errors on the part of households.

#### (*Fama-French Bootstrap*) The left-shifted alpha distribution is hard to rationalize by luck.

Fama and French ($2010$) ask: if managers had zero skill and generated only noise, how would the cross-section of alpha $t$-statistics look? They simulate the counterfactual by bootstrapping residuals from a Carhart four-factor model and comparing the simulated distribution to the actual one. The right tail of the actual distribution is *thinner* than the bootstrap — there are fewer stellar performers than chance would produce, inconsistent with a large population of truly skilled managers — and the left tail is *fatter* — more dreadful performers than chance would produce, consistent with fees and transaction costs grinding down the returns of unskilled managers. The shape of the distribution suggests active managers are slightly worse than zero-sum after fees.

#### (*Why Households Stay In Spite of It*) The puzzle is distributional, not aggregate.

If investors knew active funds lost $1\%$ per year, they would all switch to index funds overnight, and the puzzle would disappear. They don't — and the reason is not that investors are uniformly irrational. Direct-sold investors *do* reallocate toward passive: Vanguard's market share has grown from under $5\%$ to over $25\%$ of U.S. equity mutual-fund assets over the past two decades as direct-channel investors learned the lesson. The holdouts are disproportionately broker-served households, who trust an intermediary whose compensation depends on recommending the high-fee option. In aggregate, active funds lose market share every year, but slowly — because inertia, existing commissioned relationships, and the complexity of the fund menu all delay adjustment.

# Examples

>[!example] The Berk-Green zero-alpha equilibrium
>Suppose a manager has real skill: on \$$100$ million in assets, she generates $3\%$ gross alpha per year. Her fee is $1\%$. At this size, after-fee alpha is $2\%$, which is huge — rational investors see the track record and pile in. As assets grow to \$$500$ million, her trades start moving prices and gross alpha declines to $1.5\%$. After-fee alpha is now $0.5\%$. Flows continue until at \$$1$ billion in assets, gross alpha has decayed to $1\%$ and after-fee alpha is exactly $0\%$. At this point the marginal investor is indifferent between her fund and the index, and flows stop. The manager captures $1\% \times \$1\text{B} = \$10$ million per year in fees — her rents from genuine skill. Investors earn index returns. This is the Berk-Green equilibrium: skill is real, skill is rewarded, but investors see none of it. Observed after-fee alpha should be **zero**, not negative.

>[!example]- The empirical violation and the broker channel
>The three-study consensus (Gruber $1996$, French $2008$, Fama-French $2010$) shows the average active equity fund delivers about $-1\%$ after-fee alpha. This is *worse* than the Berk-Green prediction, so something in the model is wrong. Del Guercio and Reuter ($2013$) ask which: is it (a) the assumption that investors are rational, or (b) the assumption that the fund market is frictionless?
>
>They split funds into *broker-sold* (distributed with a commissioned intermediary — front-load funds, $12$b-$1$ fees, wirehouse-affiliated funds) and *direct-sold* (marketed directly to end investors, e.g. Vanguard, no-load Fidelity). Computing alpha separately:
>- **Direct-sold active funds:** after-fee alpha approximately zero, consistent with Berk-Green.
>- **Broker-sold active funds:** after-fee alpha approximately $-1$ to $-1.5\%$, accounting for essentially all of the aggregate underperformance.
>
>The implication is that rational investors who make independent choices end up approximately at Berk-Green. Investors who delegate to a commissioned broker end up dominated. The channel, not the household, is where the puzzle lives. Policy response: fiduciary rules (SEC Reg BI, DOL fiduciary rule) that constrain broker recommendations, rather than investor-education campaigns.

>[!example]- Why "active funds lose $1\%$" doesn't immediately imply "switch to index"
>A household in $2005$ with a long-standing relationship with a financial advisor at Merrill Lynch is told to buy an actively managed Putnam growth fund. The household pays a $5.75\%$ front load plus $1.2\%$ annual expense ratio. Over the next decade, the fund underperforms the S&P $500$ by $2.3\%$ per year after fees. The household could in principle switch to a Vanguard index fund at $0.04\%$, but:
>- The advisor does not recommend the switch because his commission comes from the Putnam fund.
>- The household trusts the advisor and has no independent way to compare.
>- Switching triggers a taxable event on accumulated gains.
>- The household does not distinguish "my advisor" from "my investment strategy," so firing the advisor feels like firing a relationship.
>
>The result is that the household continues to hold the dominated product for years after the underperformance is clear, even though a direct-channel alternative exists. Berk-Green assumes away all of these frictions; Del Guercio-Reuter show they are the binding ones in practice.
