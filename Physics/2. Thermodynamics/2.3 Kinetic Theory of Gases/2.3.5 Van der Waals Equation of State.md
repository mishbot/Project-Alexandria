>[!note] Van der Waals Equation of State
>The **van der Waals equation of state** corrects the [[2.2.3 Ideal Gas Law|ideal gas law]] for the two effects it ignores: the volume of the molecules themselves and the attraction between them. For $n$ moles in a volume $V$ at absolute temperature $T$,
>$$\left( P + \frac{a}{(V/n)^2} \right)\left( \frac{V}{n} - b \right) = RT,$$
>where $V/n$ is the volume per mole. The constant $b$, in $\text{m}^3/\text{mol}$, is the volume that one mole of molecules makes unavailable to the others. The constant $a$, in $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^4/\text{mol}^2$, measures the strength of the attraction between molecules. Both are fit to measurements and differ from gas to gas.
>
>| Gas | $a$ $(\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^4/\text{mol}^2)$ | $b$ $(10^{-5}\ \text{m}^3/\text{mol})$ |
>|---|---|---|
>| Helium | $0.0035$ | $2.4$ |
>| Hydrogen | $0.025$ | $2.7$ |
>| Nitrogen | $0.137$ | $3.9$ |
>| Oxygen | $0.138$ | $3.2$ |
>| Carbon dioxide | $0.364$ | $4.3$ |
>| Water vapor | $0.554$ | $3.1$ |
>
>Solved for the pressure, the equation reads
>$$P = \frac{RT}{V/n - b} - \frac{a}{(V/n)^2}.$$
>The first term is the ideal pressure in the reduced volume, and it is larger than $nRT/V$. The second term is the reduction caused by attraction, and it grows as the square of the density. When $V/n$ is much larger than $b$ and the attraction term is much smaller than $P$, both corrections vanish and the equation becomes $PV = nRT$. At room temperature and one atmosphere the corrections for air are about $0.1$ percent. They reach several percent at pressures of tens of atmospheres and become large near the temperature at which the gas liquefies.

# Intuition

The ideal gas law treats molecules as points that never notice one another. Van der Waals kept the kinetic theory picture and added the two features real molecules have. Each molecule has a size. A sample of $n$ moles therefore has less empty space to move in than $V$. The ideal gas law should use the free volume $V - nb$ instead. Squeezing the free volume raises the collision rate with the walls and raises the pressure above the ideal value. Each molecule also attracts its neighbors. A molecule in the middle of the gas is pulled equally in all directions. A molecule about to strike the wall has neighbors only on the inside, so the net pull points back into the gas. It hits the wall more gently than an ideal molecule would. The diagram shows both effects.

The size of the attraction term follows from counting. The number of molecules hitting the wall each second is proportional to the density $n/V$. The backward pull on each of them is proportional to the number of neighbors nearby, which is also proportional to $n/V$. The pressure reduction is the product, so it goes as $(n/V)^2$, which is the $a/(V/n)^2$ term. The two corrections compete. At moderate density the attraction wins and the gas is easier to compress than an ideal gas. At high density the molecules are nearly touching, the free volume shrinks toward zero, and the pressure climbs far above the ideal value.

# Properties

#### *(Which Correction Wins)* Attraction dominates at moderate density, molecular size at high density.

The ratio $PV/nRT$ equals $1$ for an ideal gas. For a van der Waals gas at fixed temperature it first drops below $1$ as the density rises. The attraction term grows as $(n/V)^2$ while the volume correction is still small. At higher density the free volume $V/n - b$ shrinks and the ratio climbs above $1$. Helium is the exception at ordinary temperatures. Its $a$ is so small that the volume correction wins at every density, and $PV/nRT$ stays above $1$.

#### *(Critical Temperature)* Above a temperature set by $a$ and $b$, no pressure can liquefy the gas.

The van der Waals isotherms, the curves of $P$ against $V/n$ at fixed $T$, change shape at the **critical temperature** $T_c = 8a / 27Rb$. Above $T_c$ each isotherm falls steadily, and compressing the gas only makes it denser. Below $T_c$ each isotherm has a flat region, which corresponds to the gas condensing into a liquid at constant pressure. For nitrogen the formula gives $T_c = 126\ \text{K}$, and the measured value is the same. The critical pressure is $P_c = a / 27b^2$ and the critical volume per mole is $3b$. *(Derivation omitted; the critical point is where the isotherm has zero slope and zero curvature.)*

#### *(Estimating Molecular Size)* The constant $b$ gives the volume, and hence the radius, of one molecule.

Dividing $b$ by Avogadro's number gives the volume that one molecule denies to the others. For nitrogen, $b / N_A = 3.9 \times 10^{-5} / 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 6.4 \times 10^{-29}\ \text{m}^3$. Treating this as the volume of a sphere gives a radius of about $2.5 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}$. The estimate is rough, because two hard spheres exclude a volume larger than their own. It still comes within a factor of two of the $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ size quoted in the [[2.1.1 Atomic Theory of Matter|atomic theory]]. The same radius sets the [[2.3.6 Mean Free Path|mean free path]].

#### *(Reading the Constants)* Large $a$ means a gas that liquefies easily, and large $b$ means a large molecule.

The gases with the largest $a$ in the table are water vapor and carbon dioxide. Their molecules attract one another strongly, and they condense at the highest temperatures. Helium and hydrogen have the smallest $a$ and the lowest boiling points. The values of $b$ vary less, because most small molecules are of similar size. The constants are fit to data over a limited range. A value taken from one table can differ by a few percent from another.

#### *(Still a Model)* The equation captures real gases qualitatively and fails in detail near condensation.

Van der Waals proposed the equation in 1873. It was the first equation of state to predict the existence of a critical point. It is not exact. Real isotherms below $T_c$ are flat across the condensation region, while the van der Waals isotherm has a wiggle there. Measured critical constants do not satisfy $P_c V_c / n R T_c = 3/8$ exactly. Engineers use more complicated equations for precise work. The van der Waals equation remains the standard first correction because it has two constants with clear physical meanings.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Carbon Dioxide in a Small Volume
>One mole of carbon dioxide is held in $1.00\ \text{L}$ at $300\ \text{K}$. The constants are $a = 0.364\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^4/\text{mol}^2$ and $b = 4.27 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{m}^3/\text{mol}$.
>
>>[!example]- Compare the pressure from the ideal gas law with the van der Waals prediction.
>>The ideal gas law gives
>>$$P_{\text{ideal}} = \frac{RT}{V/n} = \frac{(8.314)(300)}{1.00 \times 10^{-3}}\ \text{Pa} = 2.49 \times 10^{6}\ \text{Pa},$$
>>about $25$ atmospheres. The van der Waals equation gives two terms,
>>$$\frac{RT}{V/n - b} = \frac{2494}{1.00 \times 10^{-3} - 4.27 \times 10^{-5}}\ \text{Pa} = 2.61 \times 10^{6}\ \text{Pa}, \qquad \frac{a}{(V/n)^2} = \frac{0.364}{(1.00 \times 10^{-3})^2}\ \text{Pa} = 0.36 \times 10^{6}\ \text{Pa},$$
>>so $P = 2.61 \times 10^{6} - 0.36 \times 10^{6} = 2.24 \times 10^{6}\ \text{Pa}$. The real pressure is $10$ percent below the ideal value. The attraction term outweighs the volume term at this density.

>[!example] (Medium) Helium at the Same Conditions
>One mole of helium is held in $1.00\ \text{L}$ at $300\ \text{K}$, with $a = 0.00346\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^4/\text{mol}^2$ and $b = 2.38 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{m}^3/\text{mol}$.
>
>>[!example]- Is the pressure above or below the ideal value?
>>The volume term is
>>$$\frac{RT}{V/n - b} = \frac{2494}{1.00 \times 10^{-3} - 2.38 \times 10^{-5}}\ \text{Pa} = 2.555 \times 10^{6}\ \text{Pa},$$
>>and the attraction term is $a/(V/n)^2 = 0.00346 / 10^{-6} = 3.5 \times 10^{3}\ \text{Pa}$, which is negligible. The pressure is $2.55 \times 10^{6}\ \text{Pa}$, $2.3$ percent above the ideal value of $2.49 \times 10^{6}\ \text{Pa}$. Helium molecules barely attract one another, so only their size matters, and the correction goes the other way from carbon dioxide.

>[!example] (Medium) Nitrogen at Standard Conditions
>One mole of nitrogen occupies $22.4\ \text{L}$ at $273\ \text{K}$, with $a = 0.137\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^4/\text{mol}^2$ and $b = 3.87 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{m}^3/\text{mol}$.
>
>>[!example]- How far does the van der Waals pressure depart from one atmosphere?
>>The ideal pressure is $RT/(V/n) = 2271 / 0.0224 = 1.0138 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$. The van der Waals terms are
>>$$\frac{RT}{V/n - b} = \frac{2271}{0.0224 - 0.0000387}\ \text{Pa} = 1.0156 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}, \qquad \frac{a}{(V/n)^2} = \frac{0.137}{(0.0224)^2}\ \text{Pa} = 273\ \text{Pa},$$
>>so $P = 1.0156 \times 10^{5} - 273 = 1.0129 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$. The two corrections nearly cancel, and the result sits $0.1$ percent below the ideal value. At ordinary conditions the ideal gas law is accurate to three figures.
