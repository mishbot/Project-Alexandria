>[!note] Hydrostatic Equilibrium
>**Hydrostatic equilibrium** is the condition in which a [[1.7.1. Fluids|fluid]] at rest has all forces balanced, so every element experiences zero net acceleration. The downward gravitational force on each layer is exactly balanced by an upward pressure force from the fluid below.
>
>Considering a small horizontal slab of fluid of thickness $dh$, area $A$, and density $\rho$. The pressure below it must be slightly greater than the pressure above to support its weight. Setting the net vertical force to zero gives
>$$(P + dP)A - PA - \rho g A\,dh = 0 \iff \frac{dP}{dh} = \rho g.$$
>
>This expression defines hydrostatic equilibrium: the pressure in a stationary fluid increases with depth in proportion to its density and the acceleration due to gravity.
>
>For an incompressible fluid (i.e., a liquid), integrating yields
>$$P(h) = P_0 + \rho g h,$$
>where $P_0$ is the pressure at the surface and $h$ is the depth below it. 
>
>This equation implies that if the pressure at the surface level increases by $\Delta P$, then the pressure at depth $h$ also increases by the same amount. **Pascal’s Principle** extends this result, by stating that a change in pressure applied to an enclosed fluid **in hydrostatic equilibrium** is transmitted undiminished to every point of the fluid and to the walls of its container.
>
>This principle enables **hydraulic systems**, where a small input force over a small area can produce a large output force over a larger area:
>$$\frac{F_1}{A_1} = \frac{F_2}{A_2}.$$

>Mercury is poured into a U-tube as shown in the figure below. The left arm of the tube has a cross-sectional area $A_{1}$ while that of the right arm is $A_{2}$. Some water of mass mw is poured into the right arm as shown in the figure.
>
>![[Screenshot 2025-11-02 at 22.11.07.png|250]]
>
>>[!example]- Determine the length of the water column in the right arm of the U-tube.
>>We have that
>>$$m_{w}=\rho_{w}(A_{2}\ell) \implies \ell = \frac{m_{w}}{A_{2}\rho_{w}}.$$
>
>>[!example]- Suppose the density of water and the mercury are $\rho_{w}$ and $\rho_{Hg}$ respectively. What distance $h$ does the mercury rise in the left arm?
>>If the mercury rises by $h$ in the left arm, this means it rises by some $h'$ in the right arm. The relation is given by the volumes $\rho_{Hg}A_{1}h=\rho_{Hg}A_{2}h'$. As such, $h'=\frac{A_{1}}{A_{2}}h$.
>>
>>On the other hand, the pressure at the point where the water meets the mercury is equal to the pressure in the other tube at that height. More particularly, $h'$ is exactly the height between the bottom of the tube and the amount before the displacement $h$. As such,
>>$$P_{0}+\rho_{Hg}g(h+h')=P_{0}+\rho_{w}g\ell.$$
>>Since we already know that $\ell=\frac{m_{w}}{A_{2}\rho_{w}}$ from the previous subproblem, putting this all together yields
>>$$h=\frac{m_{w}}{\rho_{Hg}(A_{1}+A_{2})}.$$
