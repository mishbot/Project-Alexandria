>[!note] Gaussian Processes
>A **Gaussian process (GP)** is a distribution over functions $f: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ such that, for every finite set of input points $x_1, \ldots, x_n \in \Omega$, the random vector $(f(x_1), \ldots, f(x_n))$ is multivariate Gaussian. A GP is characterized by its *mean function* $\mu(x) = \mathbb{E}[f(x)]$ (almost always taken as zero) and its *covariance function* or *kernel* $K(x, x') = \text{Cov}(f(x), f(x'))$. The kernel must be positive semi-definite: for any finite set $u_1, \ldots, u_N \in \Omega$, the Gram matrix $\mathbf{K} = (K(u_i, u_j))_{i,j}$ must satisfy $\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{K} \mathbf{v} \geq 0$ for all $\mathbf{v}$.
>
>GPs are used in three canonical tasks:
>- **Interpolation**: given exact function values $f(x_1), \ldots, f(x_n)$, estimate $f(x)$ at a new point.
>- **Regression**: given noisy observations $y_i = f(x_i) + \epsilon_i$ with $\epsilon_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, estimate $f(x)$.
>- **Numerical integration**: given exact values, estimate $\int f(x)\,dx$.
>
>For all three, the posterior mean reduces to a kernel-weighted linear combination of the observed values, and the posterior is a new GP with closed-form mean and kernel. The choice of kernel encodes the prior beliefs about smoothness, stationarity, and scale of $f$.
>
>$$
>\text{(Regression posterior)} \quad f(x) \mid \mathbf{y} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{k}^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{y},\; K(x,x) - \mathbf{k}^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{k}\right),
>$$
>
>where $\mathbf{K}_{ij} = K(x_i, x_j)$ and $\mathbf{k}_i = K(x, x_i)$. Setting $\sigma^2 = 0$ recovers the interpolation case.

# Shared Structure

Every GP application in this folder starts from the same joint Gaussian assumption. Write $\mathbf{f} = (f(x_1), \ldots, f(x_n))^\top$ and $\mathbf{K}$ for the $n \times n$ Gram matrix. For exact observations (interpolation), the joint distribution
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{f} \\ f(x)\end{pmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{0},\; \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{K} & \mathbf{k} \\ \mathbf{k}^\top & K(x,x)\end{pmatrix}\right)
$$
and the standard Gaussian conditioning formula give
$$
f(x) \mid \mathbf{f} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{k}^\top \mathbf{K}^{-1} \mathbf{f},\; K(x,x) - \mathbf{k}^\top \mathbf{K}^{-1} \mathbf{k}\right).
$$
For noisy observations (regression), $\mathbf{K}$ is replaced by $\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}$. The *only* thing that changes across applications is the choice of kernel.

# When to Use Which Kernel

Three signals determine kernel choice:

1. **Smoothness prior.** How differentiable is $f$ believed to be? Brownian motion gives continuous-but-nowhere-differentiable samples, Integrated Brownian Motion gives once-differentiable samples and produces cubic-spline posteriors, and squared-exponential gives infinitely-differentiable samples.
2. **Boundary behavior.** Brownian motion forces $f(0) = 0$; scaled-BM-plus-constant drops this. Integrated-Brownian-Motion-plus-linear-term relaxes further by letting the linear component have unrestricted intercept and slope.
3. **Stationarity.** The squared-exponential and Matérn kernels depend only on $\|x - x'\|$, so they are stationary and isotropic — the prior looks the same everywhere in $\Omega$. Brownian and Integrated Brownian kernels are non-stationary: variance grows with $x$.

The scaled-IBM-plus-linear-term kernel is the workhorse for STAT 238's Bayesian nonparametric regression because (a) the posterior mean is a natural cubic spline — a widely used smoother — and (b) it connects directly to the ReLU-basis high-dimensional linear regression in [[3.8.1 Bayesian Linear Regression]].

# Connection to Other Bayesian Methods

The GP with ReLU basis is obtained as a limit of high-dimensional linear regression. Placing priors $\beta_2, \ldots, \beta_m \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)$ on the coefficients of a ReLU basis $\{1, x, \text{ReLU}(x-1), \ldots, \text{ReLU}(x-(m-1))\}$ and sending the grid density to infinity gives exactly the Integrated-Brownian-Motion-plus-linear-term GP. This makes GP regression the nonparametric limit of Bayesian ridge regression with a specific basis.

Inference when the kernel has hyperparameters (noise $\sigma$, signal scale $\gamma$) proceeds by computing the *marginal likelihood* $p(\mathbf{y} \mid \sigma, \gamma)$ and either maximizing it (empirical Bayes) or integrating it against a prior — see [[3.7 Marginal Likelihood]]. The Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury identity is the key tool for handling improper $\mathcal{N}(0, C)$ priors on linear-basis coefficients in the $C \to \infty$ limit.
