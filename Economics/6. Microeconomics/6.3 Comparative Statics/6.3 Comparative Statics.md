>[!note] Comparative Statics
>This section asks how the consumer's optimal choice responds when prices or income change. [[6.3.1 Comparative Statics]] sets up the method for any optimization problem. The implicit function theorem, applied to the first-order conditions, gives the derivative of an optimal choice. The envelope theorem gives the derivative of an optimal value, $dF/dp_k = \partial f/\partial p_k$ at the optimum. [[6.3.2 Homogeneity of Demand]] handles the simplest change: scaling every price and income by $t > 0$ leaves the budget set unchanged, so $x_i^*(t\mathbf{p}, tM) = x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)$.
>
>The next notes change one parameter at a time. [[6.3.3 Income Changes]] shifts the budget line and defines the Engel curve, normal goods with $\partial x_i^*/\partial M > 0$, and inferior goods, linked by the identity $p_1\,\partial x_1^*/\partial M + p_2\,\partial x_2^*/\partial M = 1$. [[6.3.4 Price Changes]] tilts the budget line and defines the demand curve, drawn with price on the vertical axis, and the Giffen good with $\partial x_i^*/\partial p_i > 0$. The [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]] splits a price effect into a substitution effect and an income effect,
>$$\frac{\partial x_i^*}{\partial p_j} = \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_j} - x_j^*\,\frac{\partial x_i^*}{\partial M}.$$
>[[6.3.6 Complements and Substitutes]] uses the signs of the cross-price terms in this equation to classify pairs of goods as gross or net substitutes and complements.
>
>Each note lists the lectures that covered it in its `lectures` property. A warning titled Not Taught in Lecture flags each part that goes beyond the lectures.

# Prerequisites

- [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]], for Marshallian demand $x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)$ and the bordered Hessian.
- [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function]], for $v(\mathbf{p}, M)$.
- [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]], for Hicksian demand $h_i(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$ and the expenditure function.
- [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem]], for derivatives of choices defined by equations.
- [[7.3 Envelope Theorem]], for derivatives of optimal values.

# Contents

- [[6.3.1 Comparative Statics]]: the question, the car examples, the implicit function theorem for choices, the envelope theorem for values, and a four-step recipe that signs a derivative using the second-order condition.
- [[6.3.2 Homogeneity of Demand]]: demand is homogeneous of degree $0$ in prices and income, so only relative prices and real income matter. **Not taught:** the Euler identity $\sum_j p_j\,\partial x_i^*/\partial p_j + M\,\partial x_i^*/\partial M = 0$.
- [[6.3.3 Income Changes]]: parallel budget shifts, the income expansion path, Engel curves, normal and inferior goods, and why two goods cannot both be inferior.
- [[6.3.4 Price Changes]]: budget tilts, the price-consumption path, demand curves, movements along versus shifts, the law of demand, and Giffen goods.
- [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]]: the substitution and income effects of a price change, for own and cross prices.
- [[6.3.6 Complements and Substitutes]]: gross and net substitutes and complements, symmetry of net effects, and why two goods are never net complements. **Not taught:** the proof that the net cross effects are equal.

# Recommended Path

Read the notes in order. No note uses a result from a later note, though some mention later notes as previews. Readers short on time should read 6.3.3, 6.3.4, and 6.3.5, which cover most exam questions on demand. Note 6.3.1 is the method behind all of them and is worth reading before any problem that asks to sign a derivative without solving. Note 6.3.6 depends on 6.3.5 and should come last.
