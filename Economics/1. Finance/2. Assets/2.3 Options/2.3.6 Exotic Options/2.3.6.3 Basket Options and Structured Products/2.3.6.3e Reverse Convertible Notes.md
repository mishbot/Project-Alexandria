>[!note] Reverse Convertible Notes
>A **reverse convertible note** (RCN) is a note on one stock that pays a coupon well above the risk-free rate. In return, the investor bears the stock's fall if the price drops through a barrier. The investor pays the notional $L$ at time $0$, when the stock price is $S_0$. The note combines two positions.
>- A **high-coupon bond** pays the coupon $H$ on each coupon date $t_1 < \dots < t_n = T$ and repays $L$ at $T$.
>- A **short down-and-in put** on $a = L/S_0$ shares has strike $S_0$ and a barrier $B < S_0$. The investor has sold this put to the issuer.
>
>Let $m_T$ be the lowest observed price up to $T$. The principal repaid at $T$ is
>$$R(S_T) = L - a\max(S_0 - S_T, 0)\,\mathbf{1}\{m_T \le B\} = \begin{cases} aS_T, & m_T \le B \text{ and } S_T < S_0, \\ L, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$
>The indicator $\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}$ equals $1$ when the event occurs and $0$ otherwise. Some notes deliver the $a$ shares instead of their value $aS_T$. With a constant risk-free rate $r$, continuously compounded, the coupon that makes the note worth $L$ today solves
>$$H\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{-rt_i} = L\big(1 - e^{-rT}\big) + a\,P_{DI}.$$
>Here $P_{DI}$ is today's price of one down-and-in put ([[2.3.6.2a Barrier Options]]). The coupon pays the interest on $L$ plus the put premium, spread over the coupon dates. An RCN is the mirror image of a [[2.3.6.3d Principal-Protected Notes|principal-protected note]]. A PPN spends the bond's interest on options it buys. An RCN sells an option to raise its coupon.

# Intuition

An investor who expects a stock to stay flat earns little by holding it. A bond pays only the risk-free rate. A reverse convertible pays more than both when the stock stays flat or rises a little. The extra coupon is the premium for a put the investor has sold. The barrier makes a loss less likely, since the stock must first fall to $B$. Once the stock has touched $B$, the investor bears the full fall from $S_0$, as a shareholder would. So the note pays a fixed amount in good states and a stock-like amount in bad states.

The name reverses a convertible bond. A convertible bond lets the investor turn the bond into shares when the shares are worth more. In a reverse convertible, the issuer delivers shares instead of the principal when the shares are worth less. The investor gives up the stock's upside, since the note never pays more than $L$ plus the coupons.

# Properties

#### *(Mirror Image of a Principal-Protected Note)* A principal-protected note buys options with the interest, while a reverse convertible sells an option to add to it.

Both notes start from a bond that pays $L$ at $T$ and costs $Le^{-rT}$. A [[2.3.6.3d Principal-Protected Notes|principal-protected note]] pays no coupon. It spends the interest $L(1 - e^{-rT})$ on calls, so its investor is long options. Its payment never falls below $L$, and it has no upper limit. A reverse convertible pays the interest out as coupons and sells puts on top. So its investor is short options. Its payment never rises above $L$ plus the coupons, and it can fall toward zero. A rise in volatility helps the PPN investor, since the calls gain value. It hurts the RCN investor, since the sold puts gain value. Low rates hurt the PPN most, because the interest is its whole option budget. At low rates an RCN coupon still holds the put premium. In the (Hard) example, the interest alone pays $3.53\%$ a year. A PPN in that market would spend those $3.53\%$ on calls. The RCN pays them out and adds $4.45\%$ from the put.

#### *(Pricing the Put)* The note is worth a bond minus $a$ down-and-in puts, and Hull's closed form prices the puts.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules give no price for the down-and-in put. The formula below is Hull's, from Chapter 26. It assumes a down barrier with $B \le K$, constant $r$, $q$ and $\sigma$, and continuous monitoring.

Take time $t$ before any touch, with stock price $S > B$ and time left $\tau = T - t$. Let $q$ be the dividend yield and $\sigma$ the volatility. Hull defines
$$\lambda = \frac{r - q + \sigma^2/2}{\sigma^2}, \qquad x_1 = \frac{\ln(S/B)}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} + \lambda\sigma\sqrt{\tau}, \qquad y_1 = \frac{\ln(B/S)}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} + \lambda\sigma\sqrt{\tau}, \qquad y = \frac{\ln\!\big(B^2/(SK)\big)}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} + \lambda\sigma\sqrt{\tau}.$$
These are inputs to the formula, and $N$ is the standard normal CDF. A down-and-in put with strike $K$ is worth
$$P_{DI} = -Se^{-q\tau}N(-x_1) + Ke^{-r\tau}N\big(-x_1 + \sigma\sqrt{\tau}\big) + Se^{-q\tau}\Big(\frac{B}{S}\Big)^{2\lambda}\big[N(y) - N(y_1)\big] - Ke^{-r\tau}\Big(\frac{B}{S}\Big)^{2\lambda - 2}\big[N(y - \sigma\sqrt{\tau}) - N(y_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau})\big].$$
For an RCN, $K = S_0$ and the note holds $a = L/S_0$ puts. [[2.3.6.2a Barrier Options]] explains each term. In-out parity gives a second route, $P_{DI} = P - P_{DO}$, with $P$ the vanilla put and $P_{DO}$ the down-and-out put. A desk that quotes the down-and-out put can price the knock-in from it. After a touch, the knock-in put is a vanilla put. From then on, the note is a bond minus $a$ vanilla puts.

#### *(What Sets the Coupon)* A higher barrier, a higher volatility or a higher dividend yield raises the put's price and the coupon.

The put premium is the only part of the coupon above the risk-free rate. Anything that makes a knock-in more likely raises it.
- A higher barrier lies closer to $S_0$, so the stock touches it on more paths.
- A higher volatility makes large falls more likely.
- A higher dividend yield lowers the stock's risk-neutral drift $r - q$, so falls become more likely.

The table reprices the two-year note of the (Hard) example, with $S_0 = K = 40$, $r = 3.5\%$, $q = 0.5\%$ and $\sigma = 25\%$. Each row changes one input.

| Change | $P_{DI}$ | Coupon per year |
|---|---|---|
| None ($B = 28$, $70\%$ of $S_0$) | $3.4109$ | $7.98\%$ |
| $B = 24$ ($60\%$ of $S_0$) | $2.1739$ | $6.37\%$ |
| $B = 32$ ($80\%$ of $S_0$) | $4.1132$ | $8.90\%$ |
| $\sigma = 30\%$ | $4.7285$ | $9.70\%$ |
| $q = 2\%$ | $3.8197$ | $8.52\%$ |

The investor is short these puts, so a rise in implied volatility lowers the note's value. A rise of one percentage point in volatility lifts $P_{DI}$ by $0.2724$. The note then loses $25 \times 0.2724 = \$6.81$ per \$1,000. A short vanilla put on the same 25 shares would lose \$5.26. The knock-in put has more vega than the vanilla put, as [[2.3.6.2a Barrier Options]] explains. So a high coupon comes with a large exposure to volatility.

#### *(Monitoring)* A barrier checked less often knocks in on fewer paths, so the put is cheaper and the coupon smaller.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The barrier shift for daily monitoring is Hull's, from Chapter 26. The modules list monitoring as a contract term without a correction.

The contract sets how the issuer checks the barrier. The table prices the put of the (Hard) example under three rules.
- With continuous monitoring, any touch of $B$ before $T$ counts.
- With daily monitoring, only the daily closes count. Hull's shift moves the barrier down to $Be^{-0.5826\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ with $\Delta t = 1/252$. Here the shifted barrier is $27.744$.
- With a check at maturity only, the put pays $S_0 - S_T$ only if $S_T < B$. This is the principal rule of an [[2.3.6.3b Autocallables|autocallable]] without protection.

| Monitoring | $P_{DI}$ | Coupon per year |
|---|---|---|
| Continuous | $3.4109$ | $7.98\%$ |
| Daily | $3.3447$ | $7.90\%$ |
| At maturity only | $2.4298$ | $6.70\%$ |

A simulation that checks each daily close gives $3.3343 \pm 0.0094$, close to Hull's shift. The maturity-only put equals the first two terms of Hull's formula, since $x_1$ equals $d_1$ at strike $B$. It loses the paths that touch $B$ and then end between $B$ and $S_0$. Those paths are worth $0.9811$ per share here, about $29\%$ of the continuous put.

# Derivation

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules state that an RCN is a high-yield bond plus a short down-and-in put. The table below checks this, and the par coupon follows from it.

We show that a bond minus $a$ down-and-in puts pays the note's cash flows, and then price both. The coupons $H$ are paid in every state, so only the payment at $T$ needs a check. Three cases cover every path.

| Position | No touch ($m_T > B$) | Touch, $S_T \ge S_0$ | Touch, $S_T < S_0$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bond: principal at $T$ | $L$ | $L$ | $L$ |
| Short $a$ down-and-in puts, strike $S_0$ | $0$ | $0$ | $-a(S_0 - S_T)$ |
| Total | $L$ | $L$ | $aS_T$ |

The last column uses $L = aS_0$. The total matches $R(S_T)$ on every path. By the [[4.2.2a Arbitrage|law of one price]], the note is worth the bond minus the puts. Each coupon is discounted from its own date ([[1.2c Present Value]]). So the note is worth
$$V_0 = H\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{-rt_i} + Le^{-rT} - a\,P_{DI}.$$
The first term is the coupons, the second the principal, and the third the puts. The investor pays $L$ for the note. Setting $V_0 = L$ and moving $Le^{-rT}$ to the right gives the par condition in the callout. Dividing by the sum of discount factors splits the coupon into two parts, $H = H_{\text{rf}} + H_{\text{put}}$, with
$$H_{\text{rf}} = \frac{L(1 - e^{-rT})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{-rt_i}}, \qquad H_{\text{put}} = \frac{a\,P_{DI}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{-rt_i}}.$$
The first part is the coupon of a risk-free bond that trades at par. The second part spreads the put premium over the coupon dates.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Payments in Four Scenarios
>A one-year reverse convertible on a stock has a notional of \$1,000 and pays a coupon of \$90 at maturity. The stock trades at $S_0 = \$50$ and pays no dividends. The put has strike \$50 and barrier \$35, monitored continuously. Ignore issuer default.
>
>>[!example]- Find the note's total payment in four scenarios, and compare it with \$1,000 invested in the stock. In scenario 1 the stock's low is \$41 and it ends at \$44. In scenario 2 the low is \$33 and it ends at \$52. In scenario 3 the low is \$30 and it ends at \$38. In scenario 4 the low is \$45 and it ends at \$60.
>>The note references $a = 1{,}000/50 = 20$ shares. The coupon of \$90 is paid in every scenario. The principal depends on whether the stock touched \$35 and where it ended.
>>
>>In scenario 1 the stock never touched \$35, so the note repays \$1,000. The total is \$1,090. The stock alone would be worth $20 \times 44 = \$880$.
>>
>>In scenario 2 the stock touched \$35, so the put knocked in. The stock then ended at \$52, above the strike, so the put pays nothing. The note repays \$1,000, and the total is \$1,090. The stock would be worth \$1,040.
>>
>>In scenario 3 the put knocked in and the stock ended at \$38. The note repays $20 \times 38 = \$760$, or 20 shares. The total is \$850. The stock would be worth \$760, so the coupon is the only difference.
>>
>>In scenario 4 the stock rose to \$60 without touching the barrier. The note pays \$1,090, while the stock would be worth \$1,200. The investor gave up the rise above the coupon.

>[!example] (Medium) The Coupon a Put Premium Buys
>A bank issues a one-year reverse convertible with a notional of \$1,000 on a stock at $S_0 = \$25$. The put has strike \$25 and barrier \$17.50, which is $70\%$ of $S_0$. The bank's desk prices the down-and-in put at \$0.95 per share. The risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding. The note pays one coupon, at maturity. Ignore issuer default and fees.
>
>>[!example]- Find the coupon that makes the note fair, and split it into its risk-free part and its put part. Then find the payment if the put knocks in and the stock ends at \$15. Below what final price does a knocked-in note pay less than a risk-free deposit?
>>The note references $a = 1{,}000/25 = 40$ shares. So the investor sells puts worth $40 \times 0.95 = \$38$. The issuer can invest $1{,}000 + 38 = \$1{,}038$ for one year. At maturity that grows to $1{,}038e^{0.04} = \$1{,}080.36$. The note repays \$1,000, so the fair coupon is \$80.36, or $8.04\%$.
>>
>>The par condition with one coupon date gives the same answer:
>>$$He^{-0.04} = 1{,}000\big(1 - e^{-0.04}\big) + 38, \qquad H = 1{,}000\big(e^{0.04} - 1\big) + 38e^{0.04} = 40.81 + 39.55 = \$80.36.$$
>>A risk-free deposit would pay \$40.81. The put premium adds \$39.55 and almost doubles the coupon.
>>
>>If the put knocks in and the stock ends at \$15, the note delivers 40 shares worth \$600. With the coupon, the investor receives \$680.36, a loss of about $32\%$. A deposit would pay \$1,040.81. After a knock-in, the note pays $40S_T + 80.36$ for any $S_T$ below \$25. This is less than the deposit when $S_T < (1{,}040.81 - 80.36)/40 = \$24.01$. So after a touch, a final price about $4\%$ below the strike already pays less than the deposit.

>This example uses Hull's closed form for the down-and-in put, from Chapter 26.

>[!example] (Hard) Pricing a Two-Year Reverse Convertible
>A bank issues a two-year reverse convertible with a notional of \$1,000 on a stock at $S_0 = \$40$. It pays a coupon every six months. The put has strike \$40 and barrier \$28, which is $70\%$ of $S_0$, monitored continuously. The stock has a dividend yield of $0.5\%$ and a volatility of $25\%$. The risk-free rate is $3.5\%$. All rates are continuously compounded. Ignore issuer default and fees.
>
>>[!example]- Price the down-and-in put with Hull's formula, and the down-and-out put with in-out parity. Find the semiannual coupon that makes the note fair, and split it into its two parts. Then find the investor's total if the stock touches \$28 and ends at \$30.
>>Here $\tau = 2$ and $\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = 0.35355$. The exponent is
>>$$\lambda = \frac{0.035 - 0.005 + 0.03125}{0.0625} = 0.98,$$
>>so $\lambda\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = 0.34648$. The three arguments are
>>$$x_1 = \frac{\ln(40/28)}{0.35355} + 0.34648 = 1.35531, \qquad y = \frac{\ln(28^2/40^2)}{0.35355} + 0.34648 = -1.67118, \qquad y_1 = \frac{\ln(28/40)}{0.35355} + 0.34648 = -0.66235.$$
>>The two powers are $(28/40)^{1.96} = 0.49704$ and $(28/40)^{-0.04} = 1.01437$. The discounted prices are $40e^{-0.01} = 39.6020$ and $40e^{-0.07} = 37.2958$.
>>
>>The four terms of the formula are
>>$$-39.6020\,N(-1.35531) = -39.6020 \times 0.08766 = -3.4715,$$
>>$$37.2958\,N(-1.00176) = 37.2958 \times 0.15823 = 5.9013,$$
>>$$39.6020 \times 0.49704\,\big[N(-1.67118) - N(-0.66235)\big] = 19.6838 \times (0.04734 - 0.25387) = -4.0653,$$
>>$$-37.2958 \times 1.01437\,\big[N(-2.02473) - N(-1.01590)\big] = -37.8317 \times (0.02145 - 0.15484) = 5.0464.$$
>>They sum to $P_{DI} = 3.4109$. A simulation of one million paths gives $3.4098 \pm 0.0021$. The vanilla put is worth $4.3185$, so in-out parity gives $P_{DO} = 4.3185 - 3.4109 = 0.9076$. The knock-in put keeps $79\%$ of the vanilla put's value.
>>
>>The note sells $a = 1{,}000/40 = 25$ puts, worth $25 \times 3.4109 = \$85.27$. The coupon dates are $0.5$, $1$, $1.5$ and $2$. Their discount factors are $0.98265$, $0.96561$, $0.94885$ and $0.93239$, which sum to $3.82951$. The interest on the principal is worth $1{,}000(1 - e^{-0.07}) = \$67.61$ today. So
>>$$H = \frac{67.61 + 85.27}{3.82951} = 17.65 + 22.27 = \$39.92$$
>>every six months, or $7.98\%$ a year. A risk-free par bond would pay $3.53\%$, and the put adds $4.45\%$.
>>
>>If the stock touches \$28 and ends at \$30, the note delivers 25 shares worth \$750. The four coupons add $4 \times 39.921 = \$159.69$, so the investor receives \$909.69, ignoring the time value of the coupons. A shareholder would have \$750 plus small dividends. The coupons cover only part of the stock's fall.
