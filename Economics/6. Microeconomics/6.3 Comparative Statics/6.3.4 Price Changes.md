>[!note] Price Changes
>A **price change** moves one price while the other price and income $M$ stay fixed. When $p_2$ falls to $p_2' < p_2$, the [[6.2.1 Budget Constraint|budget line]] tilts to $x_2 = M/p_2' - (p_1/p_2')x_1$. It pivots around the intercept of the other good, $M/p_1$ on the $x_1$ axis. The own intercept moves out from $M/p_2$ to $M/p_2'$, and the slope flattens from $-p_1/p_2$ to $-p_1/p_2'$. A change in $p_1$ instead pivots the line around $M/p_2$ on the $x_2$ axis. The **price-consumption path** is the curve traced by the optimal bundle as one price varies. The **demand curve** of good $i$ is the [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|Marshallian demand]] $x_i^*(p_i)$ as a function of its own price, holding $p_j$ and $M$ fixed. Economists draw it with the price $p_i$ on the vertical axis and the quantity $x_i$ on the horizontal axis.
>
>The **law of demand** states that $\partial x_i^*/\partial p_i < 0$, so demand falls when the good's own price rises. It holds in most cases, and the [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]] shows that it always holds for a [[6.3.3 Income Changes|normal good]]. Good $i$ is a **Giffen good** at $(\mathbf{p}, M)$ if
>$$\frac{\partial x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)}{\partial p_i} > 0,$$
>so its demand curve slopes up there. A Giffen good must be inferior. The standard example is potatoes in nineteenth-century Ireland.
>
>A change in the own price $p_i$ moves the consumer **along** the demand curve. A change in $p_j$ or $M$ **shifts** the whole curve. A rise in $M$ shifts it right if good $i$ is normal and left if it is inferior. A rise in $p_j$ shifts it right if $\partial x_i^*/\partial p_j > 0$ and left if $\partial x_i^*/\partial p_j < 0$. [[6.3.6 Complements and Substitutes]] names these two cases gross substitutes and gross complements. For [[6.4a Cobb-Douglas]] utility the demand curve $x_1^* = \alpha M/p_1$ is a hyperbola. Spending $p_1x_1^* = \alpha M$ does not change with the price, and the curve does not shift when $p_2$ changes.

>[!warning] Movement Along Versus Shift
>"Demand fell" can describe two different events. A movement along the curve comes from a change in the good's own price. A shift of the curve comes from a change in anything held fixed when the curve was drawn, here $p_j$ and $M$. A rise in $p_i$ does not shift the demand for good $i$. It moves the consumer up and to the left along an unchanged curve.

>[!warning] Giffen Goods Versus Veblen and Information Effects
>Some goods sell more at a higher price for reasons outside this model. A **Veblen good**, such as a luxury watch, is valued partly because it is expensive and signals status. A high price can also signal quality when buyers cannot judge quality directly. These effects are real. The model here cannot produce them, because utility depends only on quantities and prices enter only through the budget. A Giffen good has an upward-sloping demand curve even though preferences do not depend on prices at all.

# Intuition

A price cut differs from a raise in income. The consumer can afford more, as with a raise. The good that became cheaper is also cheaper relative to the other good, so the budget line rotates instead of shifting. The demand curve records the consumer's choice at every price. It turns tangencies in the $(x_1, x_2)$ plane into a curve in the $(x_i, p_i)$ plane.

The diagram builds a demand curve from tangencies. The consumer has $u = x_1^{1/2}x_2^{1/2}$, income $M = 8$, and $p_2 = 1$. In the top panel $p_1$ takes the values $4$, $2$, and $1$. The budget line pivots around $M/p_2 = 8$ on the $x_2$ axis, and the tangency moves right along the price-consumption path. The bottom panel plots each tangency's $x_1$ against the price that produced it, with $p_1$ on the vertical axis. The points lie on the demand curve $x_1^* = 4/p_1$. For this utility $x_2^* = M/(2p_2) = 4$ at every $p_1$, so the price-consumption path is horizontal.

# Properties

#### *(Reading the Graph)* With price on the vertical axis, a steep demand curve means demand barely responds to price.

The slope of the drawn curve is $dp_i/dx_i = 1/(\partial x_i^*/\partial p_i)$, the reciprocal of the derivative of demand. A nearly vertical curve means large price changes cause small quantity changes, as for insulin. A flat curve means demand reacts strongly. A demand function is written as $x_i^*$ in terms of $p_i$, but the drawn curve shows its inverse, $p_i$ in terms of $x_i$. For Cobb-Douglas, $x_1^* = \alpha M/p_1$ inverts to $p_1 = \alpha M/x_1$, the same hyperbola.

#### *(Shape of the Path)* The price-consumption path shows how the other good responds to the price change.

Follow the path as $p_1$ falls. If the path is flat, $x_2^*$ does not change, so $\partial x_2^*/\partial p_1 = 0$, as for Cobb-Douglas. If the path rises, the consumer buys more of good 2 as $p_1$ falls, so $\partial x_2^*/\partial p_1 < 0$. For [[6.4c Perfect Complements|perfect complements]] $\min(x_1, x_2)$ the path is the ray $x_2 = x_1$, which rises. If the path falls, the consumer buys less of good 2 as $p_1$ falls, so $\partial x_2^*/\partial p_1 > 0$. One picture of tangencies therefore gives both the demand curve for good 1 and the sign of a cross-price effect.

#### *(Spending and the Price)* Spending on a good can rise, stay constant, or fall when its price rises.

With Cobb-Douglas utility, spending $p_1x_1^* = \alpha M$ is the same at every price. A $10$ percent price rise divides the quantity by $1.1$, a fall of about $9.1$ percent. Spending on good 2, $(1-\alpha)M$, is also unaffected, which is why $x_2^*$ does not respond to $p_1$. With perfect complements, demand $x_1^* = M/(p_1 + p_2)$ falls by less than in proportion to the price. Spending on good 1, $Mp_1/(p_1 + p_2)$, then rises with $p_1$. For the CES demand in [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]] with $0 < \rho < 1$, demand falls by more than in proportion, and spending on good 1 falls. With two goods, spending on good 2 equals $M$ minus spending on good 1. So a rise in $p_1$ lowers $x_2^*$ exactly when it raises spending on good 1.

#### *(Giffen Requires Inferiority)* An upward-sloping demand curve needs an inferior good with a large budget share.

A rise in $p_i$ has two consequences. The good becomes dearer relative to other goods, which always lowers demand for it. The consumer also becomes poorer in real terms, and for an inferior good being poorer raises demand for it. Demand rises only if the second effect is the larger one. That requires the good to take a large share of the budget, so that the price rise makes the consumer much poorer. The [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]] turns this argument into an equation. Potatoes in nineteenth-century Ireland are the standard example. They were an inferior staple, and they took most of a poor household's budget.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Coffee Habit
>A student has utility $u = x_1^{0.25}x_2^{0.75}$ over cups of coffee $x_1$ and other goods $x_2$, with monthly income $M = 400$. The price of coffee rises from $p_1 = 4$ to $p_1 = 5$.
>
>>[!example]- How much coffee does the student buy at each price, and how much does the student spend on it? Does the demand curve for coffee shift if the price of other goods rises? What if income rises to $480$?
>>Cobb-Douglas demand is $x_1^* = 0.25 \cdot 400/p_1 = 100/p_1$. The student buys $25$ cups at $p_1 = 4$ and $20$ cups at $p_1 = 5$. Spending is $100$ both times. The price rise is a movement along the demand curve $x_1^* = 100/p_1$.
>>
>>The price $p_2$ does not appear in $x_1^* = 0.25M/p_1$, so a change in $p_2$ leaves the coffee demand curve where it is. A rise in income to $480$ does shift it, to $x_1^* = 120/p_1$. At $p_1 = 5$ the student would then buy $24$ cups.

>[!example] (Medium) Espresso and Milk
>A latte drinker always combines one espresso shot $x_1$ with one portion of milk $x_2$, so $u = \min(x_1, x_2)$. The weekly budget is $M = 30$ and milk costs $p_2 = 1$ per portion.
>
>>[!example]- Find the demand for shots at $p_1 = 2$ and at $p_1 = 4$. Then the milk price rises to $p_2 = 2$ while $p_1 = 2$. Which change is a movement along the demand curve for shots, and which is a shift?
>>The drinker buys equal amounts, $x_1 = x_2$, and spends $M = (p_1 + p_2)x_1$. So $x_1^* = 30/(p_1 + p_2)$. With $p_2 = 1$ the demand curve for shots is $x_1^* = 30/(p_1 + 1)$. At $p_1 = 2$ the drinker buys $10$ shots, and at $p_1 = 4$ the drinker buys $6$. This fall from $10$ to $6$ is a movement along the curve.
>>
>>When milk rises to $p_2 = 2$, the whole curve becomes $x_1^* = 30/(p_1 + 2)$. At $p_1 = 2$ demand falls from $10$ to $7.5$ shots, even though the price of shots did not change. This is a leftward shift, because $\partial x_1^*/\partial p_2 < 0$. Spending on shots is $20$ at $p_1 = 2$ and $24$ at $p_1 = 4$. It rises with the price, unlike the Cobb-Douglas case.

>[!example] (Hard) A Giffen Household
>A household in a poor farming region has $M = 150$ a month. It buys potatoes $x_1$ at $p_1 = 1$ per unit and meat $x_2$ at $p_2 = 3$ per unit. Each unit of either food feeds one person for one day, and the household needs at least $100$ units a month. Its preferences are as follows. Any bundle with at least $100$ units of food beats any bundle with less. Among bundles with enough food, more meat is better. These preferences are not continuous, but they make the mechanism easy to compute.
>
>>[!example]- Find the household's purchases at $p_1 = 1$ and at $p_1 = 1.2$. Is the potato a Giffen good here? Is it inferior?
>>The household buys as much meat as it can while still reaching $100$ units. Both constraints bind: $x_1 + x_2 = 100$ and $p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 = 150$. Substituting $x_1 = 100 - x_2$ into the budget gives
>>$$x_2^* = \frac{150 - 100p_1}{p_2 - p_1}, \qquad x_1^* = 100 - x_2^*.$$
>>At $p_1 = 1$ the household buys $x_2^* = 50/2 = 25$ units of meat and $x_1^* = 75$ units of potatoes. At $p_1 = 1.2$ it buys $x_2^* = 30/1.8 = 50/3 \approx 16.7$ units of meat and $x_1^* = 250/3 \approx 83.3$ units of potatoes. Spending is $1.2 \cdot 250/3 + 3 \cdot 50/3 = 100 + 50 = 150$.
>>
>>The potato price rose and the household bought more potatoes. Potatoes are a Giffen good at these prices. With general $M$, the solution is $x_1^* = 100 - (M - 100p_1)/(p_2 - p_1)$. Its derivatives are $\partial x_1^*/\partial p_1 = (100p_2 - M)/(p_2 - p_1)^2 > 0$ and $\partial x_1^*/\partial M = -1/(p_2 - p_1) < 0$. The first is positive whenever the household cannot afford an all-meat diet, $M < 100p_2$. The second shows that potatoes are inferior.
>>
>>Potatoes also take a large share of the budget: $75$ of $150$ at the old price and $100$ of $150$ at the new price. The price rise makes the household poorer. To keep reaching $100$ units, it gives up meat, the expensive food, and replaces it with the cheapest food, potatoes.
