>[!note] Entropy Statement of the Second Law
>The general form of the [[2.7.2 Second Law of Thermodynamics|second law]] is a statement about [[2.7.6 Entropy|entropy]]. For any process, add the entropy change of the system to the entropy change of everything it interacts with. The sum is never negative,
>$$\Delta S_{\text{system}} + \Delta S_{\text{environment}} \geq 0.$$
>The sum is zero for a [[2.7.3 Reversible Process|reversible]] process and positive for an irreversible one. The entropy of a system by itself can fall, as when a gas is cooled, but only if the environment's entropy rises by at least as much. The entropy of the universe, meaning the system and everything it touches, only ever increases or stays the same. Every real process is irreversible to some degree, so in practice it increases. Reversibility is a condition on the total. A reversible process can change the system's entropy. A cycle that returns the system's entropy to its start can still be irreversible.
>
>The statement contains the other forms of the law. Heat $Q$ flowing from $T_H$ to $T_L$ changes the total entropy by $Q/T_L - Q/T_H$, which is positive only when $T_H > T_L$. So heat flows from hot to cold and not the reverse, which is the Clausius statement. An engine that took $Q_H$ from one reservoir and made it all work would lower the reservoir's entropy by $Q_H/T_H$ and raise nothing else, so it cannot exist, which is the Kelvin-Planck statement. Applying the inequality to an engine between two reservoirs gives $Q_L/T_L \geq Q_H/T_H$ and therefore $e \leq 1 - T_L/T_H$, which is Carnot's theorem. The entropy statement is the one that turns the second law into a calculation.

# Intuition

Mixed things do not unmix on their own. A box with hot molecules on one side and cold ones on the other becomes a box of warm molecules. A box of warm molecules never sorts itself back into hot and cold. A classroom demonstration shows this with a box of balls. White balls on one side stand for hot water, and black balls on the other stand for cold water. Shaken, the balls mix. Shaken for as long as you like, they never return to white on one side and black on the other. Entropy is the number that tracks this. A process that increases the disorder of the whole is allowed and happens. A process that would decrease it is forbidden and never happens. A process that leaves it unchanged is the reversible limit, which real processes approach but do not reach.

The first law rules out processes that would create or destroy energy. The second law, in this form, rules out processes that would create order out of nothing. The system's own entropy is allowed to fall, as long as its surroundings gain at least as much. The sketch shows water freezing in a freezer. The water loses entropy. The kitchen gains more, because it receives $Q_H = Q_L + W$.

# Properties

#### *(The System Alone Can Lose Entropy)* The inequality constrains the total, not the parts.

Water freezing in a freezer loses $333\ \text{kJ}$ per kilogram at $273\ \text{K}$, an entropy drop of $1.2\ \text{kJ/K}$. The freezer pumps that heat, plus its own work, into the kitchen at $295\ \text{K}$, raising the kitchen's entropy by more than $1.2\ \text{kJ/K}$. The total rises. Life, crystals, and refrigerators all create local order, and all of them do it by exporting more disorder than they remove.

#### *(Equality Marks Reversibility)* A process with zero total entropy change is reversible, and this is the test used in practice.

Whether a process could run backward is hard to judge by inspection. Whether $\Delta S_{\text{total}}$ is zero is a calculation. This test is the second half of the definition of a [[2.7.3 Reversible Process|reversible process]]. The first half is that the process can be run backward. The Carnot cycle passes, because the hot reservoir loses $Q_H/T_H$, the cold reservoir gains $Q_L/T_L$, and the [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot relation]] $Q_L/Q_H = T_L/T_H$ makes the two equal. Heat flow across a finite temperature difference fails, as does free expansion, friction, and mixing. Any positive total is a measure of how far the process is from reversible, and of how much work was lost that a reversible route would have delivered.

#### *(Count the Environment)* A reversible process can change the system's entropy, and a system whose entropy returns to its start can still have gone through an irreversible process.

Heat a gas slowly at constant pressure. Its entropy rises by $\Delta S_{\text{sys}} = (\tfrac{d}{2} + 1) Nk \ln(T_2/T_1) > 0$. Suppose the heat comes from surroundings that are only slightly hotter than the gas at every moment. Then the surroundings lose exactly that much entropy, the total is zero, and the process is reversible. Running it backward returns the same heat to the surroundings. The opposite case is a cycle. The gas in any engine returns to its starting state, so $\Delta S_{\text{sys}} = 0$ every cycle. This says nothing about the environment. In the [[2.7.4a Otto Cycle|Otto cycle]], heat enters from burning fuel far hotter than the gas. Heat leaves to surroundings far colder than the exhaust. The environment's entropy rises every cycle, so the Otto cycle is irreversible. Among engines that run between two reservoirs, only the [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot cycle]] leaves the total unchanged.

#### *(One Reversible Refrigerator)* Many engines satisfy the inequality, but only one machine satisfies the equality, and it is the one that can be run backward.

An engine may create as much entropy as it likes, so there are infinitely many engines with infinitely many efficiencies. A [[2.7.5a Refrigerator|refrigerator]] made by reversing an engine must create no entropy at all, because running the pair forward and back must leave the universe unchanged. That single condition, $Q_H/T_H = Q_L/T_L$, fixes the ratio of the heats, so every reversible refrigerator between two reservoirs has the same coefficient of performance, $T_L/(T_H - T_L)$, and every reversible engine has the same efficiency, $1 - T_L/T_H$. This short argument shows that the Carnot value is unique.

#### *(Lost Work)* When an irreversible process creates entropy $\Delta S_{\text{total}}$, work equal to $T_L\, \Delta S_{\text{total}}$ has been wasted.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover lost work. This property follows Giancoli section 20.8, which is not on the syllabus.

Compare a real engine with a Carnot engine drawing the same $Q_H$. The real engine rejects extra heat $\Delta Q$ to the cold reservoir and delivers that much less work. Its entropy production is $\Delta Q / T_L$, so the lost work is $T_L$ times the entropy created. The colder the reservoir the heat is dumped into, the more work each unit of entropy costs. This relation is why engineers track entropy generation in a design: it is a ledger of wasted work.

#### *(Time's Direction)* The inequality picks out which way time runs for macroscopic events.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not connect entropy to the direction of time. This property follows Giancoli sections 20.7 and 20.8, which are not on the syllabus.

Every microscopic law is the same run backward, and a film of two molecules colliding looks right in reverse. A film of a glass shattering does not. The difference is the second law. Processes that raise the total entropy occur, their reverses do not, and that asymmetry is what distinguishes past from future in the macroscopic world. The universe as a whole is running toward higher entropy, and the usable energy in it, which is the energy that can still be converted to work, decreases as it does.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Is the Otto Cycle Reversible?
>The gas in a gasoline engine runs through the [[2.7.4a Otto Cycle|Otto cycle]]. It returns to its initial state at the end of every cycle.
>
>>[!example]- The gas has $\Delta S_{\text{gas}} = 0$ per cycle. Does that make the cycle reversible?
>>No. Entropy is a state function, so any gas that returns to its initial state has $\Delta S_{\text{gas}} = 0$, reversible or not. Reversibility needs the total, gas plus environment, to be zero. In the Otto cycle, heat enters on a constant-volume leg while the gas temperature climbs. It comes from burning fuel that is far hotter than the gas. Heat leaves on the other constant-volume leg to surroundings far colder than the exhaust. Heat crossing a finite temperature difference raises the total entropy, so $\Delta S_{\text{env}} > 0$ every cycle and the cycle is irreversible. The Carnot cycle avoids this. It takes in and gives out heat only on isotherms at the reservoir temperatures, so its total is zero.

>[!example] (Medium) Heating a Gas Two Ways
>One mole of a monatomic ideal gas ($d = 3$) is heated at constant pressure from $300\ \text{K}$ to $400\ \text{K}$. In case (a) the heat comes from a long series of reservoirs used in turn. Each is only slightly hotter than the gas. In case (b) it comes from a single reservoir at $400\ \text{K}$.
>

>}

>\node at (6.6,-0.55) {one reservoir, $400\ \mathrm{K}$};
>\end{tikzpicture}
>\end{document}

>
>>[!example]- Find the total entropy change in each case.
>>The gas goes between the same two states in both cases, so its entropy change is the same. With $Nk = R = 8.314\ \text{J/K}$ for one mole,
>>$$\Delta S_{\text{gas}} = \left(\tfrac{d}{2} + 1\right) Nk \ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} = (2.5)(8.314)\ln\frac{400}{300} = 5.98\ \text{J/K}.$$
>>The heat absorbed is $Q = (\tfrac{d}{2} + 1) Nk\, \Delta T = (2.5)(8.314)(100) = 2079\ \text{J}$ in both cases.
>>In case (a) each reservoir gives up heat at almost the gas's own temperature. The reservoirs together lose the same $5.98\ \text{J/K}$ that the gas gains. The total is zero, and the process is reversible, even though $\Delta S_{\text{gas}} \neq 0$.
>>In case (b) all of the heat leaves one reservoir at $400\ \text{K}$,
>>$$\Delta S_{\text{env}} = -\frac{2079}{400} = -5.20\ \text{J/K}, \qquad \Delta S_{\text{total}} = 5.98 - 5.20 = +0.78\ \text{J/K}.$$
>>The total is positive, so case (b) is irreversible. The heat crossed a finite temperature difference, up to $100\ \text{K}$ at the start.

>[!example] (Medium) A Real Engine Versus Carnot
>An engine takes $1000\ \text{J}$ from a reservoir at $500\ \text{K}$ and rejects $700\ \text{J}$ to a reservoir at $300\ \text{K}$ each cycle.
>

>\draw[thick] (1.8,2.4) circle (0.68);
>\node at (1.8,2.4) {engine};
>\draw[->,very thick,hot] (1.8,3.95) -- (1.8,3.12) node[midway,right] {$Q_H = 1000\ \mathrm{J}$};
>\draw[->,very thick,cold] (1.8,1.68) -- (1.8,0.85) node[midway,right] {$Q_L = 700\ \mathrm{J}$};
>\draw[->,very thick,grn] (2.52,2.4) -- (3.6,2.4) node[right] {$W$};
>\end{tikzpicture}
>\end{document}
>```
>
>>[!example]- Find the entropy change of the universe per cycle and the work a Carnot engine would have delivered from the same heat.
>>The engine itself returns to its starting state, so its entropy is unchanged. The reservoirs give
>>$$\Delta S_{\text{total}} = -\frac{1000}{500} + \frac{700}{300} = -2.00 + 2.33 = +0.33\ \text{J/K}.$$
>>The engine delivers $300\ \text{J}$ and has $e = 0.30$. A Carnot engine has $e = 1 - 300/500 = 0.40$ and would deliver $400\ \text{J}$, rejecting only $600\ \text{J}$. The $100\ \text{J}$ of lost work equals $T_L\, \Delta S_{\text{total}} = (300)(0.33) = 100\ \text{J}$, as the lost-work rule predicts.

>Lecture stated that no engine beats the Carnot efficiency but did not derive it. This derivation from the entropy inequality is standard textbook material.

>[!example] (Hard) Deriving the Carnot Limit from Entropy
>An engine runs in cycles between reservoirs at $T_H$ and $T_L$, taking in $Q_H$ and rejecting $Q_L$.
>
>>[!example]- Show that its efficiency cannot exceed $1 - T_L / T_H$.
>>Over a cycle the working substance returns to its initial state, so $\Delta S_{\text{engine}} = 0$. The hot reservoir loses $Q_H / T_H$ and the cold reservoir gains $Q_L / T_L$. The entropy statement requires
>>$$-\frac{Q_H}{T_H} + \frac{Q_L}{T_L} \geq 0, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \frac{Q_L}{Q_H} \geq \frac{T_L}{T_H}.$$
>>The efficiency is $e = 1 - Q_L / Q_H$, and a larger $Q_L / Q_H$ means a smaller $e$, so
>>$$e \leq 1 - \frac{T_L}{T_H},$$
>>with equality only when the total entropy change is zero, which is the reversible case. Carnot's theorem, proved in the [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot note]] by an argument about coupled engines, is here a two-line consequence of the entropy inequality. This is what makes the entropy statement the working form of the second law.
