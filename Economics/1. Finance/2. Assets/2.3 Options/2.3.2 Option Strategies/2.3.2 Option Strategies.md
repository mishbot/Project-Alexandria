>[!note] Option Strategies
>An **option strategy** is a position built from several European options on the same stock, sometimes together with the stock itself. Each leg is a [[2.3.1a Option Contract|vanilla]] call or put, held long or short. Six strategies cover the common views on a stock: the [[2.3.2a Vertical Spread|vertical spread]], the [[2.3.2b Collar|collar]], the [[2.3.2c Straddle|straddle]], the [[2.3.2d Strangle|strangle]], the [[2.3.2e Butterfly Spread|butterfly spread]], and the [[2.3.2f Calendar Spread|calendar spread]].
>
>The strategies share one description. When every leg expires at the same date $T$, the payoff is a sum of call and put payoffs. It is therefore a piecewise-linear function of the stock price $S_T$, with kinks only at the strikes. A long call adds $+1$ to the slope for $S_T$ above its strike. A long put adds $-1$ to the slope for $S_T$ below its strike. A long share adds $+1$ to the slope everywhere, and short legs reverse each sign. Each strategy also has a **net premium** today: the premiums paid for long legs minus the premiums received for short legs. A positive net premium is a cost, and a negative one is a credit. The profit at $T$ is the payoff minus the net premium, ignoring interest on the premium. The calendar spread is the exception to this description. Its legs expire at two dates $T_1 < T_2$, so its value at $T_1$ includes an option that is still alive.
>
>A trader picks a strategy from a view on the direction and the size of the next move. A bullish or bearish view, with a capped cost and a capped gain, suits a vertical spread. Protection for a share already owned, at low or zero cost, suits a collar. A large move in either direction suits a straddle, or a strangle for less premium. A small move suits a butterfly spread. A view on the volatility term structure (how the volatility implied by option prices changes with expiry) suits a calendar spread. So does the wish to fund a long-dated option by selling a short-dated one.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The butterfly spread and the short straddle do not appear in the lecture notes. The homework uses the butterfly only to prove a bound on call prices.

# Intuition

A single long call is the simplest bet that a stock will rise. Its payoff has no upper limit, and its premium pays for all of it. A trader often has a narrower view. Most of the strategies give up a part of the payoff the trader does not expect to need, and this lowers the cost. The bull call spread sells the gains above a second strike $K_2$. The collar sells the share's gains above $K_2$ to pay for a put at $K_1$. The strangle moves the call and put strikes away from the stock price, so it gives up the payoff from small moves. The butterfly sells two middle calls and keeps only a narrow peak.

The slope rule makes every shape easy to build. Start from the slope at the far left. Then move right and add the slope change of each leg at its strike. Any piecewise-linear payoff with kinks at traded strikes can be built this way from calls, a share, and a zero-coupon bond. The strategies below are the simplest cases of this construction.

# Comparison Table

Prices $c_i$ and $p_i$ are the call and put premiums at strike $K_i$, and $S_0$ is the stock price today. Max gain and max loss include the net premium and ignore interest on it. For the collar they are measured from a share bought at $S_0$.

| Strategy | Legs | Payoff shape | Max gain | Max loss | Net premium | View |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [[2.3.2a Vertical Spread]] (bull call) | long $K_1$ call, short $K_2$ call, $K_1 < K_2$ | flat at $0$, rises with slope $1$, flat at $K_2 - K_1$ | $K_2 - K_1 - (c_1 - c_2)$ | $c_1 - c_2$ | paid; the bull put version receives $p_2 - p_1$ | moderate rise; the bear versions bet on a fall |
| [[2.3.2b Collar]] | long share, long $K_1$ put, short $K_2$ call | flat at $K_1$, rises with slope $1$, flat at $K_2$ | $K_2 - S_0 - (p_1 - c_2)$ | $S_0 - K_1 + (p_1 - c_2)$ | $p_1 - c_2$, often set to zero | protect a share already owned |
| [[2.3.2c Straddle]] | long $K_0$ call, long $K_0$ put | V with its vertex at $K_0$ | unlimited | $c + p$, at $S_T = K_0$ | paid | large move, direction unknown |
| [[2.3.2d Strangle]] | long $K_2$ call, long $K_1$ put | V with a flat bottom on $[K_1, K_2]$ | unlimited | $c_2 + p_1$, for $K_1 \le S_T \le K_2$ | paid, less than a straddle | very large move, direction unknown |
| [[2.3.2e Butterfly Spread]] | long $K_1$ and $K_3$ calls, short two $K_2$ calls | tent with its peak at $K_2$ | $K_2 - K_1 - D$ | $D = c_1 + c_3 - 2c_2$ | paid, never negative | small move, stock ends near $K_2$ |
| [[2.3.2f Calendar Spread]] | short $T_1$ call, long $T_2$ call, same $K$ | smooth peak at $K$, at time $T_1$ | depends on the volatility at $T_1$ | $c(T_2) - c(T_1)$ | paid | stock near $K$ at $T_1$; long-dated options stay expensive |

# When to Use Which

Start with direction. A trader who expects a moderate rise buys a bull spread, and one who expects a moderate fall buys a bear spread. The trader then picks calls or puts by the cash flow wanted today. A bull call spread costs money now, and a bull put spread brings money in now. Once interest is counted, the two have the same profit at expiry. A trader who already owns the share and wants protection uses a collar. In a zero-cost collar, the call premium pays for the put.

If the direction is unknown, the size of the expected move decides. A trader who expects a large move buys a straddle with its strike near the stock price. A trader who expects an even larger move, or wants to pay less, buys a strangle. The strangle needs a larger move to break even. A trader who expects the stock to stay near its current price buys a butterfly centered there. Selling a straddle takes the same view, but its loss has no limit, while the butterfly caps the loss at its premium.

A view about time leads to the calendar spread. It gains most when the stock sits near the strike at the first expiry. It also gains when the market still prices the long-dated option at a high volatility at that date. Its cost is only the difference between two premiums, because the short-dated call pays for part of the long-dated one.
