>[!note] Acceleration
>**Average acceleration** is the rate at which an object’s [[1.1.1. Speed and Velocity|velocity]] changes over a time interval. It is a vector that points in the direction of the velocity change, defined as
>$$\vec{a}_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\vec{v} - \vec{v_{0}}}{t - t_{0}} = \frac{\Delta \vec{v}}{\Delta t},$$
>where $\vec{v}_0$ is the initial velocity and $\vec{v}$ is the final velocity at time $t$.
>
>On the other hand, **instantaneous acceleration** is the object’s acceleration at a specific moment in time. It is defined as the derivative of velocity with respect to time, such that
>$$\vec{a} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta \vec{v}}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt}.$$
>
>If acceleration stays **constant** during a time interval, we can use the following equations to describe motion:
>$$
>\begin{align}
>v &= v_0 + at && \enspace \text{(Velocity)} \\
>x &= x_0+v_0t+\frac{1}{2}at^2 && \enspace \text{(Displacement)} \\
>v^2 &= v_0^2+2a(x-x_0) && \enspace \text{(Velocity-Displacement)}
>\end{align}
>$$

# Derivation
Let's derive the equations for motion at constant acceleration:
1. Per the definition of instantaneous acceleration, $a=\dfrac{dv}{dt}$.  By integrating, we get $v=\large\int adt=at+C$. The constant $C=v_{0}$ only works if $a$ is constant and can be pulled out of the integral.
2. We start with $v=\dfrac{dx}{dt}=v_{0}+at$ and integrate to obtain $x = \large\int (v_{0}+at) dt=v_{0}t+\frac{1}{2}at^2+C$.
3. This equation combines the above and eliminates time.

# Examples
>[!example] Colliding Balls
>Thelma stands on top of a building whose height is $H$ and throws a ball upward. The speed of the ball when it leaves her hand is $v_{T}$ . Bob is standing at the bottom of the building and at the same instant throws another ball upwards with speed $v_{B}$. 
>>[!example]- What is the slowest that $v_{B}$ can be if Bob’s ball hits Thelma’s ball when still on the way up?
>>Since Bob and Thelma are trying to collide the two balls that are strictly moving in the $y$ direction, then there must be a point where $x_{B}(t)=x_{T}(t)$, such that $v_{T}-gt > 0$. 
>>
>>We solve the equality:
>>$$H+v_{T}t-\dfrac{1}{2}gt^2=v_{B}t-\dfrac{1}{2}gt^2 \iff t=\dfrac{H}{v_{B}-v_{T}}.$$
>>
>>Then, we satisfy the constraint, such that $v_{T}-g\dfrac{H}{v_{B}-v_{T}}>0 \iff v_{B} > \dfrac{gH+v_{T}^2}{v_{T}}.$
>>
>>So Bob's ball needs to have a starting speed of at least $\dfrac{gH+v_{T}^2}{v_{T}}$.

>>[!example]- Find the initial speed of the object $B$.
>>Since object $B$ is moving up the ramp, it has an acceleration in its $x$-direction of $g\sin\theta$ (and therefore an acceleration in its $y$-direction of $g\cos\theta$, however we only need the $x$-direction here). Draw a plot with $g$, $x$ and $y$ to convince yourself
>>
>>Object $A$ has the exact same acceleration, and starts from rest. In order for them to meet in the middle, we deduce that the time of the collision is $v_{B_{0}}-(g\sin\theta)t=0 \iff t=\dfrac{v_{B_{0}}}{g\sin\theta}$. 
>>
>>Then, we equate the two positions, such that
>>$$x_{A_{0}}-\left(\frac{1}{2}g\sin\theta\right)t^2=v_{B_{0}}t-\left(\frac{1}{2}g\sin\theta\right)t^2 \iff x_{A_{0}}=v_{B_{0}}t.$$
>>
>>Notice that we can express the starting displacement of $A$ as $x_{A_{0}}=\dfrac{h}{\sin\theta}$ if we assume that $B$ starts at the origin. Plugging the values for $t$ and $x_{A_{0}}$ into our equation, we obtain $v_{B}=\sqrt{ gh }$.
>
>>[!example]- What are the speeds of $A$ and $B$ when they meet?
>>Given that $A$ and $B$ meet in the center of the hypotenuse with length $\dfrac{h}{\sin\theta}$, their ending position will be $x=\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{h}{\sin\theta}$.
>>
>>As such, the final speed of object $A$ right before the collision is 
>>$$\begin{align}v_{A}&=\sqrt{ 0-2g\sin\theta\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{h}{\sin\theta} \right) }\\&=\sqrt{gh}.\end{align}$$
>>
>>Meanwhile, object $B$ will have a speed of 
>>$$\begin{align}v_{B}&=\sqrt{ gh - 2g\sin\theta \cdot \dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{h}{\sin\theta} } \\ &= \sqrt{ gh - gh } \\ &= 0.\end{align}$$

>[!example] Dropping a Ball
>Suppose a ball is dropped from the top of a building from rest. In the last $\tau$ seconds before the ball hits the ground, the ball covers the last $1/3$ of the building’s height.
>>[!example]- How much time does it take the ball to hit the ground?
>>Let $t_{f}$ be the total time the ball takes to hit the ground, and let $h$ be the height of the building. Since the ball is dropped from rest, the total height is $h = \dfrac{1}{2} g t_f^2$.
>>
>>Consider the origin to be the final destination of the ball. Then, the ball is $\frac{1}{3}h$ away from the origin in the positive direction after $t_{f}-\tau$ seconds. As such, the distance covered in the last $\tau$ seconds is $\dfrac{1}{3}h = h-\dfrac{1}{2}g(t_{f}-\tau)^2$.
>>
>>Solving for $h$ in the latter equation,  $h=\dfrac{3}{4}g(t_{f}^2-2t_{f}\tau+\tau^2)$.
>>
>>Plugging into the first equation, $\dfrac{3}{4}g(t_{f}^2-2t_{f}\tau+\tau^2)-\dfrac{1}{2}gt_{f}^2=0$.
>>
>>This equation yields a solution $t_{f}=\tau(3 \pm \sqrt{ 6 })$. The only solution here is $t_{f}=\tau(3+\sqrt{ 6 })$, since $3-\sqrt{ 6 }<1$.
