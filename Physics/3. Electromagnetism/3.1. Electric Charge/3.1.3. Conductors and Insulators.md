>[!note] Conductors and Insulators
>Materials are sorted by how freely their electrons move:
>- In a **conductor**, such as a metal, each atom gives up its outermost electrons, called **valence electrons**. These **free electrons** wander through the whole object. The positive ions they leave behind stay fixed in place. A metal is a lattice of positive ions inside a sea of free electrons.
>- In an **insulator**, such as plastic, glass, rubber, or fur, every electron stays bound. It stays with its own atom or with the bond between two atoms. Plastic is made of long chain molecules held together by covalent bonds, which share their electrons tightly.
>
>Aluminum is a conductor. Each aluminum atom has three valence electrons, and all three become free in the metal. Plastic is an insulator.
>
>The two kinds of material handle extra charge differently:
>- Extra charge placed on a conductor spreads over the whole object. Like charges repel, and the electrons are free to move apart.
>- Extra charge placed on an insulator stays at the spot where it was put. Rubbing one end of a plastic rod charges only that end.
>- A force on a free electron makes it move. A nearby charge therefore rearranges the electrons of a conductor, but only slightly distorts the atoms of an insulator.
>
>Charging by rubbing uses insulators, because they hold the transferred electrons in place.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover semiconductors, such as silicon, whose free-electron count lies between those of conductors and insulators. Giancoli section 21.3 mentions them.

# Intuition

In mechanics the material of an object did not matter. A rod was described by its mass, which sets its inertia. Its length and shape set its rotational inertia. In electromagnetism the material matters a great deal. An aluminum rod and a plastic rod of the same mass respond to charge in very different ways.

The outer electrons of each atom explain the difference. In a metal these electrons are loosely held. When many metal atoms pack together, the outer electrons need less energy to leave their atoms. They spread over the whole metal. A piece of metal then holds a fixed array of positive ions with free electrons moving among them. Push on any free electron and it moves. In plastic the electrons are bound in the bonds that hold each molecule together. Push on one and it shifts only slightly within its atom. Getting charge onto plastic takes rubbing, and the charge then stays on the rubbed spot.

# Properties

#### *(Valence Electrons Decide)* The way an element holds its outermost electrons decides whether its solid conducts.

The first three elements show the pattern. Hydrogen has a single electron. Helium has two electrons that fill its first shell. It is a noble gas, holds both electrons very tightly, and does not form a metal. At ordinary pressure helium does not even form a solid, and it becomes a liquid only near absolute zero. Lithium has three electrons, two in the inner shell and one valence electron outside it. Packed together, lithium atoms release that outer electron and form a metal. Lithium and hydrogen both have one valence electron. Under extreme pressure, solid hydrogen is predicted to become a metal as well.

#### *(Positive Metals)* A positive metal object has fewer electrons than protons, and its ions stay in place.

A metal turns positive when some of its free electrons are removed. The remaining free electrons rearrange, and the deficit spreads over the object. The ions stay where they are. The positive charge appears to spread, but only the electrons moved. The note [[3.3.3. Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium]] shows where the final charge sits on any charged conductor.

#### *(Sharing Between Spheres)* Two identical metal spheres that touch end with equal charges, each half of their combined charge.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover charge sharing by contact. This property follows Giancoli section 21.2 and the problems at the end of chapter 21.

While the spheres touch, free electrons move between them until neither sphere is favored. Identical spheres look the same, so they end with equal shares. [[3.1.2. Conservation of Charge|Conservation of charge]] fixes the total $Q_A + Q_B$, so the final charges $Q_A'$ and $Q_B'$ are
$$Q_A' = Q_B' = \frac{Q_A + Q_B}{2}.$$
The rule needs identical spheres. A large sphere touching a small one takes the larger share. The rule also needs conductors, because an insulator cannot pass its charge along.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Charging One End
>A student rubs only the left end of a plastic straw with fur. Then she rubs only the left end of an aluminum straw, held on a plastic handle.
>
>>[!example]- Where does the charge sit on each straw afterward?
>>On the plastic straw the charge stays at the left end, because the plastic holds its electrons in place. On the aluminum straw the charge spreads over the whole straw. Its free electrons repel each other and move apart. The plastic handle keeps the charge from leaving the aluminum through the student's hand.

>[!example] (Medium) Counting Free Electrons
>Aluminum has a density of $2.70\ \text{g/cm}^3$, a molar mass of $26.98\ \text{g/mol}$, and three free electrons per atom.
>
>>[!example]- How many free electrons does one cubic centimeter of aluminum hold, and what is their total charge?
>>One cubic centimeter has a mass of $2.70\ \text{g}$, or $2.70 / 26.98 = 0.100\ \text{mol}$ of atoms. With three free electrons per atom,
>>$$N = 3 \times 0.100\ \text{mol} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1} = 1.81 \times 10^{23}.$$
>>Their total charge is
>>$$Q = -N e = -(1.81 \times 10^{23})(1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}) = -2.90 \times 10^{4}\ \text{C}.$$
>>The ions carry the same amount of positive charge, so the block is neutral.

>[!example] (Hard) How Much Does the Electron Count Change?
>A $1.00\ \text{cm}^3$ aluminum cube is given a charge of $-1.0\ \text{nC}$. Use the free-electron count from the previous example.
>
>>[!example]- By what fraction does the number of free electrons in the cube change?
>>The added electrons number
>>$$\Delta N = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{C}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}} = 6.24 \times 10^{9}.$$
>>The fraction is
>>$$\frac{\Delta N}{N} = \frac{6.24 \times 10^{9}}{1.81 \times 10^{23}} = 3.5 \times 10^{-14}.$$
>>A charged conductor differs from a neutral one by a tiny change in its electron count. The extra electrons still spread over the cube, because each one is free to move.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The next two examples use charge sharing between touching spheres, which lecture did not cover.

>[!example] (Medium) Two Touches
>Identical metal spheres A, B, and C start with charges $+6.0\ \mu\text{C}$, $-2.0\ \mu\text{C}$, and $0$. Sphere A touches B and is pulled away. Then B touches C and is pulled away.
>
>>[!example]- Find the final charge on each sphere.
>>After the first touch, A and B share $+6.0 - 2.0 = +4.0\ \mu\text{C}$, so each holds $+2.0\ \mu\text{C}$. After the second touch, B and C share $+2.0 + 0 = +2.0\ \mu\text{C}$, so each holds $+1.0\ \mu\text{C}$. The final charges are A $= +2.0$, B $= +1.0$, and C $= +1.0\ \mu\text{C}$. Their sum is $+4.0\ \mu\text{C}$, the same as the starting sum.

>[!example] (Hard) Reading Back the Start
>Two identical metal spheres carry unknown charges $Q_A$ and $Q_B$. They are touched together and separated, and each then holds $+3.0\ \mu\text{C}$. Before the touch, A carried $-1.0\ \mu\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the starting charge of B, and the number of electrons that moved during the touch.
>>Conservation gives $Q_A + Q_B = 2 \times 3.0 = 6.0\ \mu\text{C}$, so $Q_B = 6.0 - (-1.0) = +7.0\ \mu\text{C}$. Sphere A went from $-1.0$ to $+3.0\ \mu\text{C}$, a change of $+4.0\ \mu\text{C}$. Only electrons move, so A lost electrons to B. Their number is
>>$$N = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}} = 2.5 \times 10^{13}.$$
>>Sphere B went from $+7.0$ to $+3.0\ \mu\text{C}$, a change of $-4.0\ \mu\text{C}$, which matches.
