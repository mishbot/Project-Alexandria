>[!note] Forward Price
>The **forward price** $F_0$ of an asset is the delivery price that makes a new [[2.2.2a Forward Contract|forward contract]] worth zero today. When the contract starts, its delivery price $K$ is set equal to $F_0$, so neither side pays anything to enter. Let $S_0$ be the spot price today, $T$ the time to delivery in years, and $r$ the constant, continuously compounded risk-free rate. No arbitrage then fixes the forward price of an investment asset. For an asset with no income, with known cash dividends, and with a continuous dividend yield $q$, in that order,
>$$F_0 = S_0e^{rT}, \qquad F_0 = \big(S_0 - \text{PV}(D)\big)e^{rT}, \qquad F_0 = S_0e^{(r - q)T}.$$
>Here $\text{PV}(D) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} D_i e^{-rt_i}$ is the present value of the cash dividends $D_i$ paid at times $t_i < T$. If the yield is a known function of time $q_t$, the term $qT$ is replaced by $\int_0^T q_s\,ds$. A forward on a foreign currency has the same form as the yield case, with the foreign interest rate in place of $q$.
>
>The formulas assume a frictionless market: borrowing and lending at $r$, free short selling, and no transaction costs, taxes, or counterparty risk. They also assume an investment asset, one held for its return that can be borrowed and sold short. A quoted forward price above the formula allows a cash-and-carry arbitrage: sell the forward, borrow, and buy the asset. A quote below it allows the reverse: buy the forward, short the asset, and lend the proceeds. The delivery price stays fixed for the life of the contract, while the contract's value moves with $S_t$; [[2.2.2c Value of a Forward Contract]] covers that value. [[2.2.4 Cash-and-Carry Arbitrage]] extends the formula to storage and other carry costs.

>[!warning] Forward Price Versus Forward Value
>Some texts write $K$ for the forward price and $F_t$ for the value of the forward contract at time $t$. These notes write $F_0$ for the forward price, $K$ for the delivery price written into the contract, and $f$ for the value of the contract. At inception $K = F_0$ and $f = 0$.

# Intuition

A forward lets the buyer get the asset at $T$ for a price fixed today. The buyer has a second way to own the asset at $T$: borrow $S_0$ today, buy the asset, and hold it. Both routes end with the asset at $T$, and neither costs anything today. So the forward price must equal what the second route costs at $T$, which is the loan balance $S_0e^{rT}$. Any income the asset pays before $T$ goes to whoever holds it. The forward buyer does not receive that income, so the forward price is lower by the value of the income at $T$.

# Properties

#### *(Cost of Carry)* The forward price is the spot price plus the cost of financing the asset, minus the income the asset pays.

In $F_0 = S_0e^{(r - q)T}$ the rate $r$ is the cost of the money tied up in the asset. The yield $q$ is the income that the asset holder receives and the forward holder gives up. When $q > r$, the forward price is below the spot price. For an asset with no income, $F_0 = S_0 + S_0(e^{rT} - 1)$, and the second term is the financing part of the **carry**. [[2.2.4 Cash-and-Carry Arbitrage]] solves the no-income formula for the rate implied by a quoted forward price, the implied repo rate.

#### *(Independent of Expectations)* The forward price does not depend on where traders expect the asset price to be at $T$.

The replication uses only $S_0$, $r$, the income, and $T$. The expected return of the asset, the probability of a rise, and attitudes to risk never enter it. Two traders who disagree about the future still agree on $F_0$, because either one can lock in a profit at any other price. A forward price above the spot price therefore says that the interest rate exceeds the dividend yield. It says nothing about whether the market expects the price to rise.

#### *(Changing Rates and Yields)* A yield that changes over time enters through its total over the contract, and each dividend is discounted at its own rate.

If the yield is a known function $q_t$ and every dividend is reinvested in the asset, one unit grows to $\exp\big(\int_0^T q_s\,ds\big)$ units by $T$. A portfolio that starts with $\exp\big(-\int_0^T q_s\,ds\big)$ units therefore ends with exactly one, and
$$F_0 = S_0\exp\Big(rT - \int_0^T q_s\,ds\Big).$$
The integral is the total yield earned over the contract. For a yield that is constant within each month, it is the sum of each month's annual yield times $1/12$. When the yield curve is not flat, each dividend is discounted at its own zero rate $r_i$, the continuously compounded rate on a loan from today to $t_i$. Then $\text{PV}(D) = \sum_i D_i e^{-r_i t_i}$. The reverse arbitrage borrows $D_i$ at the future date $t_i$ to pay the dividend owed on the short share. That loan rate is unknown today. To keep the final payoff certain, the trader fixes it today with a forward rate agreement (FRA), a contract that sets the interest rate on a future loan.

#### *(Short Positions Pay the Income)* A short seller owes the asset's income to the lender, so the reverse arbitrage must fund every payout.

Cash dividends paid while a share is on loan belong to the lender. In the reverse trade for cash dividends, the short seller borrows $D_i$ at each $t_i$ to pay the lender. The cash left at $T$ is the short-sale proceeds with interest minus these loans with interest,
$$S_0e^{rT} - \sum_{i=1}^{n} D_ie^{r(T - t_i)} = \big(S_0 - \text{PV}(D)\big)e^{rT}.$$
This is the forward price, so the reverse trade profits exactly when the quoted price is below it. With a continuous yield $q$, the lender is repaid in shares instead of cash. A trader who borrows $x$ shares at time $0$ returns $xe^{qT}$ shares at $T$, the holding the lender would have had after reinvesting. The reverse trade therefore shorts only $e^{-qT}$ shares, and the one share bought through the forward repays the loan exactly. The reverse trade needs an asset that can be borrowed. Many commodities are hard to borrow. For them the cash-and-carry trade still caps the forward price. Nothing enforces the floor, unless a long-term owner sells from inventory and buys back through the forward.

#### *(Foreign Currency)* A forward exchange rate follows the yield formula with the foreign interest rate in place of $q$.

Let $X_0$ be the spot exchange rate in dollars per unit of foreign currency, $r_d$ the dollar interest rate, and $r_f$ the foreign interest rate, both continuously compounded. One unit of foreign currency earns $r_f$ abroad, the way a share earns its dividend yield. Converting today and earning $r_d$ must give the same dollars at $T$ as earning $r_f$ and converting at the forward rate $F_0$,
$$X_0e^{r_dT} = e^{r_fT}F_0, \qquad F_0 = X_0e^{(r_d - r_f)T}.$$
This relation is **covered interest parity**. The forward points $F_0 - X_0$ are positive (a premium) when $r_d > r_f$ and negative (a discount) when $r_d < r_f$. [[2.2.2d Covered Interest Parity]] gives the arbitrage trades in both directions and the value of a currency forward.

# Derivation

We want the delivery price $K$ that gives a new forward a value of zero, in each of the three cases. Let $f$ be the value today of a long forward with delivery price $K$.

With no income, form two portfolios today. Portfolio A holds the long forward and cash $Ke^{-rT}$ invested at $r$. Portfolio B holds one share. At $T$, A is worth $(S_T - K) + K = S_T$ and B is worth $S_T$. Neither has a cash flow before $T$, so by the [[4.2.2a Arbitrage|law of one price]] they cost the same today,
$$f + Ke^{-rT} = S_0.$$
Setting $f = 0$ gives $K = S_0e^{rT}$, the first formula. If a quoted $K$ differs, one of the two trades below makes a risk-free profit at $T$ from a zero position today.

| | $K > S_0e^{rT}$ | $K < S_0e^{rT}$ |
|---|---|---|
| At $t = 0$ | Short the forward, borrow $S_0$, buy one share | Long the forward, short one share, lend $S_0$ |
| At $T$ | Deliver the share for $K$, repay $S_0e^{rT}$ | Pay $K$ for a share, return it, collect $S_0e^{rT}$ |
| Profit at $T$ | $K - S_0e^{rT}$ | $S_0e^{rT} - K$ |

With cash dividends, replace B by B', which holds one share and borrows $\text{PV}(D)$. The borrowing is split into loans of $D_ie^{-rt_i}$ maturing at each $t_i$, so each dividend repays one loan. B' is worth $S_T$ at $T$ and costs $S_0 - \text{PV}(D)$ today. Hence $f + Ke^{-rT} = S_0 - \text{PV}(D)$, and $f = 0$ gives the second formula. The cash-and-carry trade follows the timeline above. The reverse trade borrows each $D_i$ at $t_i$, as in the property on short positions.

With a dividend yield $q$, replace B by B'', which holds $e^{-qT}$ shares and reinvests every dividend in more shares. The share count grows at rate $q$, so B'' holds one share worth $S_T$ at $T$. It costs $S_0e^{-qT}$ today. Hence $f + Ke^{-rT} = S_0e^{-qT}$, and $f = 0$ gives $K = S_0e^{(r - q)T}$, the third formula. The arbitrage trades are the same as in the table, with $e^{-qT}$ shares in place of one share and $S_0e^{(r - q)T}$ in place of $S_0e^{rT}$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Forward on a Stock With No Dividends
>A stock trades at $\$62$ and pays no dividends in the next nine months. The risk-free rate is $4.5\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A nine-month long forward contract on one share is entered into today.
>
>>[!example]- Find the forward price, the initial value of the contract, and the long side's payoff if the stock ends at $\$70$.
>>The forward price is
>>$$F_0 = 62e^{0.045 \times 0.75} = 62e^{0.03375} = \$64.13.$$
>>The delivery price is set to $K = \$64.13$, so the contract is worth $f = S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = 62 - 64.13e^{-0.03375} = 0$ at inception. Neither side pays to enter. If the stock ends at $\$70$, the long side pays $\$64.13$ for a share worth $\$70$ and gains $70 - 64.13 = \$5.87$. The short side loses the same amount.

>[!example] (Medium) Two Cash Dividends
>A stock trades at $\$55$. It will pay a dividend of $\$0.90$ in two months and another $\$0.90$ in eight months. The risk-free rate is $6\%$ per year with continuous compounding for all maturities. A dealer sells a ten-month forward contract on one share.
>
>>[!example]- Find the forward price, and list the loans the dealer takes to hedge the contract.
>>The present value of the dividends is
>>$$\text{PV}(D) = 0.90e^{-0.06 \times 2/12} + 0.90e^{-0.06 \times 8/12} = 0.891 + 0.865 = \$1.756.$$
>>The forward price is
>>$$F_0 = (55 - 1.756)\,e^{0.06 \times 10/12} = 53.244\,e^{0.05} = \$55.97.$$
>>The dealer borrows $\$0.891$ for two months, $\$0.865$ for eight months, and $\$53.244$ for ten months. The three loans add up to $\$55.00$, the price of one share, which the dealer buys. Each dividend of $\$0.90$ repays one of the first two loans with interest. At ten months the third loan has grown to $53.244e^{0.05} = \$55.97$, which the forward buyer pays for the share. Every cash flow nets to zero.
>>
>>Without dividends the forward price would be $55e^{0.05} = \$57.82$. The dividends lower it by $\$1.85$, the value of the two dividends carried forward to the delivery date.

>[!example] (Hard) An Index Yield That Changes by Month
>The risk-free rate is $5\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A stock index pays dividends at a yield of $6\%$ per year during March, June, September, and December, and $1.5\%$ per year in the other months. On April 30 the index stands at $4{,}800$. A futures contract for delivery on October 31 trades at $4{,}875$, with a multiplier of $\$50$ per index point. Treat the futures contract as a forward and ignore daily settlement.
>
>>[!example]- Find the no-arbitrage futures price, and describe the arbitrage for a trader who trades $10$ contracts.
>>The contract runs from May through October, six months, so $T = 0.5$. Two of those months, June and September, have the $6\%$ yield. The other four have $1.5\%$. The total yield over the contract is
>>$$\int_0^T q_s\,ds = \frac{2 \times 0.06 + 4 \times 0.015}{12} = 0.015,$$
>>an average of $3\%$ per year. The no-arbitrage price is
>>$$F_0 = 4{,}800\exp(0.05 \times 0.5 - 0.015) = 4{,}800e^{0.01} = 4{,}848.24.$$
>>
>>The quote of $4{,}875$ is $26.76$ points too high, so the trader sells the futures and builds a long position in the index. For each index unit, borrow $4{,}800e^{-0.015} = 4{,}728.54$ and buy $e^{-0.015} = 0.9851$ units of the index. Reinvest every dividend in the index, so the holding grows to exactly one unit by October 31. Deliver it, or settle in cash, and receive $4{,}875$. Repay the loan, which has grown to $4{,}728.54e^{0.025} = 4{,}848.24$. The profit is $26.76$ points per index unit, whatever the index does. With $10$ contracts at $\$50$ per point, the profit is about $26.76 \times 50 \times 10 = \$13{,}380$.
