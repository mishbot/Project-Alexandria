>[!note] Thermal Expansion
>**Thermal expansion** is the change in the size of an object when its temperature changes. Most solids and liquids expand when heated and contract when cooled. For moderate temperature changes, experiments show that the change in length of a solid is proportional to its original length and to the temperature change. We write this relation as
>$$\Delta L = \alpha L_0\, \Delta T, \qquad L = L_0\,(1 + \alpha\, \Delta T),$$
>where $L_0$ is the length at the initial temperature $T_0$, $L$ is the length at temperature $T$, and $\Delta T = T - T_0$. The constant $\alpha$ is the **coefficient of linear expansion** of the material, with units of $\text{K}^{-1}$.
>
>The volume of a solid or a liquid follows the same pattern,
>$$\Delta V = \beta V_0\, \Delta T,$$
>where $V_0$ is the original volume and $\beta$ is the **coefficient of volume expansion**. Liquids have no fixed shape, so only $\beta$ applies to them. Their coefficients are about ten times those of solids. A gas needs the ideal gas law instead, as the property on gases explains. For a solid that expands equally in all directions, the two coefficients satisfy $\beta \approx 3\alpha$.
>
>Both relations hold when $\alpha\,\Delta T$ and $\beta\,\Delta T$ are much smaller than $1$ and the material stays in one phase. The coefficients vary slowly with temperature, so tables quote them near $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Because $\Delta T$ is a difference, it has the same value in Celsius degrees and in kelvins. The table gives typical values.
>
>| Material | $\alpha$ $(10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1})$ | $\beta$ $(10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1})$ |
>|---|---|---|
>| Aluminum | $25$ | $75$ |
>| Brass | $19$ | $56$ |
>| Copper | $17$ | $50$ |
>| Iron or steel | $12$ | $35$ |
>| Glass (ordinary) | $9$ | $27$ |
>| Glass (Pyrex) | $3$ | $9$ |
>| Mercury | | $180$ |
>| Water | | $210$ |
>| Glycerin | | $500$ |
>| Gasoline | | $950$ |
>| Ethyl alcohol | | $1100$ |
>| Air at one atmosphere | | $3400$ |

# Intuition

The [[2.1.1 Atomic Theory of Matter|atomic theory]] explains why heating makes a solid larger. Each atom in a solid vibrates about an equilibrium position. The potential energy between two neighboring atoms rises steeply when they move closer and gently when they move apart. A higher temperature means a larger vibration. Because of the uneven curve, a vibrating atom reaches farther on the outward side than on the inward side. The average distance between neighbors therefore grows with temperature. Every distance in the object grows by the same fraction, including the diameters of holes. In a crystal the atoms sit on a regular lattice. The spacing between neighbors is the **lattice constant** $a$, about $0.4\ \text{nm}$ for aluminum. A rod with $N_s$ atoms along its length has length $L = N_s a$, since $N_s$ is so large that $N_s - 1 \approx N_s$. Heating changes $a$ but not $N_s$, so
$$\frac{\Delta L}{L_0} = \frac{\Delta a}{a_0}.$$

The fractional growth per kelvin is tiny, about $10^{-5}$ for metals. The formula $\Delta L = \alpha L_0\, \Delta T$ is a [[2.4. Linear Approximation|linear approximation]] to the true behavior. It keeps the first term of the Taylor series of $L(T)$ about $T_0$,
$$L(T) \approx L(T_0) + \frac{dL}{dT}\bigg|_{T_0} \Delta T, \qquad \alpha = \frac{1}{L_0}\,\frac{dL}{dT}.$$
The next term is of order $(\alpha\,\Delta T)^2$. The effect is so small that the approximation stays accurate over hundreds of degrees. The effect still matters in engineering. A steel bridge $1\ \text{km}$ long grows by about half a meter when its temperature rises by $40\ \text{K}$, so bridges need expansion joints. Two classroom demonstrations show how strong the effect is. In the first, an iron rod is heated with a Bunsen burner until it has fully expanded. A steel peg $2\ \text{mm}$ across is then slipped through a hole near its end, outside a fixed bracket. As the rod cools, it tries to shrink back. The bracket holds the peg, and the peg snaps in half. The sketch shows the setup. In the second, a cast-iron pipe elbow holding water is chilled with liquid nitrogen. The water freezes, expands, and cracks the quarter-inch iron wall, which is how pipes burst in cold winters. That case is a phase change, not linear expansion, but the forces are of the same kind. Rails that are anchored too tightly buckle into S shapes on hot days for the same reason.

# Properties

#### *(Holes Expand)* A hole in a heated object grows as if it were filled with the material.

Thermal expansion multiplies every distance between two points of the material by $1 + \alpha\,\Delta T$. The diameter of a hole is such a distance, so it grows by $\Delta D = \alpha D_0\, \Delta T$. The material does not swell into the hole. The same rule gives the capacity of a container, which expands with the volume coefficient $\beta \approx 3\alpha$ of the container material. Heating a tight metal lid or collar loosens it for this reason.

#### *(Dimensionality Rule)* A length grows by $\alpha\,\Delta T$, an area by $2\alpha\,\Delta T$, and a volume by $3\alpha\,\Delta T$, because $(1 + u)^p \approx 1 + pu$ for small $u$.

The tool behind every expansion formula is the [[2.4. Linear Approximation|linear approximation]] $(1 + u)^p \approx 1 + pu$, valid when $u$ is much smaller than one. Here $u = \alpha\,\Delta T$, and the power $p$ counts the dimensions of the quantity. For a length $p = 1$, for an area $p = 2$, and for a volume $p = 3$. A plate of area $A_0$ therefore gains $\Delta A \approx 2\alpha A_0\, \Delta T$, whatever its shape, and the same holds for the area of a hole. The rule also runs the other way: a density goes as $V^{-1}$, so $p = -1$ and $\rho \approx \rho_0 (1 - 3\alpha\,\Delta T)$. The approximation is safe because $\alpha$ is of order $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-5}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ and $\Delta T$ cannot exceed about $10^{3}\ \text{K}$ before the material melts or boils, so $u$ stays below about $10^{-2}$ and the dropped term $u^2$ is below $10^{-4}$.

#### *(Not Every Solid Is the Same in Every Direction)* Crystals can expand by different amounts along different axes, and a few materials shrink when heated.

The volume coefficient is the sum of the three linear coefficients, $\beta = \alpha_x + \alpha_y + \alpha_z$, and $\beta = 3\alpha$ holds only for an isotropic material, one with the same $\alpha$ in every direction. Metals and glasses are isotropic in practice because their grains point every way. Single crystals such as calcite and graphite are not, and a rod cut from one expands by an amount that depends on the cutting direction. Some plastics and rubbers have a negative $\alpha$ along a stretched direction: heat-shrink tubing contracts when warmed. Both cases obey the same $\Delta L = \alpha L_0\, \Delta T$, with a direction-dependent or negative $\alpha$.

#### *(Apparent Expansion)* A liquid in a container overflows by the difference between the two volume expansions.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover liquids overflowing their containers. This property follows Giancoli section 17.4.

Heating expands the liquid and the container together. A container that is full at the start spills a volume
$$\Delta V_{\text{spill}} = (\beta_{\text{liquid}} - \beta_{\text{container}})\, V_0\, \Delta T,$$
where $V_0$ is the shared initial volume. Liquids have much larger coefficients than solids, so the liquid term dominates. A liquid-in-glass thermometer works for the same reason. The liquid expands more than the glass bulb, and the excess rises in the narrow tube.

#### *(Density Change)* Heating lowers the density of a material because the mass stays fixed while the volume grows.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not discuss the change in density on heating. This property follows Giancoli section 17.4.

The [[1.7.1. Fluids|density]] after heating is $\rho = m / V = \rho_0 / (1 + \beta\,\Delta T)$, where $\rho_0$ is the initial density. For small $\beta\,\Delta T$ this is close to $\rho_0 (1 - \beta\,\Delta T)$. Warm fluid is therefore less dense than the cooler fluid around it, and [[1.7.3. Buoyancy|buoyancy]] pushes it upward. This motion drives [[2.6.2 Convection|convection]].

#### *(Bimetallic Strip)* Two bonded metals with different coefficients bend when the temperature changes.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover the bimetallic strip. This property follows Giancoli section 17.4.

The strip joins two thin layers, for example brass and steel, along their length. Heating lengthens the brass more than the steel. The strip can accommodate the two lengths only by curving, with the brass on the outside of the curve. Cooling bends it the other way. Thermostats use the bending to open and close an electrical contact, and dial [[2.1.2 Temperature Scales|thermometers]] use it to turn a pointer.

#### *(Gases)* The linear volume formula is a poor tool for gases, and the gas law replaces it.

The coefficient for a gas is large and changes quickly with temperature. For a gas at constant pressure, the [[2.2.3 Ideal Gas Law|ideal gas law]] gives $V \propto T$ with $T$ in kelvins, so $\beta = 1/T$. At $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ this equals $1/(293\ \text{K}) = 3400 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$, which matches the table entry for air. The value is different at any other temperature, and it has no meaning unless the pressure is held constant.

>[!warning] Anomalous Expansion of Water
>Water does not follow the usual pattern near its freezing point. Between $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $4\ ^\circ\text{C}$, water contracts when heated, and its density reaches a maximum at $4\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Above $4\ ^\circ\text{C}$ it expands with rising temperature like other liquids. The table value $\beta = 210 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ applies near $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ only. This behavior explains why lakes freeze from the top. Water cooled to $4\ ^\circ\text{C}$ sinks to the bottom. Water colder than $4\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is less dense, so it stays at the surface and freezes there. Ice is less dense than liquid water, so it floats, and the liquid below stays near $4\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

# Derivation

We want to show that $\beta \approx \alpha_x + \alpha_y + \alpha_z$, and that $\beta \approx 3\alpha$ for a solid that expands equally in all directions. Consider a rectangular block with sides $a_0$, $b_0$, and $c_0$ and volume $V_0 = a_0 b_0 c_0$. Let the material have coefficient $\alpha_x$ along the first side, $\alpha_y$ along the second, and $\alpha_z$ along the third. After a temperature change $\Delta T$, each side grows by its own factor, and the new volume is
$$V = V_0\,(1 + \alpha_x \Delta T)(1 + \alpha_y \Delta T)(1 + \alpha_z \Delta T).$$
Multiplying out gives
$$V = V_0 \left[ 1 + (\alpha_x + \alpha_y + \alpha_z)\,\Delta T + (\alpha_x \alpha_y + \alpha_y \alpha_z + \alpha_z \alpha_x)\,\Delta T^2 + \alpha_x \alpha_y \alpha_z\,\Delta T^3 \right].$$
The last two terms are smaller than the first by a factor of order $\alpha\,\Delta T$, so $V \approx V_0 (1 + \beta\,\Delta T)$ with $\beta = \alpha_x + \alpha_y + \alpha_z$. For an isotropic material all three coefficients equal $\alpha$. Each side is then longer by the factor $1 + \alpha\,\Delta T$, and the new volume is
$$V = V_0\,(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T)^3 = V_0 \left[ 1 + 3\alpha\,\Delta T + 3(\alpha\,\Delta T)^2 + (\alpha\,\Delta T)^3 \right],$$
where the second form expands the cube. For aluminum heated by $100\ \text{K}$, $\alpha\,\Delta T = 2.5 \times 10^{-3}$. The term $3\alpha\,\Delta T$ is then $7.5 \times 10^{-3}$, and the next term is only $1.9 \times 10^{-5}$. Dropping the two smallest terms changes the result by about $0.25$ percent and leaves
$$\Delta V = V - V_0 \approx 3\alpha V_0\, \Delta T.$$
Comparing this result with the definition $\Delta V = \beta V_0\, \Delta T$ gives $\beta \approx 3\alpha$. Any solid shape can be divided into small blocks, so the result does not depend on the shape.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Gap Between Steel Rails
>A railway crew lays steel rails, each $25.0\ \text{m}$ long, on a day when the rails are at $10\ ^\circ\text{C}$. In summer the rails reach $45\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>```tikz
>\usepackage{tikz}
>\begin{document}
>\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1cm,y=1cm,>=latex]
>\draw[very thick] (0,0) rectangle (4.0,0.35);
>\draw[very thick] (4.4,0) rectangle (8.4,0.35);
>\draw[<->] (4.0,0.7) -- (4.4,0.7) node[above] {gap};
>\draw[<->] (0,-0.3) -- (4.0,-0.3) node[midway,below] {$25.0\ \mathrm{m}$ at $10\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$};
>\draw[<->] (4.4,-0.3) -- (8.4,-0.3) node[midway,below] {$25.0\ \mathrm{m}$ at $10\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$};
>\end{tikzpicture}
>\end{document}
>```
>
>>[!example]- What gap must the crew leave between neighboring rails so that they never press against each other?
>>Each rail lengthens by
>>$$\Delta L = \alpha L_0\, \Delta T = (12 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1})(25.0\ \text{m})(35\ \text{K}) = 1.05 \times 10^{-2}\ \text{m},$$
>>where $\Delta T = 45 - 10 = 35\ \text{K}$. Half of this growth appears at each end of a rail, and each gap receives growth from the two rails beside it. Each gap therefore closes by one full $\Delta L$, and the crew must leave at least $1.05\ \text{cm}$.

>>[!example]- Find the new radii and the change in the area of the washer.
>>For steel, $\alpha\,\Delta T = (12 \times 10^{-6})(200) = 2.4 \times 10^{-3}$. Both radii grow by this fraction,
>>$$a = a_0 (1 + \alpha\,\Delta T) = 1.0024\ \text{cm}, \qquad b = b_0 (1 + \alpha\,\Delta T) = 2.0048\ \text{cm}.$$
>>The hole grows, as the property on holes says. The area of the washer is the outer disk minus the hole,
>>$$A = \pi (b^2 - a^2) = \pi (b_0^2 - a_0^2)(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T)^2 = A_0 (1 + \alpha\,\Delta T)^2 \approx A_0 (1 + 2\alpha\,\Delta T).$$
>>Here $A_0 = \pi (4.00 - 1.00) = 9.42\ \text{cm}^2$, so $\Delta A \approx 2\alpha A_0 \Delta T = 0.045\ \text{cm}^2$. The factor $2$ is the dimension of an area.

>
>```tikz

>\end{tikzpicture}
>\end{document}
>```
>
>>[!example]- The shaft stays at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$. To what temperature must the collar be heated?
>>The hole expands like a disk of aluminum, so its diameter follows $\Delta D = \alpha D_0\, \Delta T$. The diameter must grow by $0.020\ \text{cm}$, which requires
>>$$\Delta T = \frac{\Delta D}{\alpha D_0} = \frac{0.020\ \text{cm}}{(25 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1})(4.980\ \text{cm})} = 161\ \text{K}.$$
>>The collar must reach $20 + 161 = 181\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- The collar and the shaft are heated together instead. At what temperature does the collar fit?
>>Both diameters now grow, and the collar fits when they are equal,
>>$$4.980\,(1 + 25 \times 10^{-6}\, \Delta T) = 5.000\,(1 + 12 \times 10^{-6}\, \Delta T),$$
>>with $\Delta T$ in kelvins and both sides in centimeters. Collecting the $\Delta T$ terms gives
>>$$\Delta T = \frac{5.000 - 4.980}{(25 \times 10^{-6})(4.980) - (12 \times 10^{-6})(5.000)}\ \text{K} = 310\ \text{K}.$$
>>The parts fit at $330\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The required temperature is higher than before because the shaft also expands. Only the difference between the two expansions closes the mismatch.

>[!example] (Medium) Glycerin in a Pyrex Flask
>A Pyrex flask holds exactly $1.000\ \text{L}$ and is filled to the brim with glycerin at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The flask and the glycerin are warmed to $80\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- How much glycerin spills out?
>>The glycerin expands by
>>$$\Delta V_{\text{gly}} = (500 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1})(1000\ \text{mL})(60\ \text{K}) = 30.0\ \text{mL}.$$
>>The capacity of the flask grows as if the cavity were solid Pyrex, with $\beta = 9 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$,
>>$$\Delta V_{\text{flask}} = (9 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1})(1000\ \text{mL})(60\ \text{K}) = 0.54\ \text{mL}.$$
>>The spilled volume is the difference, $30.0 - 0.54 = 29.5\ \text{mL}$. Ignoring the flask would overstate the spill by about $2$ percent.

>[!example] (Hard) A Pendulum Clock in a Warm Room
>A clock uses a brass pendulum and keeps correct time at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The room warms to $35\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and stays there.
>
>>[!example]- How many seconds does the clock lose in one day?
>>The period of a [[1.1u.2 Simple Pendulum|simple pendulum]] of length $L$ is $\tau = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$, so $\tau$ is proportional to $\sqrt{L}$. The warm pendulum has length $L_0(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T)$, and the ratio of the new period to the old one is
>>$$\frac{\tau}{\tau_0} = \sqrt{1 + \alpha\,\Delta T} \approx 1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\alpha\,\Delta T,$$
>>where the last step uses $\sqrt{1 + x} \approx 1 + x/2$ for small $x$. With $\alpha = 19 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ and $\Delta T = 15\ \text{K}$, the fractional increase in the period is
>>$$\tfrac{1}{2}\,\alpha\,\Delta T = \tfrac{1}{2}(19 \times 10^{-6})(15) = 1.425 \times 10^{-4}.$$
>>Each swing takes longer, so the clock counts too few swings and runs slow. Over one day of $86\,400\ \text{s}$, the clock loses
>>$$(1.425 \times 10^{-4})(86\,400\ \text{s}) = 12\ \text{s}.$$
