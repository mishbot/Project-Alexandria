>[!note] Rigid Body Velocity
>While [[2.2. Twists and Screws|twists]] describe the *direction* of infinitesimal rigid motion, **rigid body velocity** concerns the *rate* at which a body moves along such a twist. Given a trajectory $g_{sb}(t) \in SE(3)$ — the **pose of the body frame $\{b\}$ in the spatial frame $\{s\}$** — the velocity is a tangent vector in $\mathfrak{se}(3)$, and there are two canonical ways to express it depending on the reference frame.
>
>**Angular velocity** is the rotational component. For a time-varying rotation $R_{sb}(t) \in SO(3)$, the **spatial angular velocity** $\hat{\omega}^s_{sb}$ and **body angular velocity** $\hat{\omega}^b_{sb}$ are defined by
>$$\hat{\omega}^s_{sb} = \dot{R}_{sb}\,R_{sb}^T \in so(3), \qquad \hat{\omega}^b_{sb} = R_{sb}^T\,\dot{R}_{sb} \in so(3),$$
>where $so(3)$ is the space of $3\times 3$ skew-symmetric matrices, and $R_{sb}^{-1} = R_{sb}^T$ since $R_{sb} \in SO(3)$ is orthogonal. Both matrices are skew-symmetric because differentiating $R_{sb}^T R_{sb} = I$ gives $\dot{R}_{sb}^T R_{sb} + R_{sb}^T \dot{R}_{sb} = 0$, so $R_{sb}^T \dot{R}_{sb}$ is skew. The spatial form describes the rotation as seen from the fixed frame; the body form describes it in the moving body frame. They are related by a similarity transform:
>$$\omega^s_{sb} = R_{sb}\,\omega^b_{sb}.$$
>
>For full rigid body motion $g_{sb}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} R_{sb}(t) & p_{sb}(t) \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \in SE(3)$, the **spatial velocity** and **body velocity** are the $\mathfrak{se}(3)$-valued quantities
>$$\hat{V}^s_{sb} = \dot{g}_{sb}\,g_{sb}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\omega}^s_{sb} & \dot{p}_{sb} - \hat{\omega}^s_{sb}\,p_{sb} \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \hat{V}^b_{sb} = g_{sb}^{-1}\,\dot{g}_{sb} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\omega}^b_{sb} & R_{sb}^T\,\dot{p}_{sb} \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix},$$
>where $g_{sb}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} R_{sb}^T & -R_{sb}^T\,p_{sb} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ is the closed-form inverse of a rigid motion (again using $R_{sb}^{-1} = R_{sb}^T$).
>Extracting the 6-vectors using the vee map $(\cdot)^\vee$:
>$$V^s_{sb} = \begin{bmatrix} v^s_{sb} \\ \omega^s_{sb} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \dot{p}_{sb} - \hat{\omega}^s_{sb}\,p_{sb} \\ \omega^s_{sb} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad V^b_{sb} = \begin{bmatrix} v^b_{sb} \\ \omega^b_{sb} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} R_{sb}^T\,\dot{p}_{sb} \\ \omega^b_{sb} \end{bmatrix}.$$
>The body velocity has a direct physical interpretation: $v^b_{sb} = R_{sb}^T\,\dot{p}_{sb}$ is the translational velocity of the body-frame origin expressed in body coordinates, and $\omega^b_{sb}$ is the angular velocity in body coordinates. The spatial velocity is more subtle: $v^s_{sb} = \dot{p}_{sb} - \hat{\omega}^s_{sb}\,p_{sb}$ is **not** the velocity of any physical point on the body, but rather the velocity of an imaginary point on the body that is instantaneously passing through the spatial origin.
>
>The two velocities are related by the **adjoint map** of $SE(3)$:
>$$V^s_{sb} = \mathrm{Ad}_{g_{sb}}\, V^b_{sb}, \qquad \mathrm{Ad}_{g_{sb}} = \begin{bmatrix} R_{sb} & \hat{p}_{sb}R_{sb} \\ 0 & R_{sb} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{6\times 6}.$$
>
>>[!warning] Notation
>>The convention $\xi = \begin{bmatrix} v \\ \omega \end{bmatrix}$ places linear velocity on top and angular velocity on the bottom. Some references reverse this. The adjoint matrix changes form accordingly—always check which convention a source uses before combining formulas.

# Intuition
The spatial and body velocities capture the same physical motion but report it from different vantage points, exactly as a surveillance camera (spatial frame) and a body-mounted camera (body frame) would perceive the same movement differently.

The spatial velocity $\hat{V}^s = \dot{g}\,g^{-1}$ can be understood by imagining that you first undo the current pose (multiply by $g^{-1}$, bringing the body back to the identity), then apply the infinitesimal change $\dot{g}$. This "transports" the velocity back to the spatial frame. Conversely, the body velocity $\hat{V}^b = g^{-1}\dot{g}$ first undoes the pose and then looks at the change—reporting everything in body-attached coordinates.

The linear component $v^s$ of the spatial velocity has no obvious physical meaning because it describes the velocity of a fictitious point—the point rigidly attached to the body that happens to coincide with the spatial origin at this instant. This abstraction is useful because the spatial twist of a joint is constant (independent of configuration), making spatial Jacobians simpler to construct.

# Properties

## Angular Velocity

#### *(Skew-symmetry)* Both angular-velocity matrices are skew-symmetric elements of $so(3)$.

Differentiating the orthogonality constraint $R^TR = I$ gives $\dot{R}^TR + R^T\dot{R} = 0$, so $R^T\dot{R}$ equals its own negative transpose — i.e., $\hat{\omega}^b$ is skew. The spatial form $\hat{\omega}^s = \dot{R}R^T$ inherits skew-symmetry through $\hat{\omega}^s = R\hat{\omega}^b R^T$, since similarity by an orthogonal matrix preserves skewness.

#### *(Frame relation)* Spatial and body angular velocities differ by a rotation: $\omega^s = R\,\omega^b$.

Equivalently, $\hat{\omega}^s = R\,\hat{\omega}^b R^T$. The identity follows from the [[1.4. Rodrigues' Formula#Hat conjugation identity|hat conjugation identity]] $\widehat{R\omega} = R\hat{\omega}R^T$ applied to $\hat{\omega}^s = \dot{R}R^T = R(R^T\dot{R})R^T$.

#### *(Joint angular velocity)* A single revolute joint produces a constant body angular velocity and a rotating spatial one.

For axis $\omega$ and angle $\theta(t)$: $\omega^b = \omega\,\dot{\theta}$ (direction fixed in the body frame, magnitude scaled by joint speed); $\omega^s = R(t)\,\omega\,\dot{\theta}$ (direction rotates with the link as the joint turns).

## Spatial and Body Velocity

#### *(Spatial velocity derivation)* $\hat{V}^s = \dot{g}\,g^{-1}$ has angular block $\hat{\omega}^s$ and linear block $\dot{p} - \hat{\omega}^s p$.

From $g = \begin{bmatrix} R & p \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $g^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} R^T & -R^Tp \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$:
$$\dot{g}\,g^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \dot{R} & \dot{p} \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} R^T & -R^Tp \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\omega}^s & \dot{p} - \hat{\omega}^s p \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$
The linear part is the velocity of the fictitious body point currently at the spatial origin — not the physical origin of the body frame.

#### *(Body velocity derivation)* $\hat{V}^b = g^{-1}\dot{g}$ has angular block $\hat{\omega}^b$ and linear block $R^T\dot{p}$.

Computing directly:
$$g^{-1}\dot{g} = \begin{bmatrix} R^T & -R^Tp \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \dot{R} & \dot{p} \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\omega}^b & R^T\dot{p} \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$
Here $v^b = R^T\dot{p}$ is the genuine physical velocity of the body-frame origin, rotated into body coordinates — which is why body velocity is often easier to reason about physically.

## The Adjoint Map

#### *(Definition)* The adjoint $\mathrm{Ad}_{g_{sb}} \in \mathbb{R}^{6\times 6}$ transports twist coordinates between frames.

$$\mathrm{Ad}_{g_{sb}} = \begin{bmatrix} R_{sb} & \hat{p}_{sb}R_{sb} \\ 0 & R_{sb} \end{bmatrix},$$
and $V^s_{sb} = \mathrm{Ad}_{g_{sb}}\, V^b_{sb}$. The adjoint generalizes the pure rotation $\omega^s_{sb} = R_{sb}\,\omega^b_{sb}$ to include the linear-angular coupling that arises when the frame is translated as well as rotated.

#### *(Composition)* The adjoint respects rigid-body composition: $\mathrm{Ad}_{g_{ab}\,g_{bc}} = \mathrm{Ad}_{g_{ab}}\,\mathrm{Ad}_{g_{bc}}$.

$g \mapsto \mathrm{Ad}_g$ is a group homomorphism from $SE(3)$ to $GL(6)$. Chained coordinate changes on twists simplify by composing the underlying rigid transforms first, then taking a single adjoint.

#### *(Inverse)* $\mathrm{Ad}_{g_{sb}^{-1}} = \mathrm{Ad}_{g_{bs}} = (\mathrm{Ad}_{g_{sb}})^{-1}$ has a closed form from $g_{sb}^{-1} = g_{bs}$.

$$\mathrm{Ad}_{g_{bs}} = \begin{bmatrix} R_{sb}^T & -R_{sb}^T\hat{p}_{sb} \\ 0 & R_{sb}^T \end{bmatrix}.$$
This maps spatial-frame twists back to body-frame twists: $V^b_{sb} = \mathrm{Ad}_{g_{bs}}\,V^s_{sb}$.

#### *(Pure rotation)* When $p_{sb} = 0$, the adjoint rotates linear and angular parts identically.

$$\mathrm{Ad}_{g_{sb}} = \begin{bmatrix} R_{sb} & 0 \\ 0 & R_{sb} \end{bmatrix}.$$
No linear-angular coupling appears — rotating about the origin shifts both velocity components by the same rotation.

#### *(Pure translation)* When $R_{sb} = I$, the adjoint couples angular velocity into the linear part.

$$\mathrm{Ad}_{g_{sb}} = \begin{bmatrix} I & \hat{p}_{sb} \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix}.$$
Translating a frame by $p_{sb}$ shifts the linear velocity by $\hat{p}_{sb}\,\omega = p_{sb} \times \omega$ — the lever arm of the angular velocity — while leaving $\omega$ unchanged.

## Joint Velocity

#### *(Revolute joint)* A revolute joint with spatial twist $\xi_s$ and angle $\theta(t)$ produces spatial velocity $V^s_{sb} = \xi_s\,\dot{\theta}$.

For axis $\omega$ through point $q$ (both in spatial coordinates), $\xi_s = \begin{bmatrix} -\omega \times q \\ \omega \end{bmatrix}$, and the twist is constant in the spatial frame regardless of $\theta$. This is why spatial-frame PoE columns involve only the cumulative adjoints of *earlier* joints.

#### *(Prismatic joint)* A prismatic joint with direction $v$ and displacement $\theta(t)$ produces $V^s_{sb} = \xi_s\,\dot{\theta}$ with zero angular component.

$\xi_s = \begin{bmatrix} v \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. The translational axis is a free vector, so the constant-in-spatial-frame property holds trivially.

## Velocity Composition

#### *(Chain rule for frames)* Velocities compose via the adjoint of the intermediate transform.

If $\{c\}$ moves relative to $\{b\}$ which moves relative to $\{a\}$:
$$V^a_{ac} = V^a_{ab} + \mathrm{Ad}_{g_{ab}}\,V^b_{bc}.$$
The adjoint transports $V^b_{bc}$ from the $\{b\}$ tangent space into the $\{a\}$ tangent space before addition — the rigid-body analogue of "velocities add" from Newtonian kinematics.

## Point Velocity

#### *(Velocity of a body point)* A point $q$ rigidly attached to the body at body-frame coordinates $q^b$ has world velocity $\dot{q}^s = \omega^s_{sb} \times (q^s - p_{sb}) + \dot{p}_{sb}$.

Substituting $q^s = R_{sb}\,q^b + p_{sb}$ and differentiating:
$$\dot{q}^s = \dot{R}_{sb}\,q^b + \dot{p}_{sb} = \hat{\omega}^s_{sb}\,R_{sb}\,q^b + \dot{p}_{sb} = \omega^s_{sb} \times (q^s - p_{sb}) + \dot{p}_{sb}.$$
The decomposition shows that a rigid body point's velocity is the sum of the origin's translational velocity $\dot{p}_{sb}$ and the rotational velocity $\omega^s_{sb} \times r$ about that origin, where $r = q^s - p_{sb}$ is the lever arm.

# Examples
>[!example] Velocity of a Rotating Body
>A body rotates about the $z$-axis at rate $\dot{\theta}$ with no translation: $g_{sb}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} R_z(\theta) & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
>>[!example]- Spatial and body velocities
>>$\dot{R}_{sb} = \dot{\theta}\hat{e}_3\,R_{sb}$ where $\hat{e}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.
>>
>>**Body angular velocity:** $\hat{\omega}^b_{sb} = R_{sb}^T\,\dot{R}_{sb} = \dot{\theta}\,R_{sb}^T\,\hat{e}_3\,R_{sb} = \dot{\theta}\hat{e}_3$ (since $R_z$ commutes with $\hat{e}_3$). So $\omega^b_{sb} = \dot{\theta}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
>>
>>**Spatial angular velocity:** $\hat{\omega}^s_{sb} = \dot{R}_{sb}\,R_{sb}^T = \dot{\theta}\hat{e}_3$. So $\omega^s_{sb} = \dot{\theta}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
>>
>>Both agree because the rotation axis is fixed and passes through the origin. For an off-axis rotation, $\omega^s_{sb}$ and $\omega^b_{sb}$ would differ by the rotation $R_{sb}$.
>>
>>**Full velocities:** $V^s_{sb} = V^b_{sb} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ \dot{\theta} \end{bmatrix}$ since $p_{sb} = 0$ and $\dot{p}_{sb} = 0$.

>[!example] Adjoint Example
>For $g_{sb} = \begin{bmatrix} I & p_{sb} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ with $p_{sb} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ (pure translation along $x$), and a body velocity $V^b_{sb} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (pure rotation about $z$ in body frame):
>>[!example]- Computing spatial velocity via adjoint
>>$\mathrm{Ad}_{g_{sb}} = \begin{bmatrix} I & \hat{p}_{sb} \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix}$, where $\hat{p}_{sb} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.
>>
>>$$V^s_{sb} = \mathrm{Ad}_{g_{sb}}\,V^b_{sb} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{p}_{sb}\,\omega^b_{sb} \\ \omega^b_{sb} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$
>>
>>The spatial velocity has a linear component $v^s_{sb} = p_{sb} \times \omega^b_{sb} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$: rotating about $z$ at a point displaced along $x$ creates a $-y$ velocity at the spatial origin. The angular component is unchanged since $R_{sb} = I$.
