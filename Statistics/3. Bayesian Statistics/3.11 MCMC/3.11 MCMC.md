Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) is a class of algorithms that generate samples from a target probability distribution $\pi$ by simulating a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is $\pi$. The samples are correlated, not i.i.d., but long-run averages still converge to expectations under $\pi$ by the ergodic theorem. MCMC solves the core computational problem of Bayesian inference: how to compute posterior expectations, quantiles, and marginal densities when the posterior $\pi(\theta \mid y) \propto p(\theta)\,p(y \mid \theta)$ has no closed form and its normalizing constant $\int p(\theta)p(y \mid \theta)\,d\theta$ is intractable.

The common structure across MCMC algorithms is the [[5.4 Reversibility|detailed-balance]] construction: propose a move from the current state $\theta$ to a candidate $\theta'$ using a proposal distribution $Q$, then accept or reject the move to enforce $\pi(\theta)P(\theta, \theta') = \pi(\theta')P(\theta', \theta)$. This single mechanism produces Random Walk Metropolis, Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs sampling, MALA, and HMC — the difference is entirely in how $Q$ is chosen and what accept-reject rule implements balance.

# Shared Concepts

#### *(Acceptance Rate)* Fraction of proposals accepted; measures how well the proposal matches the target.

Too-small proposal scales produce high acceptance rates (near $1$) but tiny moves, yielding strong autocorrelation and slow exploration. Too-large proposal scales produce most moves into low-density regions and few acceptances, yielding a chain that rarely moves. For high-dimensional Gaussian-like targets, theory suggests tuning to roughly $0.234$; $0.2$–$0.4$ is a practical working range.

#### *(Burn-in)* Early iterates depend on the starting point and are discarded.

The ergodic theorem guarantees convergence as $N \to \infty$, but for finite $N$ the estimate $\frac{1}{N}\sum g(\theta^{(t)})$ is biased by the starting point. Standard practice is to discard the first $B$ iterates and use only $\theta^{(B+1)}, \dots, \theta^{(N)}$ for averages. Initializing at the MLE or MAP (via Newton's method or the [[3.9 Laplace Approximation|Laplace approximation]]) minimizes burn-in.

#### *(Trace Plots)* Plot each coordinate of $\theta^{(t)}$ against iteration $t$ to diagnose mixing.

A well-mixed chain looks like stationary noise around a stable mean. A poorly-mixed chain drifts for many iterations (still in burn-in), stays stuck at constant values (acceptance rate too low), or explores visibly different modes at different times (bimodal target with poor transition).

#### *(Effective Sample Size)* Correlated samples carry less information than i.i.d. ones.

If $\theta^{(1)}, \dots, \theta^{(N)}$ have autocorrelation $\rho_k$ at lag $k$, the effective sample size is approximately $N_{\text{eff}} = N / (1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \rho_k)$. An MCMC run of $N = 10{,}000$ iterations with $N_{\text{eff}} = 500$ is worth about $500$ i.i.d. samples, not $10{,}000$. Low $N_{\text{eff}}/N$ is the single most common sign of a poorly-tuned chain.

#### *(Convergence Diagnostics)* No single test proves convergence; use multiple chains and visual checks.

The Gelman-Rubin statistic $\hat R$ compares within-chain and between-chain variance from multiple independently-initialized chains; $\hat R \approx 1$ is consistent with convergence, $\hat R > 1.1$ is a warning. Running $4$–$8$ chains from overdispersed starting points and checking that they overlap in the trace plots is the practical version of this diagnostic.

#### *(Choice of Proposal)* Algorithm correctness does not depend on $Q$; efficiency does.

Any proposal $Q$ that is irreducible with respect to $\pi$ eventually samples from $\pi$. The engineering challenge is choosing $Q$ so the chain mixes quickly. A proposal matched to the local curvature of $\pi$ — typically via a Laplace approximation at the MAP — dramatically outperforms isotropic random-walk proposals in high dimensions. Gradient-informed proposals ([[3.11.3 MALA|MALA]], [[3.11.4 Hamiltonian Monte Carlo|HMC]]) go further by using $\nabla \log \pi$.
