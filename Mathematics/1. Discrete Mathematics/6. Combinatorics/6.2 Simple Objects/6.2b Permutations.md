>[!note] Permutation
>A **permutation** is a [[6.2a Sequences|sequence]] (i.e., an ordered list of elements from a set), where elements are not replaced. The total number of permutations of $k \leq n$ elements from a set of $n$ elements is
>$$N=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!},$$
>where the denominator "cuts off" the arrangements of the $n - k$ elements that are not selected.
>
>Permutation counting is used to count the number of ways to arrange distinct objects in a specific order, such as the number of ways to seat five different people around a table.

# Intuition
The number of ways to rearrange a sequence is given as $n^k=n \cdot n \cdot \ldots \cdot n$. This is valid when elements in the set of origin are not taken out of the set. However, given a starting amount of $n$ elements, which ARE taken out of the set, the total number of elements is $n \cdot (n-1) \cdot \ldots \cdot 2 \cdot 1$. 

However, this formula only accounts for sequences where **all elements in the set are used up**. If we are trying to solve for a certain constrained length of a sequence $k \leq n$, then we must "remove" any arrangements of the remaining $n-k$ elements, thereby deriving $\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}$.

# Examples
>[!example] Anagrams
>Anagrams are rearrangements of a word's letters.
>>[!example]- How many anagrams of the word "COUNT" are there?
>>Since all 5 letters are distinct, the total amount of anagrams in count is $5!$.
>
>>[!example]- How many anagrams of "BERKELEY" are there?
>>We have 8 letters, but this time there are 3 E's. Therefore, the number of positions where the E's could go are $\dbinom{8}{3}$, since we don't want to treat each E as a distinct letter. Then, we have 5 more empty slots that we could fill up with the other 5 distinct letters. Therefore,
>>$$N=\binom{8}{3} \cdot 5! = 8! / 3!.$$
