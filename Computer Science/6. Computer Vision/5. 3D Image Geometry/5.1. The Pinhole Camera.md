>[!note] The Pinhole Camera
>The **pinhole camera** is the simplest geometric model of how a camera forms an image. All light entering the camera passes through a single point, the **optical center**, before reaching a flat **image plane**. Every 3D point in the world projects to a 2D point on this plane along a straight ray through the optical center.
>
>Three coordinate frames are used:
>- **World frame:** $X_w = [x_w, y_w, z_w, 1]^T$ — positions of objects in the scene.
>- **Camera frame:** $X_c = [x_c, y_c, z_c]^T$ — origin at the optical center, $z$-axis pointing forward along the optical axis, $x$ to the right, $y$ down (or up, depending on convention).
>- **Image (pixel) frame:** $(u, v)$ — indexed from a corner of the sensor.
>
>The map world → camera → image composes three operations:
>
>1. **Extrinsics** (rigid transformation): $X_c = R X_w + T$, where $R \in SO(3)$ is the camera's orientation and $T \in \mathbb{R}^3$ its position.
>2. **Perspective projection** onto the image plane at $z = f$:
>$$x_i = f \frac{x_c}{z_c}, \qquad y_i = f \frac{y_c}{z_c}.$$
>3. **Intrinsics** (pixelation): multiply by the intrinsic matrix
>$$K = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & o_x \\ 0 & f_y & o_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix},$$
>where $f_x, f_y$ are focal lengths in pixels and $(o_x, o_y)$ is the principal point (pixel where the optical axis meets the image).
>
>In homogeneous coordinates, the composite projection is
>$$s\begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = K\,[R \mid T]\begin{bmatrix} x_w \\ y_w \\ z_w \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},$$
>where $s = z_c$ is the projective scale factor. Dividing the homogeneous result by $s$ gives the pixel coordinates.
>
>>[!warning] The Pinhole Inversion
>>In pure pinhole geometry the image plane lies in *front* of the optical center, and the image is inverted (flipped horizontally and vertically). Real cameras place the sensor *behind* the pinhole, which is geometrically equivalent but produces an uninverted image.

# Intuition
A camera takes a 3D world and flattens it onto a 2D image. The pinhole model makes this flattening exact and geometric: each pixel corresponds to a single ray through the optical center, and the scene point that produced the pixel is somewhere along that ray. The division by $z_c$ in the projection formula is the single most important feature of the model — it is *perspective foreshortening*, the reason distant objects look smaller, and the reason a single image cannot tell you how far away anything is (the ray alone is known; the depth along the ray is lost).

The intrinsic and extrinsic decomposition is what makes the model composable. Extrinsics tell you how the camera is placed in the world; intrinsics tell you how the camera turns metric-units rays into pixel-units measurements. Calibration, stereo, and multi-view reconstruction all rest on this split: extrinsics change as the camera moves, intrinsics stay fixed.

# Properties

## Projection

#### *(Perspective division)* Depth scaling is the hallmark of projection: $x_i = f x_c/z_c$.

The division by $z_c$ encodes that a point twice as far away projects to half the image-space offset. Translated into images, this is why two parallel rails appear to converge to a vanishing point — the ratio $x_c/z_c$ tends to a limit as $z_c \to \infty$.

#### *(Loss of depth)* A single image determines the ray through each pixel but not the depth along it.

Given a pixel $(u, v)$ and known intrinsics $K$, one can recover the ray $K^{-1}(u, v, 1)^T$ in camera coordinates, but the scene point could lie anywhere along that ray. Recovering depth requires a second view ([[5.2. Stereo Geometry|stereo]]), motion ([[6.1. Structure from Motion (SfM)|SfM]]), or active sensing (structured light, LiDAR).

#### *(Homogeneous projection matrix)* The full $3 \times 4$ projection matrix is $P = K[R \mid T]$.

In homogeneous coordinates the projection reads $\tilde{x} = P\tilde{X}$ as a linear map. This linearity is what makes projective multi-view geometry tractable: epipolar constraints, triangulation, and bundle adjustment all rely on working in homogeneous coordinates so that the nonlinear "divide by $z_c$" disappears from the estimator and returns only at the final de-homogenization.

## Intrinsic Parameters

#### *(Focal length)* $f_x$ and $f_y$ are the focal lengths measured in *pixels*.

In physical units $f$ is a distance (millimeters). Converting to pixels requires multiplying by the sensor-pixel density: $f_x = f / s_x$ where $s_x$ is the pixel pitch along $x$. Non-square pixels produce $f_x \neq f_y$; for most modern sensors these are equal to within measurement precision.

#### *(Principal point)* $(o_x, o_y)$ is the pixel location where the optical axis pierces the image.

Ideally the principal point is at the image center, but manufacturing tolerances shift it by a few pixels. Calibration recovers $o_x, o_y$ along with $f_x, f_y$.

#### *(Skew)* The off-diagonal entry of $K$ encodes sensor axis non-orthogonality and is almost always zero for modern sensors.

Some texts write $K$ with a skew parameter $s$ in the $(1,2)$ position. It accounts for the angle between $x$ and $y$ sensor axes deviating from 90° and is negligible for CMOS/CCD sensors.

## Extrinsic Parameters

#### *(Rigid body transform)* Extrinsics $(R, T) \in SE(3)$ describe the camera's pose.

$R$ rotates the world frame into the camera frame; $T$ translates the world origin into the camera frame. Together they specify where the camera is and where it is looking. When multiple cameras share a scene, their relative pose $(R_{12}, T_{12}) = (R_2 R_1^T, T_2 - R_2 R_1^T T_1)$ is the input to [[5.3. Epipolar Geometry|epipolar geometry]].

#### *(Calibration recovers intrinsics)* A standard Zhang calibration recovers $K$, $R$, $T$ from images of a known planar target.

Several views of a checkerboard of known dimensions give enough correspondences to fit $K$ via the DLT, then refine $K$ jointly with per-view $(R_i, T_i)$ by minimizing reprojection error. OpenCV's `calibrateCamera` is the standard implementation.

## Limitations

#### *(No lens distortion)* Real lenses deviate from the pinhole model at wide fields of view.

Radial distortion (barrel and pincushion) bends straight lines near the image edges; a standard correction is the Brown-Conrady model
$$x_d = x(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6) + \text{tangential terms},$$
whose coefficients are fit alongside $K$ during calibration. After undistortion the pinhole model holds to sub-pixel accuracy.

#### *(No finite aperture)* The pinhole model assumes an infinitesimal aperture, so everything is in focus.

Real cameras have finite apertures and depth-of-field effects (blur away from the focal plane). For small apertures the pinhole model is an excellent approximation; for large apertures (shallow DoF) the thin-lens model is needed.

# Examples
>[!example] Pixel Coordinates from a World Point
>A camera at world origin with $R = I$, $T = 0$, $f_x = f_y = 500$ px, $(o_x, o_y) = (320, 240)$. Where does the world point $X_w = (1, 0.5, 5)$ project?
>>[!example]- Solution
>>With identity extrinsics, $X_c = X_w = (1, 0.5, 5)$, so $z_c = 5$.
>>
>>Projection: $x_i = f_x \cdot 1/5 = 100$, $y_i = f_y \cdot 0.5/5 = 50$.
>>
>>Pixel: $u = x_i + o_x = 420$, $v = y_i + o_y = 290$.
>>
>>Equivalently, $s(u, v, 1)^T = K(X_c) = (500 \cdot 1 + 320 \cdot 5,\; 500 \cdot 0.5 + 240 \cdot 5,\; 5) = (2100, 1450, 5)$, and dividing by $s = 5$ gives $(420, 290, 1)$.

>[!example] Ray from a Pixel
>Given a pixel $(u, v) = (420, 290)$ and the same intrinsics, what ray in camera coordinates does it correspond to?
>>[!example]- Solution
>>$K^{-1}(u, v, 1)^T = \left(\frac{u - o_x}{f_x}, \frac{v - o_y}{f_y}, 1\right)^T = (0.2, 0.1, 1)^T$.
>>
>>The ray is $\{t(0.2, 0.1, 1) : t > 0\}$. Depth is unknown from a single pixel — any scene point along this ray would project to the same pixel.
