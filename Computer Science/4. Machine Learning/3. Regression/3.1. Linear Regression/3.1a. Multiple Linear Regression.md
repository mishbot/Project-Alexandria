>[!note] Multiple Linear Regression
>**Multiple linear regression** generalizes [[3.1. Linear Regression|simple linear regression]] from one predictor to $p$ predictors per observation. With observations $\{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ where each $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{p}$ holds the predictor values for observation $i$, the model is
>$$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \cdots + \beta_{p} x_{ip} + \varepsilon_i,$$
>or equivalently $y_i = \mathbf{x}_i^\top \boldsymbol\beta + \varepsilon_i$ with $\mathbf{x}_i = (1, x_{i1}, \ldots, x_{ip})^\top$ absorbing the intercept and $\boldsymbol\beta = (\beta_0, \beta_1, \ldots, \beta_{p})^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{p+1}$. Stacking all $n$ observations into matrix form,
>$$\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon,$$
>where $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (p+1)}$ is the **design matrix** whose row $i$ is $\mathbf{x}_i^\top$, and $\boldsymbol\varepsilon \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the vector of noise terms.
>
>The OLS estimator is the closed-form minimizer of $\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2$:
>$$\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} \;=\; (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}.$$
>The inverse exists when the columns of $\mathbf{X}$ are linearly independent, which requires $p + 1 \leq n$ at minimum. Geometrically, $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ is the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{y}$ onto the column space of $\mathbf{X}$. The residual vector $\mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is perpendicular to every predictor. The plain $R^2$ never falls when a predictor is added. The [[2.1.4. R-Squared|adjusted R-squared]] corrects for this when fits have different numbers of predictors.
>
>>[!warning] Multicollinearity
>>When predictors are highly correlated, $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$ is close to singular and the OLS estimator becomes numerically unstable. Small changes in $\mathbf{y}$ produce large swings in $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$, and individual coefficient standard errors become very large. Individual coefficients can flip sign from one sample to the next while the joint fit remains stable. The standard remedies are dropping correlated predictors, pre-processing with [[7.1. Principal Component Analysis|PCA]], or moving to [[3.2.1. Ridge Regression|ridge regression]]. Ridge adds $\lambda \mathbf{I}$ to $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$, which stabilizes the inverse.

# Intuition

Multiple linear regression is the same idea as simple regression, scaled up. It finds the coefficient vector that makes the linear combination of predictors closest to the response in squared-error sense. The matrix formula $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$ is the multidimensional version of the slope formula from simple regression. In it, $(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}$ plays the role of $1/\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(x)$, and $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$ plays the role of $\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(x, y)$.

Geometrically, the fitted vector $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is a linear combination of the predictors. It therefore lies in the column space of $\mathbf{X}$. The residual vector $\mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is the part of $\mathbf{y}$ that the predictors cannot explain. The OLS criterion picks the unique $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ in the column space that minimizes the residual length. By elementary geometry, this is the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{y}$ onto the column space ([[10. Projection Theorem]]). The orthogonality condition $\mathbf{X}^\top (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) = \mathbf{0}$ is exactly the matrix equation that produces the closed-form solution.

# Properties

#### *(Rank-Deficient Designs)* Without full column rank, OLS has infinitely many solutions, and all of them give the same fitted values.

When $n < p + 1$ or columns are collinear, the inverse of $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$ fails to exist and the problem is ill-posed without regularization. The **normal equations** $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$ still have solutions, because $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$ lies in the column space of $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$. Adding any vector from the null space of $\mathbf{X}$ to a solution gives another solution. Every solution produces the same $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$, since the projection onto the column space is unique. The fitted values are identified, and the individual coefficients are not. The [[13.2 Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse|pseudoinverse]] picks the solution with the smallest norm, $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \mathbf{X}^{+}\mathbf{y}$. [[3.2.1. Ridge Regression|Ridge regression]] gives a unique solution for every $\lambda > 0$, and it approaches $\mathbf{X}^{+}\mathbf{y}$ as $\lambda \to 0$.

#### *(Hat Matrix)* The fitted values are a linear projection of the response.

Defining the **hat matrix** $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top$, the fitted values are $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{y}$, justifying the name (it puts the "hat" on $\mathbf{y}$). The hat matrix is the orthogonal projector onto the column space of $\mathbf{X}$. It is symmetric and idempotent ($\mathbf{H}^2 = \mathbf{H}$). Its trace equals $p + 1$, the number of fitted parameters and the rank of the projection. The diagonal entries $h_{ii}$ are the **leverages** of each observation. Each measures how much observation $i$'s own response influences its own fitted value, since $h_{ii} = \partial \hat y_i / \partial y_i$. The leverages lie between $0$ and $1$ and average $(p + 1)/n$. Large leverages identify points whose removal could significantly change the fit.

The animation shows least squares as a projection for $n = 3$ observations. Each axis is one observation, and the columns $\mathbf{1}$ and $\mathbf{x}$ span a plane. The closest point of that plane to $\mathbf{y}$ is $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$, where the residual is perpendicular to the plane.

#### *(Gauss-Markov Theorem)* OLS is the BLUE under uncorrelated, constant-variance noise.

If $\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol\varepsilon] = \mathbf{0}$ and $\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}$, then OLS has the smallest variance among all linear unbiased estimators of $\boldsymbol\beta$. It is the **best linear unbiased estimator** (BLUE). "Smallest" means that $\mathrm{Cov}(\tilde{\boldsymbol\beta}) - \mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})$ is [[4.2e Positive Semidefinite Matrix|positive semidefinite]] for every other linear unbiased estimator $\tilde{\boldsymbol\beta}$. The result requires no distributional assumption beyond the first two moments. When the noise is correlated or heteroskedastic, with $\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \boldsymbol\Sigma \neq \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}$, the BLUE becomes the **generalized least squares** estimator
$$\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\text{GLS}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{y},$$
which weights observations by the inverse noise covariance.

#### *(Residual Variance)* $\hat\sigma^2 = \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\|^2 / (n - p - 1)$ is the unbiased estimator of $\sigma^2$.

The denominator is the **degrees of freedom** remaining after fitting $p + 1$ parameters. The MLE under Gaussian noise is the corresponding biased estimator that divides by $n$. Dividing by $n - p - 1$ corrects for the lost degrees of freedom and produces an unbiased estimator. This is the variance estimator that goes into standard errors and $t$-statistics.

#### *(Inference Under Normality)* The coefficient vector is jointly Gaussian.

Under $\boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})$, the estimator is exactly Gaussian: $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\beta, \sigma^2 (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1})$. Individual coefficients can be tested via $t_j = \hat\beta_j / \mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_j) \sim t_{n-p-1}$ under $H_0: \beta_j = 0$. Groups of coefficients are tested via $F$-tests on nested models. Without Gaussianity, the same statistics are approximately Normal by the central limit theorem when $n$ is large.

#### *(Spurious Regression)* Regressing one trending variable on another can produce false significance.

Two unrelated variables can both trend over time, or both have unit-root behavior. An OLS regression of one on the other then often produces a high $R^2$ and small $p$-values. This happens even though the variables share no real dependence, because the common trend is mistaken for a relationship. A regression of one time series on another therefore needs checks for trends and unit roots. The standard remedies are differencing the series, including a trend term explicitly, or testing for **cointegration**. See [[2.4 Time Series Regression]] for the time-series-specific treatment.

#### *(Iterative Computation by Gradient Descent)* When $n$ or $p$ is too large for the closed form, gradient descent solves the same optimization.

Forming and inverting $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$ costs $O(np^2 + p^3)$. For very large $n$ (millions of rows) or very large $p$ (thousands of features), this cost becomes prohibitive. Iterative methods such as [[6.4. Gradient Descent|gradient descent]] on $\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2$,
$$\boldsymbol\beta^{(t+1)} = \boldsymbol\beta^{(t)} - \eta\, \mathbf{X}^\top (\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta^{(t)} - \mathbf{y}),$$
converge to the same OLS minimum at $O(np)$ per step and avoid the matrix inversion entirely. Convergence needs a learning rate $\eta < 2/\sigma_{\max}^2(\mathbf{X})$, where $\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{X})$ is the largest singular value of $\mathbf{X}$. This is the standard algorithm for fitting linear models on big data. [[4.3. Logistic Regression|Logistic regression]] and most of deep learning use the same algorithm with a different loss.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Predicting House Price from Multiple Features
>$y_i$ is sale price. The predictors are square footage, number of bedrooms, age, and distance to downtown, so $p = 4$ plus an intercept. Fit OLS on $n = 1000$ houses.
>
>>[!example]- How do we read the fit?
>>The coefficient vector $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ has five entries. Each of the four slopes is the marginal effect of a one-unit change in its predictor, *holding all others fixed*. So $\hat\beta_{\text{bedrooms}}$ is the predicted price increase per added bedroom, conditional on square footage, age, and distance staying constant. The standard error of each coefficient depends on $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$. If bedrooms and square footage are highly correlated, both standard errors will be large. The data cannot cleanly attribute price increases to one or the other. The adjusted $R^2$ then compares this fit with smaller models, since it penalizes each added predictor.

>[!example] (Medium) Polynomial Regression as a Special Case
>Fit $y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \beta_2 x_i^2 + \beta_3 x_i^3 + \varepsilon_i$ to a curved scatter plot.
>
>>[!example]- Why is this still linear regression?
>>Although the *function* of $x$ is non-linear, the *parameters* $(\beta_0, \beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3)$ enter linearly. Setting up the design matrix with columns $(1, x_i, x_i^2, x_i^3)$ recovers a multiple linear regression problem with $p = 3$. The OLS solution and all the inferential machinery apply unchanged. This is the standard way to fit non-linear shapes with linear regression. Pick a basis (polynomials, splines, sinusoids) and treat the basis evaluations as new predictors, as in [[3.1b. Basis Function Regression]].

>[!example] (Medium) Geometric Derivation of the Normal Equations
>Show that $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ being the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{y}$ onto $\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{X})$ implies $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$.
>
>>[!example]- How does orthogonality give the formula?
>>The orthogonal projection is the unique vector in $\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{X})$ such that $\mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is perpendicular to every column of $\mathbf{X}$. In matrix form, $\mathbf{X}^\top (\mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}) = \mathbf{0}$. Substituting $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ gives the normal equations $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$. Inverting the Gram matrix, which needs full column rank, produces $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$. The orthogonality condition is therefore the same matrix equation that defines the OLS solution.
