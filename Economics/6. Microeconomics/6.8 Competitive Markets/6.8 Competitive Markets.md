>[!note] Competitive Markets
>This section puts the consumer and the firm together in one market for a single good, with all other prices held fixed. [[6.8.1 Aggregation]] builds the two market curves. Industry supply is the horizontal sum of firm supply curves, $Y_i(p_i, w, r) = \sum_j y_i^{j*}(p_i, w, r)$, and market demand is the horizontal sum of individual demands, $X_i = \sum_j x_i^{j*}(p_1, \dots, p_n, M^j)$. Market demand depends on the whole income distribution, and both curves can bend or jump where agents enter.
>
>[[6.8.2 Short-Run Market Equilibrium]] fixes the number of firms and finds the price with no excess supply and no excess demand, $Y^S(p^*, w, r) = X^D(p^*, M)$. Firms can earn profits in the short run. The implicit function theorem gives the effect of any shock $\alpha$, $\partial p^*/\partial \alpha = -(Y^S_\alpha - X^D_\alpha)/(Y^S_p - X^D_p)$. The denominator is positive, so a fad raises $p^*$ and a cost shock that cuts supply raises it too. [[6.8.3 Elasticities]] makes the formula unit-free with $\varepsilon_{x,p} = (\partial x/\partial p)(p/x) = \partial \ln x/\partial \ln p$, which gives $\varepsilon_{p,\alpha} = -(\varepsilon_{S,\alpha} - \varepsilon_{D,\alpha})/(\varepsilon_{S,p} - \varepsilon_{D,p})$. [[6.8.4 Tax Incidence]] applies it to a per-unit tax. Buyers' price rises by $\varepsilon_{S,p}/(\varepsilon_{S,p} - \varepsilon_{D,p})$ per dollar of tax, so the less elastic side bears more of the tax, whoever pays it legally.
>
>[[6.8.5 Consumer and Producer Surplus]] measures gains from trade in dollars. Producer surplus is profit, the area between price and marginal cost minus the fixed cost. The consumer's welfare change is $e(p_0, u) - e(p_1, u) = \int_{p_1}^{p_0} h(p, u)\,dp$, the area beside the Hicksian demand curve. [[6.8.6 Welfare Applications]] uses these areas to study the deadweight loss of a tax, opening a market to trade at a lower world price, and rent control. [[6.8.7 Long-Run Equilibrium]] adds free entry. Firms enter while profits are positive, which shifts and flattens industry supply until price equals minimum average cost and profit is zero.

# Contents

- [[6.8.1 Aggregation]]: industry supply and market demand as horizontal sums, kinks and jumps where agents enter, and the role of the income distribution. **Not taught:** the piecewise formulas at kinks and jumps and the formula for an income transfer.
- [[6.8.2 Short-Run Market Equilibrium]]: the equilibrium condition, excess supply and demand, comparative statics of $p^*$ and $Y^*$ with the implicit function theorem, and the excess supply surface in 3D. **Not taught:** the formula for $\partial Y^*/\partial \alpha$.
- [[6.8.3 Elasticities]]: the definition and its log form, the Cobb-Douglas and constant-elasticity cases, and the elasticity form of equilibrium comparative statics. **Not taught:** the names elastic, inelastic, and income elasticity, elasticity along a linear demand, the revenue rule $\varepsilon_{R,p} = 1 + \varepsilon_{x,p}$, and $\varepsilon_{Y,\alpha}$.
- [[6.8.4 Tax Incidence]]: the tax wedge, the price effects $\varepsilon_{S,p}/(\varepsilon_{S,p} - \varepsilon_{D,p})$ and $\varepsilon_{D,p}/(\varepsilon_{S,p} - \varepsilon_{D,p})$, the extreme cases, subsidies, and the effect on quantity. **Not taught:** the irrelevance of which side legally pays and the percent formula for the quantity.
- [[6.8.5 Consumer and Producer Surplus]]: profit as a rectangle and as the area above MC, and the Hicksian welfare measure from the expenditure function. **Not taught:** how market surplus adds up, why equilibrium maximizes total surplus, and how the ordinary-demand area compares with the Hicksian one.
- [[6.8.6 Welfare Applications]]: the deadweight loss of a tax, gains and losses from opening to trade, and the two costs of rent control. **Not taught:** the formula $DWL = t^2/\big(2(b + s)\big)$ for linear curves and how fast the loss grows with $t$.
- [[6.8.7 Long-Run Equilibrium]]: free entry, the shift and flattening of industry supply, price equal to min AC with zero profit, and the constant cost industry. **Not taught:** the cases where the long-run result changes, under constant, decreasing, or increasing returns, with a whole number of firms, and in an increasing-cost industry.

# Recommended Path

Read the notes in order. [[6.8.1 Aggregation]] and [[6.8.2 Short-Run Market Equilibrium]] set up the market, and [[6.8.3 Elasticities]] restates the equilibrium formula in a form that empirical numbers can fill. [[6.8.4 Tax Incidence]] is the main application of the three and the most likely exam question. Read [[6.8.5 Consumer and Producer Surplus]] before [[6.8.6 Welfare Applications]], since the applications measure gains and losses as surplus areas. [[6.8.7 Long-Run Equilibrium]] can be read right after 6.8.2. If short on time, read the callouts of 6.8.2 and 6.8.4, then work the Easy examples of 6.8.2 and 6.8.4. Both use the market $AY = 25p$ and $AD = 100 - 50p$.
