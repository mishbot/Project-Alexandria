>[!note] AR(p) Process
>An **autoregressive process of order $p$** describes a [[2.1. Time Series|time series]] $\{X_t\}$ whose value at any time is a linear regression of its own $p$ most recent past values plus a random shock. The name is literal: *auto* (self) plus *regression* (linear prediction), so an AR model regresses the series on lagged copies of itself. In equation form,
>$$X_t \;=\; \phi_1 X_{t-1} + \phi_2 X_{t-2} + \cdots + \phi_p X_{t-p} + W_t,$$
>where each coefficient $\phi_j$ is a fixed real constant, $\phi_p \neq 0$ (otherwise the order would actually be smaller than $p$), and $\{W_t\} \sim \text{WN}(0, \sigma_w^2)$ is a white-noise *shock*: an unpredictable disturbance, uncorrelated across time and with the past of $X$. The same equation written through the [[2.5.1 Backshift Operator|backshift operator]] becomes the more compact $\phi(B) X_t = W_t$ with $\phi(B) = 1 - \phi_1 B - \cdots - \phi_p B^p$.
>
>Whether or not the equation describes a sensible (stationary) time series depends on the coefficients. The condition is that all roots of the polynomial $\phi(z) = 1 - \phi_1 z - \cdots - \phi_p z^p$ must lie strictly outside the unit circle in the complex plane; on this condition $\{X_t\}$ has constant mean, finite variance, and an autocovariance that depends only on lag, and the recursion can be unrolled into a convergent expression in past shocks (see [[2.5.5 Causality and Invertibility]]).
>
>The first-order case AR(1), $X_t = \phi X_{t-1} + W_t$, is the cleanest example. With $|\phi| < 1$ and zero mean, the variance, autocovariance, and autocorrelation collapse to $\gamma(0) = \sigma_w^2/(1 - \phi^2)$, $\gamma(h) = \phi^{|h|}\gamma(0)$, and $\rho(h) = \phi^{|h|}$. The variance grows toward infinity as $|\phi|$ approaches $1$, which is the algebraic form of the stationarity condition $|\phi| < 1$. Once $\phi$ reaches $\pm 1$ the formula breaks down because the process accumulates past shocks indefinitely instead of forgetting them.
>
>The data signature of an AR($p$) is unmistakable: the [[2.2.3 Autocorrelation Function|autocorrelation]] decays smoothly toward zero (geometrically when the roots are real, with damped oscillation when complex), and the [[2.2.6 Partial Autocorrelation Function|partial autocorrelation]] is exactly zero past lag $p$. This combination, a slow tail in the ACF and a clean cutoff at lag $p$ in the PACF, is what allows the order to be read straight off two diagnostic plots.

# Intuition

An AR process is a feedback loop. Each new value is a re-scaled echo of recent values plus a fresh, unforecastable shock. The coefficient $\phi$ in the AR(1) controls how loud that echo is. When $\phi$ is positive and close to $1$, today's value is almost the same as yesterday's, with only a small new shock added: the series drifts slowly, takes long excursions away from its mean, and "remembers" past disturbances for many time steps. As $\phi$ shrinks toward $0$, the echo fades and the series collapses toward white noise: the past tells you nothing, and each value is just the current shock.

The phrase **mean-reverting** describes what happens between any two consecutive steps when the long-run mean is zero and $0 < \phi < 1$. Take the conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[X_t \mid X_{t-1}] = \phi X_{t-1}$. If $X_{t-1}$ sits a distance $d$ above zero, the *expected* next value is only $\phi d$ above zero, closer to the mean by a factor of $1 - \phi$. Each step pulls the series partway back toward its long-run level, which is why values that wander far from the mean tend to walk back. With $\phi$ negative the same shrinkage happens, but with a sign flip, so the series alternates above and below zero in a saw-tooth pattern.

Once $p \geq 2$, the dynamics split into two regimes determined by the roots of the polynomial $\phi(z)$. When the roots are real and outside the unit circle, the autocovariance is a sum of decaying exponentials and the process slides smoothly back to its mean (*overdamped* in the language of mechanical oscillators). When the roots are complex conjugates, the autocovariance is a product of an exponential decay and a cosine, and realizations *ring* at a characteristic frequency before dying out. The location of the roots in the complex plane therefore translates directly into observable behavior: how persistent the memory is, whether and at what rate the series oscillates, how sharply concentrated the energy is in any one frequency band.

# Properties

#### *(Stationarity Condition)* All roots of $\phi(z)$ outside the unit circle.

The series has a constant mean and a lag-only autocovariance (the two conditions for weak stationarity) exactly when every solution $z$ of $1 - \phi_1 z - \cdots - \phi_p z^p = 0$ satisfies $|z| > 1$. For AR(1), $\phi(z) = 1 - \phi z$ has the single root $z = 1/\phi$, so the condition reduces to $|\phi| < 1$. For AR(2), the same condition translates to the parameter region $\phi_2 + \phi_1 < 1$, $\phi_2 - \phi_1 < 1$, and $|\phi_2| < 1$, an open triangle in the $(\phi_1, \phi_2)$-plane on whose boundary the variance and autocovariance formulas blow up.

#### *(MA($\infty$) Representation)* A stationary AR($p$) writes today's value as a convergent infinite sum of past shocks.

Inverting the operator polynomial gives $X_t = \phi(B)^{-1} W_t = \sum_{j = 0}^{\infty} \psi_j W_{t-j}$, where the weights $\psi_j$ are determined by the algebraic identity $\phi(B) \psi(B) = 1$ and satisfy $\sum_j |\psi_j| < \infty$. For AR(1) the weights work out to the simple geometric series $\psi_j = \phi^j$, so $X_t = W_t + \phi W_{t-1} + \phi^2 W_{t-2} + \cdots$. This expression makes explicit that today's value is an exponentially weighted average of all past shocks, and that the influence of any single shock decays geometrically with rate $\phi$, which is the algebraic source of the autocorrelation $\rho(h) = \phi^{|h|}$.

#### *(Yule-Walker Equations)* The autocovariances satisfy a linear recursion at every lag.

Multiplying the defining equation $\phi(B) X_t = W_t$ by $X_{t-h}$ and taking expectations gives
$$\gamma(h) - \phi_1 \gamma(h-1) - \cdots - \phi_p \gamma(h-p) = \begin{cases} \sigma_w^2 & h = 0 \\ 0 & h \geq 1, \end{cases}$$
because $W_t$ is uncorrelated with everything in the past. The equations at $h = 0, 1, \dots, p$ form a linear system in the unknowns $\gamma(0), \gamma(1), \dots, \gamma(p)$ and $\sigma_w^2$, and solving the system gives the autocovariance directly from the AR coefficients. Reading the system in the other direction (substituting *sample* autocovariances on the left and solving for the $\phi_j$) produces the **Yule-Walker estimator**, the method-of-moments fit that is often used as a starting point for maximum-likelihood estimation.

#### *(ACF Tails Off)* The autocorrelation $\rho(h)$ never reaches exact zero.

By the Yule-Walker recursion, $\rho(h)$ at large lags is a fixed linear combination of $\rho(h-1), \dots, \rho(h-p)$ with the same coefficients as the AR equation itself. The general solution is a sum of terms of the form $z_i^{-h}$ where $z_i$ are the AR roots, so the tail decays geometrically (real roots) or as a decaying cosine (complex conjugate roots), but is non-zero at every finite lag. This continual non-vanishing of $\rho(h)$ is the structural opposite of the [[2.5.3 MA(q) Process|moving-average]] case, whose autocorrelation reaches exact zero past lag $q$.

#### *(PACF Cuts Off at Lag $p$)* The partial autocorrelation $\phi_{hh}$ is exactly zero for every $h > p$.

The [[2.2.6 Partial Autocorrelation Function|partial autocorrelation]] at lag $h$ measures the correlation between $X_t$ and $X_{t-h}$ after the linear effect of the intermediate values $X_{t-1}, \dots, X_{t-h+1}$ has been removed. Because the AR($p$) equation says exactly that those intermediate values, plus the shock $W_t$, are everything that matters for $X_t$, no additional past observation can add predictive information beyond lag $p$, and the corresponding partial correlation vanishes. This sharp cutoff is the diagnostic that identifies the AR order from data: a sample PACF whose only significant entries are at lags $1, \dots, p$ points unambiguously at AR($p$).

#### *(Spectral Density)* The frequency-domain shape of an AR process is the reciprocal of a polynomial.

By the linear-filter identity (see [[2.3.4 Linear Filters and Frequency Response]]), the [[2.3.3 Spectral Density|spectral density]] is
$$f_X(\omega) = \frac{\sigma_w^2}{|\phi(e^{-2\pi i \omega})|^2},$$
the variance of the white-noise driver divided by the squared magnitude of the AR polynomial evaluated on the unit circle. This rational form is what gives AR spectra their characteristic **peaks**: wherever $\phi(e^{-2\pi i \omega})$ comes close to zero (a *pole* of the spectrum), the density spikes upward, and the closer the corresponding root is to the unit circle, the sharper the spike. For AR(1) with positive $\phi$ the peak sits at $\omega = 0$, capturing the slow, long-memory drift; with negative $\phi$ it sits at $\omega = 1/2$, capturing the rapid alternation. For AR(2) with complex roots at angle $\theta$, the peak sits near $\omega = \theta/(2\pi)$, the natural ringing frequency.

# Examples

>[!example] AR(1) with $\phi = 0.9$
>What does the variance, the lag-5 autocorrelation, and a typical realization look like?
>>[!example]- Solution
>>The variance is $\gamma(0) = \sigma_w^2/(1 - 0.81) \approx 5.26\,\sigma_w^2$, more than five times the variance of the white-noise shocks alone. The interpretation is that today's value is essentially the sum of the recent few dozen shocks (the geometric tail $\phi^j$ has half-life around $\log 2 / |\log \phi| \approx 6.6$ steps), so the standard deviation of the series is much larger than that of one shock. The lag-5 autocorrelation is $\rho(5) = 0.9^5 \approx 0.59$, still substantial: even five steps apart the values share most of their variation. Realizations look like long, gentle drifts above and below zero with no preferred direction, qualitatively similar to a random walk over short stretches but eventually returning toward the mean.

>[!example] AR(2) with complex roots
>For $X_t = 0.6 X_{t-1} - 0.5 X_{t-2} + W_t$, locate the roots of the AR polynomial, decide whether the process is stationary, and estimate the natural ringing frequency.
>>[!example]- Solution
>>The polynomial $1 - 0.6 z + 0.5 z^2 = 0$ has roots $z = (0.6 \pm \sqrt{0.36 - 2})/1 = 0.6 \pm \sqrt{-1.64}/1 = 0.6 \pm 1.28 i$ from the quadratic formula. The modulus is $|z| = \sqrt{0.36 + 1.64} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$, comfortably greater than $1$, so all roots lie outside the unit circle and the process is stationary. The argument of the root is $\arg z = \arctan(1.28/0.6) \approx 1.13$ radians, and the autocovariance therefore oscillates with period $2\pi / 1.13 \approx 5.6$ time steps. Realizations should show a faintly oscillatory pattern at that period, with the oscillation amplitude decaying at a rate set by the modulus: closer to $1$ would mean longer-lasting ringing, closer to infinity (impossible here) would mean immediate damping.

>[!example] Yule-Walker estimation of AR(2)
>Given sample autocovariances $\hat\gamma(0), \hat\gamma(1), \hat\gamma(2)$ from data believed to be AR(2), recover the coefficients $\phi_1, \phi_2$.
>>[!example]- Solution
>>Substituting into the Yule-Walker equations at lags $h = 1$ and $h = 2$ gives the linear system
>>$$\hat\gamma(1) = \phi_1\hat\gamma(0) + \phi_2 \hat\gamma(1), \qquad \hat\gamma(2) = \phi_1 \hat\gamma(1) + \phi_2 \hat\gamma(0),$$
>>which can be stacked into the matrix form
>>$$\begin{pmatrix} \hat\gamma(0) & \hat\gamma(1) \\ \hat\gamma(1) & \hat\gamma(0) \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \phi_1 \\ \phi_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \hat\gamma(1) \\ \hat\gamma(2) \end{pmatrix}.$$
>>Inverting the $2 \times 2$ Toeplitz matrix on the left yields explicit formulas for the estimates $\hat\phi_1, \hat\phi_2$. These are the method-of-moments coefficients; software (R `arima`, statsmodels) typically uses them as starting values for a subsequent maximum-likelihood refinement, which slightly corrects for the small-sample bias of the Yule-Walker estimates.
