>[!note] Generative Adversarial Networks
>A **generative adversarial network** (GAN; Goodfellow et al., 2014) trains two networks with opposite goals. The **generator** $G$ maps a random latent vector $z$, usually drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, to an image $G(z)$. The **discriminator** $D$ is a binary classifier that looks at one image and outputs a probability. $G$ tries to synthesize fake images that fool the *best* $D$, and $D$ tries to identify the fakes. In the lecture's convention $D$ outputs the probability that its input is fake, and the objective is
>$$\arg\min_G \max_D\ \mathbb{E}_{z,\, x}\Big[\log D\big(G(z)\big) + \log\big(1 - D(x)\big)\Big],$$
>where $x$ is a real image from the training data. The original paper lets $D$ output the probability that the input is real. Replacing $D$ by $1 - D$ gives the standard form
>$$\min_G \max_D\ \mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\log D(x)\big] + \mathbb{E}_{z}\Big[\log\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)\Big].$$
>The inner $\max_D$ makes $D$ the best classifier of real against fake for the current $G$. The outer $\min_G$ then moves $G$ against that best $D$. Training alternates [[6.4.1. Stochastic Gradient Descent|stochastic gradient]] steps. One step improves $D$ on a batch of real and generated images, and the next improves $G$ through the gradient of $D$. From $G$'s point of view, $D$ is a **learned loss function**, a loss that is trained from data along with $G$. The game is solved when the generated distribution equals the data distribution, and then $D$ outputs $1/2$ for every image. Sampling takes one forward pass, $x = G(z)$. Moving $z$ along a line moves $G(z)$ smoothly through realistic images. A conditional GAN feeds an input image to both networks and lets $D$ judge (input, output) pairs, as in [[8.4. Pix2Pix]] and [[8.5. CycleGAN]]. Known failure modes are unstable training and **mode collapse**, where $G$ produces only a few kinds of output.

>[!warning] Two Conventions for the Discriminator Output
>The lecture slides show $D$ answering "fake (0.9)" for a generated image and "real (0.1)" for a real one. Their $D(\cdot)$ is therefore the probability of *fake*. Most papers and libraries use the probability of *real*. Under the swap $D \mapsto 1 - D$ the two objectives are identical, but the terms trade places. The lecture also writes the generator input as $x$ (an input image) and the real image as $y$ when it moves to image-to-image translation. Before using a formula, check which output $D$ reports.

# Intuition

Hand-designed losses fail for image synthesis. An L2 loss to a target image averages all plausible answers. Colorization with L2 gives brownish, desaturated images, and a one-step denoiser trained with L2 on pure noise outputs the mean digit. A good loss would say "this does not look like a real photo." Nobody can write that rule by hand, but a classifier can learn it from examples. A GAN trains such a classifier on real and generated images and trains the generator to beat it. The word *adversarial* refers to these opposite goals.

The order of the min and the max matters. $G$ is judged against the best discriminator for its current outputs, so any visible flaw in the fakes becomes a feature that $D$ learns to detect. As $G$ removes one flaw, $D$ looks for subtler ones. The loss therefore keeps adapting to the generator's current mistakes. The figure shows one round of the game in the lecture's convention.

# Properties

#### *(Optimal Discriminator)* For a fixed generator, the best discriminator compares the two densities at each image.

Let $p_{\text{data}}$ be the density of real images and $p_g$ the density of generated ones. In the standard convention, $D$ maximizes $\int \big[p_{\text{data}}(x)\log D(x) + p_g(x)\log(1 - D(x))\big]\,dx$. Each $x$ can be optimized on its own. Setting the derivative $a/D - b/(1 - D)$ to zero, with $a = p_{\text{data}}(x)$ and $b = p_g(x)$, gives
$$D^*(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)}.$$
In the lecture's convention the best discriminator is $1 - D^*(x) = p_g(x)/\big(p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)\big)$. When $p_g = p_{\text{data}}$, $D^* = 1/2$ everywhere and the value of the game is $2\log\frac{1}{2} = -\log 4$. For any other $p_g$ the value with the best $D$ is larger, so the generator's best move is to match the data distribution. In practice $D$ is never optimal, since it gets only a few gradient steps for each generator step.

#### *(Learned Loss)* The generator's training signal is the gradient of the discriminator's verdict, which penalizes any flaw that $D$ can detect.

In the generator step, $D$ is held fixed and the gradient of $\log D(G(z))$ (lecture convention) flows back through $D$ into $G$ by the chain rule. Blur is easy to detect, because real photos are sharp, so $G$ is pushed toward sharp detail. An L2 loss has no such pressure and rewards the blurry average of plausible answers. Image-to-image models such as [[8.4. Pix2Pix]] combine the two. The GAN term asks for realistic texture, and an [[2.1.2. Mean Absolute Error|L1]] term keeps the output close to the paired target.

#### *(Walking on the Manifold)* Straight lines in latent space map to smooth paths of realistic images.

The latent space is a simple Gaussian, and $G$ maps it onto the natural image manifold. Interpolating $z_t = (1 - t)\,z_a + t\,z_b$ and decoding each $z_t$ gives a sequence of realistic images that changes gradually from $G(z_a)$ to $G(z_b)$ (BigGAN, Brock et al., 2018). Linear interpolation in pixel space instead gives a cross-dissolve with ghosting, as discussed in [[3.5. Image Morphing]].

#### *(Alternating Updates)* Training switches between a classification step for $D$ and a fooling step for $G$, and neither loss alone shows progress.

The discriminator step draws a batch of real images and a batch of fakes from the current $G$. It trains $D$ as a binary classifier with the log loss of [[4.3. Logistic Regression|logistic regression]]. In the lecture's convention the labels are $1$ for fake and $0$ for real. The generator step draws new latent vectors and updates only $G$, with $D$ frozen. Each loss depends on the other network's current weights, so the loss values oscillate and do not show whether the images are improving. Practitioners judge training by looking at samples.

#### *(Failure Modes)* GAN training can oscillate instead of converging, and the generator can cover only part of the data.

Gradient steps on a two-player game do not always settle. Each network keeps reacting to the other's last update, and the parameters can cycle around a solution without reaching it. In mode collapse, $G$ maps many latent vectors to a few outputs that the current $D$ accepts. $D$ then learns to reject those outputs, and $G$ moves to a different small set. Flow and diffusion models train by plain regression and do not have this two-player instability. They pay for it with many network evaluations per sample instead of one.

#### *(GANs and Texture Statistics)* The discriminator may learn the statistics that matter for natural images instead of having them chosen by hand.

Parametric texture synthesis matches hand-picked statistics, such as filter-response histograms or Gram matrices of CNN features, as in [[8.1. Parametric Texture Synthesis]]. A GAN discriminator learns its own features for telling real from fake. The lecture presents as a conjecture that GANs might be learning the "right" features to match for natural images.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Reading the Discriminator's Outputs
>In the lecture's example, $D$ answers "fake (0.9)" for a generated image $G(z)$ and "real (0.1)" for a real photo $x$. Use the lecture's convention, where $D$ outputs the probability of fake.
>
>>[!example]- Evaluate the objective for this pair. How does it change when $G$ improves so that $D(G(z)) = 0.5$? What are the numbers in the standard convention?
>>The objective is $\log D(G(z)) + \log(1 - D(x)) = \log 0.9 + \log 0.9 = -0.211$. Its maximum is $0$, reached when $D$ is certain and correct, so this $D$ is doing well. The generator minimizes the first term. If $G$ improves until $D(G(z)) = 0.5$, the objective drops to $\log 0.5 + \log 0.9 = -0.799$. At the equilibrium both outputs are $0.5$ and the objective is $2\log 0.5 = -1.386 = -\log 4$. In the standard convention $D$ reports $0.1$ for the fake and $0.9$ for the real image. The objective $\log D(x) + \log(1 - D(G(z))) = \log 0.9 + \log 0.9$ has the same value, $-0.211$.

>[!example] (Medium) The Best Discriminator for One Pixel
>Consider a world of one-pixel images. After normalization, real brightness values follow $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. The current generator is too bright, and its outputs follow $\mathcal{N}(1, 1)$. Use the standard convention, where $D$ outputs the probability of real.
>
>>[!example]- Find the best discriminator $D^*(x)$ and evaluate it at $x = 0, 0.5, 2$. Compute the value of the game with $D^*$ and say which way the generator should move.
>>The log-ratio of the two Gaussian densities is $\log p_{\text{data}}(x) - \log p_g(x) = \big[-x^2 + (x - 1)^2\big]/2 = 0.5 - x$. Hence
>>$$D^*(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{\,x - 0.5}},$$
>>a logistic function with its decision boundary at $x = 0.5$, halfway between the two means. It gives $D^*(0) = 0.622$, $D^*(0.5) = 0.5$ and $D^*(2) = 0.182$. Integrating numerically, the value of the game with $D^*$ is $-1.163$, which is above the minimum $-\log 4 = -1.386$. The generator wants its outputs labeled real, and $D^*$ rises as $x$ falls. The gradient through $D^*$ therefore pushes $G$ toward darker outputs, which moves $p_g$ toward $p_{\text{data}}$. At $p_g = p_{\text{data}}$ the best discriminator is $1/2$ everywhere, and the value reaches $-\log 4$.
