>[!note] Heat
>**Heat** is energy that passes from one object to another because the two are at different temperatures. The symbol is $Q$, and the SI unit is the joule. Heat flows on its own from the hotter object to the colder one and stops when the temperatures match, which is [[2.1.3 Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics|thermal equilibrium]]. The sign convention used throughout these notes is that $Q > 0$ when heat flows into the system under study and $Q < 0$ when heat flows out. Physicists and chemists agree on this sign. Every change is written final minus initial, never the reverse.
>
>Heat is energy in transit, like [[1.3a Work|work]]. An object does not contain heat. It contains [[2.4.1 Internal Energy|internal energy]], and heat is one of two ways that internal energy can change. The other is work. The [[2.5.2 First Law of Thermodynamics|first law of thermodynamics]] combines them as $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = Q - W$.
>
>The older unit of heat is the **calorie**. One calorie is the heat needed to raise the temperature of one gram of water by one Celsius degree. The **kilocalorie** (kcal, also written Calorie with a capital C on food labels) is $1000$ calories. James Joule measured the conversion between mechanical work and heat in the 1840s. The modern value, called the **mechanical equivalent of heat**, is
>$$1\ \text{cal} = 4.186\ \text{J}, \qquad 1\ \text{kcal} = 4186\ \text{J}.$$
>The British thermal unit is $1\ \text{Btu} = 1055\ \text{J}$. Food labels use the kilocalorie and heating equipment the Btu, and both are converted to joules before use in any formula. The one place the calorie survives is the definition of the [[2.4.3 Specific Heat|specific heat]] of water, $4186\ \text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K})$. Heat reaches an object by three routes, [[2.6.1 Thermal Conduction|conduction]], [[2.6.2 Convection|convection]], and [[2.6.3 Thermal Radiation|radiation]], which the [[2.6 Heat Transfer|heat transfer]] section covers. The rate of heat flow is measured in watts, like any other [[1.3e Power|power]].

# Intuition

Heat and temperature are different things. Temperature is a property of one object, and it tells which way heat will flow when two objects touch. Heat is an amount of energy that moves during a process. A cup of boiling water has a higher temperature than a bathtub of warm water. The bathtub can still give more heat to a cold room, because it holds far more energy. The word *heat* names only the energy that crosses from one body to another, and only while it is crossing. Until the 1840s most scientists treated heat as a fluid, called caloric, that flowed from hot bodies into cold ones. That picture could not explain why drilling a cannon barrel made it hot without limit as long as the drill kept turning. Joule settled the matter with a falling weight. The weight turned a paddle wheel in a tub of water, the paddles stirred the water, and the water warmed. The rise was small, but it was repeatable, and it always matched the work the weight had done. Mechanical work and heat were the same thing measured in different units. The diagram shows the apparatus.

# Properties

#### *(A Process Quantity)* Heat describes a transfer, so it depends on the path and is not a property of a state.

Two samples of gas in the same final state can have received different amounts of heat, depending on how they got there. Gas warmed at constant volume takes in less heat than gas warmed by the same amount at constant pressure. The second also does work on its surroundings. The phrase "the heat in the object" has no meaning. The right phrase is the internal energy of the object, and internal energy is a state quantity. This is the same distinction as between work and energy in mechanics.

#### *(Direction of Flow)* Heat flows by itself only from higher temperature to lower temperature.

Nothing in energy conservation forbids heat from flowing from a cold body to a hot one. It never happens on its own, and the [[2.7.2 Second Law of Thermodynamics|second law of thermodynamics]] states this as a law. A refrigerator does move heat from cold to hot, but only by consuming work. Heat therefore flows until temperatures are equal, and two bodies at the same temperature exchange no net heat however much energy each contains.

#### *(Work Can Replace Heat)* Any process that raises an object's temperature by heating can also be done by work.

Rubbing hands, drilling metal, stirring a liquid, and braking a car all raise temperatures without a hotter body nearby. In each case work done against [[1.2.5. Friction|friction]] or viscosity ends up as internal energy. The final state of the object is the same as if heat had been added. Only the history differs. This is the content of Joule's experiment and the reason the first law adds $Q$ and $-W$ on equal terms.

# Examples

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover Joule's experiment or the calorie. This example follows Giancoli section 19.1.

>[!example] (Medium) Joule's Paddle Wheel
>A $20.0\ \text{kg}$ weight falls $2.00\ \text{m}$ and turns paddles in $500\ \text{g}$ of water. All of the work goes into the water.
>
>>[!example]- By how much does the water temperature rise?
>>The work done is
>>$$W = mgh = (20.0\ \text{kg})(9.80\ \text{m/s}^2)(2.00\ \text{m}) = 392\ \text{J} = \frac{392}{4.186}\ \text{cal} = 93.6\ \text{cal}.$$
>>By the definition of the calorie, $1\ \text{cal}$ raises $1\ \text{g}$ of water by $1\ ^\circ\text{C}$, so $93.6\ \text{cal}$ raises $500\ \text{g}$ by $93.6 / 500 = 0.19\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The rise is small, which is why Joule needed a sensitive thermometer and many repeated drops to measure the equivalence.
