>[!note] Beta Distribution
>A continuous random variable $X \in [0,1]$ follows the **Beta distribution** $X \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ with **shape** parameters $\alpha > 0$ and $\beta > 0$, defined by the [[2.2c.1 Probability Density Function|probability density function]]
>$$f_X(x) = \frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}, \qquad x \in [0,1],$$
>where $B(\alpha,\beta) = \dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}={\Large\int}\limits_{0}^{1} \enspace t^{\alpha-1}(1-t)^{\beta - 1}dt$  is the **Beta function**, which serves as a normalizing constant ensuring the sum-to-one condition.
>
>The Beta distribution models **random proportions, probabilities, and rates**—any quantity naturally bounded between $0$ and $1$. Because its two shape parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ control the density's shape independently, the Beta family can produce symmetric, left-skewed, right-skewed, U-shaped, and uniform densities, making it exceptionally flexible.
>
>Special cases:
>- If $\alpha = \beta = 1$, then $X \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$, since $f_X(x) = 1$ for all $x \in [0,1]$.
>- If $\alpha = 1$ and $\beta > 1$, the density is strictly decreasing on $[0,1]$.
>- If $\alpha > 1$ and $\beta = 1$, the density is strictly increasing on $[0,1]$.
>
>The cumulative distribution function of the Beta distribution is
>$$F_X(x) = I_x(\alpha, \beta) = \frac{1}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\int_0^x t^{\alpha-1}(1-t)^{\beta-1}\,dt,$$
>where $I_x(\alpha,\beta)$ is the **regularized incomplete Beta function**, which has no closed-form expression in general.
>
>A key characteristic of the Beta distribution is **shape control**: $\alpha$ governs how much mass is pulled toward $1$, and $\beta$ governs how much mass is pulled toward $0$. When both $\alpha, \beta > 1$, the density is unimodal with mode at $\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+\beta-2}$; when both $\alpha, \beta < 1$, the density is U-shaped, concentrating mass near $0$ and $1$.
>
>>[!warning] Bayesian Interpretation
>>In Bayesian inference, the Beta distribution is the **conjugate prior** for the [[2.2b.2 Bernoulli Distribution|Bernoulli]] and [[2.2b.3 Binomial Distribution|Binomial]] likelihoods. If $\Theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)$ is a prior belief about a probability parameter and $X_1,\dots,X_n \mid \Theta \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Ber}(\Theta)$, then the posterior is
>>$$\Theta \mid X_{1:n} \sim \text{Beta}\!\left(\alpha + \sum_{i=1}^n X_i,\; \beta + n - \sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right).$$
>>The parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ can thus be interpreted as **pseudo-counts**: $\alpha - 1$ prior successes and $\beta - 1$ prior failures.

# Intuition
The Beta distribution is best understood as a distribution **over probabilities**. Suppose you are trying to estimate the true probability $\theta$ that a coin lands heads. Before flipping the coin, you might express your uncertainty about $\theta$ using a $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ prior. After observing $s$ heads and $f$ tails, your updated belief is $\text{Beta}(\alpha + s, \beta + f)$. Each success shifts mass toward $1$; each failure shifts mass toward $0$. As the number of observations grows, the posterior concentrates around the true $\theta$.

This makes the Beta distribution central to [[1.2.2 Maximum a Posteriori Estimation|Bayesian estimation]] and to any setting where one needs to reason about unknown proportions—click-through rates, disease prevalence, batting averages, and so on.

# Properties
#### (*Mean*) $\mathbb{E}[X \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)] = \dfrac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}$.
#### (*Variance*) $\text{Var}[X \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)] = \dfrac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)}$.
As $\alpha + \beta$ grows, the variance shrinks, concentrating the distribution more tightly around its mean. This reflects greater certainty—in a Bayesian context, more data yields a more concentrated posterior.

#### (*Mode*) If $\alpha, \beta > 1$, $\text{mode}(X) = \dfrac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha + \beta - 2}$.
#### (*Symmetry*) If $X \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$, then $1 - X \sim \text{Beta}(\beta, \alpha)$.
This means swapping $\alpha$ and $\beta$ reflects the density about $x = 1/2$. In particular, if $\alpha = \beta$, then $X$ is symmetric around $1/2$.

#### (*Reflection of the Gamma*) If $Y_1 \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, 1)$ and $Y_2 \sim \text{Gamma}(\beta, 1)$ are independent, then
$$\frac{Y_1}{Y_1 + Y_2} \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta).$$
This construction gives a concrete way to generate Beta random variables from [[2.2c.6 Gamma Distribution|Gamma]] random variables, and reveals the Beta as measuring the **relative share** of one Gamma process against the total.

#### (*Order Statistics*) If $U_{(k)}$ is the $k$-th [[2.6d Derived Distributions|order statistic]] of $n$ i.i.d. $\text{Uniform}(0,1)$ random variables, then
$$U_{(k)} \sim \text{Beta}(k,\; n - k + 1).$$

#### (*MGF*) The moment generating function of $X \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ has no simple closed form, but the $r$-th raw moment is given by
$$\mathbb{E}[X^r] = \frac{B(\alpha + r, \beta)}{B(\alpha, \beta)} = \prod_{j=0}^{r-1} \frac{\alpha + j}{\alpha + \beta + j}.$$

# Examples
>[!example] Coin Fairness
>Suppose you want to determine whether a coin is fair. You start with no prior information, so you set $\Theta \sim \text{Beta}(1, 1) = \text{Uniform}(0,1)$. You flip the coin $10$ times and observe $7$ heads and $3$ tails.
>>[!example]- What is the posterior distribution of $\Theta$, and what is the MAP estimate?
>>By conjugacy, the posterior is
>>$$\Theta \mid \text{data} \sim \text{Beta}(1 + 7, \; 1 + 3) = \text{Beta}(8, 4).$$
>>The MAP estimate is the mode of the posterior:
>>$$\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \frac{8 - 1}{8 + 4 - 2} = \frac{7}{10} = 0.7,$$
>>which coincides with the sample proportion. The posterior mean, however, is
>>$$\mathbb{E}[\Theta \mid \text{data}] = \frac{8}{8 + 4} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667,$$
>>which is **shrunk toward** $1/2$ relative to the MLE, reflecting the influence of the uniform prior.

>[!example] Order Statistic Distribution
>Let $U_1, U_2, U_3 \sim_{\text{iid}} \text{Uniform}(0,1)$. Define $M = \text{median}(U_1, U_2, U_3) = U_{(2)}$.
>>[!example]- What is the distribution and expected value of $M$?
>>By the order statistic property, the $k$-th order statistic of $n$ i.i.d. $\text{Uniform}(0,1)$ random variables is $\text{Beta}(k, n - k + 1)$. Here $k = 2$ and $n = 3$, so
>>$$M \sim \text{Beta}(2, 2).$$
>>Since $\alpha = \beta = 2$, the distribution is symmetric about $1/2$ (by the symmetry property). Its expected value is
>>$$\mathbb{E}[M] = \frac{2}{2 + 2} = \frac{1}{2},$$
>>and its variance is $\text{Var}(M) = \frac{2 \cdot 2}{4^2 \cdot 5} = \frac{1}{20}$. The median of three uniform draws is, on average, exactly $1/2$, but with less variability than any single draw (which has variance $1/12$).
