>[!note] Template Matching
>**Template matching** finds where a small image patch, the **template** $g$, appears inside a larger image $f$. For every offset $(m, n)$, a score $h[m,n]$ compares $g$ with the patch of $f$ whose top-left corner is at $(m, n)$. The best match is the offset with the highest similarity or the lowest distance. In the formulas below, the sums run over all template pixels $(k, l)$. The template mean is $\bar{g}$, and $\bar{f}_{m,n}$ is the mean of the image patch under the template.
>
>The lecture compares four scores. **Correlation** (method 0) is the cross-correlation of the image with the template:
>$$h[m,n] = \sum_{k,l} g[k,l]\, f[m+k,\, n+l].$$
>**Zero-mean correlation** (method 1) subtracts the template mean first, $h[m,n] = \sum_{k,l} (g[k,l] - \bar{g})\, f[m+k, n+l]$. The **sum of squared differences** (SSD, or L2, method 2) is a distance, and it expands into three terms:
>$$h[m,n] = \sum_{k,l} \big(g[k,l] - f[m+k,\, n+l]\big)^2 = \sum_{k,l} g[k,l]^2 \;-\; 2\sum_{k,l} g[k,l]\, f[m+k,\, n+l] \;+\; \sum_{k,l} f[m+k,\, n+l]^2.$$
>The first term is a constant. The second is $-2$ times the correlation map. The third is the squared image $f^2$ filtered with a box of ones the size of the template. SSD can therefore be computed with two filtering operations and a sum. **Normalized cross-correlation** (NCC, method 3) subtracts both means and divides by both norms:
>$$h[m,n] = \frac{\sum_{k,l} (g[k,l] - \bar{g})(f[m+k, n+l] - \bar{f}_{m,n})}{\left(\sum_{k,l} (g[k,l] - \bar{g})^2 \sum_{k,l} (f[m+k, n+l] - \bar{f}_{m,n})^2\right)^{1/2}}.$$
>NCC lies in $[-1, 1]$. It equals $1$ exactly when the patch is $a\,g + b$ for some contrast factor $a > 0$ and brightness offset $b$. The lecture ranks the methods by speed and quality. The zero-mean filter is fastest but a poor matcher. SSD is next fastest and is sensitive to overall intensity. NCC is slowest, and it is invariant to local average intensity and contrast.
>
>Project 1 aligns the three color channels of a Prokudin-Gorskii glass-plate scan with this machinery. It searches every displacement in a window such as $[-15, 15]^2$ and shifts one channel with `np.roll`. It scores the overlap with the L2 norm $\sqrt{\text{SSD}}$ or with NCC. Border pixels are left out of the score. Large scans use a coarse-to-fine [[2.5. Image Pyramids|image pyramid]].

# Intuition

A template is a stencil, and matching slides it across the image and checks the fit at each position. The first idea is to score the fit with a dot product. Correlation computes the [[3.4a Inner Product|inner product]] between the template and each image patch. The inner product grows with the brightness of the patch, so a plain white wall can outscore a perfect copy of the template. Each later method removes one way the score can be fooled. Zero-mean correlation stops rewarding brightness. SSD measures the pixel differences, so it rewards only a near-copy. NCC treats the mean-removed template and patch as vectors $\tilde{\mathbf{g}}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{m,n}$ and measures the angle between them. Raising the patch's contrast stretches its vector without turning it, so the angle and the score stay the same.

# Properties

#### *(Zero-Mean Correlation)* Subtracting the template mean also cancels the patch mean, but the score still grows with patch contrast.

The weights $g - \bar{g}$ sum to $0$. Hence $\sum (g - \bar{g})\, f = \sum (g - \bar{g})(f - \bar{f}_{m,n})$, and adding a constant to the patch does not change the score. A bright wall no longer wins. A patch with the same pattern at twice the contrast still scores twice as high. High-contrast regions with a roughly similar layout of dark and light therefore beat the true match. The lecture's eye search shows this failure, with false detections well away from the eyes. A fixed detection threshold is also hard to choose, because the score has no fixed scale.

#### *(Reading the SSD Expansion)* Minimizing SSD is the same as maximizing correlation only where the local energy $\sum f^2$ is constant.

The third term $\sum f^2$ changes from patch to patch. A bright patch has a large $\sum f^2$, which raises its SSD, so SSD prefers patches whose energy matches the template's. This is how SSD avoids the white-wall failure of plain correlation. Adding a constant $c$ to a patch of $P$ pixels changes its SSD by $-2c\sum(g - f) + Pc^2$, so SSD is sensitive to overall intensity. In code, the expansion turns an SSD map into one correlation and one box filter of $f^2$. Both are linear filters, so both can use the FFT for large templates.

#### *(NCC Invariance)* NCC is unchanged when the patch is replaced by $a f + b$ with $a > 0$, and it changes sign when $a < 0$.

Subtracting the patch mean removes $b$, and dividing by the patch norm removes $a$. In vector form, $\text{NCC} = \tilde{\mathbf{g}} \cdot \tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{m,n} / (\|\tilde{\mathbf{g}}\|\, \|\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_{m,n}\|) = \cos\theta$, the Pearson correlation of the pixel values. The invariance cuts both ways. A faint smudge with the right shape scores as high as a crisp copy. NCC is undefined on a flat patch, where the denominator is $0$, so implementations add a small constant to the denominator or skip such patches.

#### *(Speed Ranking)* The extra cost of each method is the local statistics it needs at every offset.

Zero-mean correlation is one filtering pass with the fixed kernel $g - \bar{g}$. SSD adds a box filter of $f^2$ to the correlation. NCC needs the mean and the norm of every patch, which costs box filters of $f$ and $f^2$, and then a division at every offset. All three are linear in the number of template pixels per offset. The ranking reflects the constant factors.

#### *(Coarse-to-Fine Search)* A pyramid search estimates the shift on a small image, doubles it, and refines it by a pixel or two at each finer level.

An error of half a pixel at one level becomes one pixel at the next finer level. A search of $\pm 1$ or $\pm 2$ pixels around the doubled estimate is therefore enough. Each level must be blurred before it is subsampled, or [[2.4. Anti-Aliasing|aliasing]] creates false structure that misleads the coarse search. Too many levels leave a coarsest image with too few pixels to match, so the first estimate can lock onto the wrong shift.

#### *(Borders)* `np.roll` wraps pixels around the edges, so the score must ignore a margin.

After a roll by $d$ pixels, the first $d$ columns hold pixels from the opposite side of the image. Glass-plate scans also have black and white borders that differ from channel to channel. Both effects add large errors that have nothing to do with alignment. The fix is to score only an interior crop, for example the image with $10\%$ removed from each side.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Four Scores on One Row
>A 1D template $g = [1, 3, 1]$ is searched in the row
>$$f = [8, 8, 8, 2, 1, 3, 1, 0, 6, 0, 2],$$
>indexed $0$ to $10$. The offset $m$ is the index of the window's first pixel. The windows at $m = 0$, $m = 4$ and $m = 7$ are $[8, 8, 8]$, $[1, 3, 1]$ and $[0, 6, 0]$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute all four scores at these three offsets. Which offset does each method choose?
>>Correlation gives $8 + 24 + 8 = 40$ at $m = 0$, $1 + 9 + 1 = 11$ at $m = 4$, and $18$ at $m = 7$. Its maximum over all offsets is $40$, the flat bright plateau, which is the wrong answer.
>>
>>The template mean is $5/3$, so $g - \bar{g} = [-2/3, 4/3, -2/3]$. Zero-mean correlation gives $0$ at $m = 0$, $8/3$ at $m = 4$, and $8$ at $m = 7$. Its maximum is $8$ at $m = 7$, a copy with higher contrast. The true copy loses.
>>
>>SSD gives $49 + 25 + 49 = 123$ at $m = 0$, $0$ at $m = 4$, and $1 + 9 + 1 = 11$ at $m = 7$. Its minimum is $0$ at $m = 4$, the exact copy. The expansion checks out at $m = 0$: $\sum g^2 - 2\cdot 40 + \sum f^2 = 11 - 80 + 192 = 123$.
>>
>>NCC is undefined at $m = 0$, because the patch is flat. At $m = 4$ the patch equals the template, so NCC is $1$. At $m = 7$ the patch $[0, 6, 0]$ equals $3g - 3$, so NCC is also $1$. NCC cannot tell the exact copy from the high-contrast copy. Only SSD separates them.

>[!example] (Medium) Why Project 1 Needs a Pyramid
>A full-resolution glass-plate channel is about $3000 \times 3000$ pixels, and the true shift between channels can reach $150$ pixels in each direction. Scoring one offset costs about one operation per pixel.
>
>>[!example]- Estimate the cost of a single-scale search, then of a five-level pyramid search that covers the same range.
>>A single-scale search needs $(2 \cdot 150 + 1)^2 = 90{,}601$ offsets. Each costs about $9 \times 10^6$ operations, for a total near $8.2 \times 10^{11}$.
>>
>>With four halvings, the coarsest level is about $188 \times 188$ and the maximum shift is $150/16 \approx 9.4$ pixels. A search over $[-10, 10]^2$ costs $441$ offsets on about $35{,}000$ pixels, or $1.6 \times 10^7$ operations. Each of the four finer levels doubles the estimate and searches $\pm 1$, which is $9$ offsets. Those levels cost $9 \times 375^2$, $9 \times 750^2$, $9 \times 1500^2$ and $9 \times 3000^2$ operations, about $1.1 \times 10^8$ together. The pyramid total is about $1.2 \times 10^8$, roughly $6600$ times less than the single-scale search. Most of the remaining cost sits at the finest level.

>[!example] (Hard) When NCC Fails Across Color Channels
>Project 1 names the portrait of the Emir of Bukhara as a scan whose channels do not share brightness values. Suppose a robe is bright through the blue filter and dark through the red filter. At the correct alignment, a 1D slice across the robe reads $B = [100, 200, 200, 100]$ in blue and $R = [100, 20, 20, 100]$ in red.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the NCC of the raw values at the correct alignment. Then compute the NCC of the gradient magnitudes $|B[i+1] - B[i]|$ and $|R[i+1] - R[i]|$.
>>The means are $150$ and $60$. The mean-removed vectors are $\tilde{B} = [-50, 50, 50, -50]$ and $\tilde{R} = [40, -40, -40, 40] = -0.8\,\tilde{B}$. NCC is therefore $-1$, the worst possible score, at the correct alignment. Any wrong shift with a positive NCC beats it. SSD is also large here, $64{,}800$, because the channels differ by $180$ on the robe.
>>
>>The gradient magnitudes are $[100, 0, 100]$ for blue and $[80, 0, 80]$ for red. These are positive multiples of each other, so their NCC is $1$. The robe's outline sits in the same place in every channel even though its brightness does not. Matching [[2.1.2. Edge Detection|gradients or edges]] instead of raw pixels fixes the alignment.
