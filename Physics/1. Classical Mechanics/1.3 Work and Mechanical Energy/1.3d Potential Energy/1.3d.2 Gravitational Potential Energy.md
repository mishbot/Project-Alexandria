>[!note] Gravitational Potential Energy
>**Gravitational [[1.3d.1 Potential Energy|potential energy]]** (SI: Joule / $\text{J} \equiv \text{kg} \cdot \text{m}^2 \cdot \text{s}^{-2}$) describes the interaction between two objects with mass based on how far apart they are. It is defined for systems where gravity is the internal conservative force.
>
>If two spherical objects of mass $m_{1}$ and $m_{2}$ are separated by a center-to-center distance $r$, the gravitational potential energy of the system is given by
>$$U(r) = -G\frac{ m_{1} m_{2}}{r},$$
>where $G$ is the [[1.2.2. Law of Universal Gravitation|gravitational constant]].
>
>For **objects near Earth's surface**, it holds that for $r=R+h$ such that $R$ is Earth's radius and $h$ is the height of the object between sea level and the Kármán line (i.e., $h_{max} \approx 100 \; \text{km}$),
>$$U(r) \approx U(R)+mgh = mgh,$$
>where $m$ is the object's mass, $g=G \dfrac{M}{R^2}$ is the gravitational field strength near Earth's surface, and $h$ is the height of the object from Earth's surface. Note that $U(R) = 0$ since the baseline is defined as Earth's surface.
>
>>[!warning] Infinity as the Potential Energy Baseline
>The general formula assumes that $U(\infty) \equiv 0$, meaning the potential energy is zero when the two objects are infinitely far apart and $U(0) \equiv -\infty$ if they are at a singularity. The negative sign indicates that energy must be added to the system (i.e., work must be done) to separate the objects and overcome gravity.
# Intuition
Observe the gravitational force on $m_{2}$ an external force. Define $\hat{r}$ as the positive unit vector, and $\vec{r}$ as the displacement vector from $m_{1}$ to $m_2$. Then, the work done by the gravity of the left object with mass $m_1$ on the right object with mass $m_{2}$ between center-to-center distances $r_{i}$ and $r_{f}$ can be defined as
$$\begin{align}
W_{G} &= \int^{\vec{r}_{f}}_{\vec{r}_{i}} F_{G} \cdot d\vec{r}  \\
&= \int^{\vec{r}_{f}}_{\vec{r}_{i}} -G \frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{r^2}\hat{r} \cdot d\vec{r} \\
&= -Gm_{1}m_{2} \int^{r_{f}}_{r_{i}} \frac{1}{r^2}dr \\
&= G \frac{Mm}{r_{f}} - G \frac{Mm}{r_{i}}.
\end{align}$$
Because $W_G$ has been regarded as an external force, it follows that the change in potential energy between $r_{i}$ and $r_{f}$ is equal to $\Delta U = -W_{G}=G \dfrac{Mm}{r_{i}} - G \dfrac{Mm}{r_{f}}$.

Here is where the reference point of $U(\infty) \equiv 0$ comes in. Given this reference point, it follows that the potential energy at a center-to-center distance of $r$ is equal to the potential energy at an infinite distance away, plus the change from infinity to $r$, such that
$$\begin{align}
U(r) &= U(\infty) + \Delta_{\infty \to r}U \\
&=0+G \dfrac{Mm}{\infty} - G \dfrac{Mm}{r} \\
&= -G \frac{Mm}{r}.
\end{align}$$

### GPE at Earth's Surface
Now we proceed to our definition for potential energy near Earth's surface. here, the reference point is sea level, such that $U(R) = 0$. Let an object be $h$ meters above sea level (or more generally, Earth's surface). Then,
$$\begin{align}
U(R+h) &= -G \frac{Mm}{R+h} \\
&= -G \frac{Mm}{R} + G \frac{Mm}{R^2}h+\ldots \\
&\approx -G \frac{Mm}{R}+m \frac{GM}{R^2}h \\
&= U(R)+mgh \\
&= mgh
\end{align}$$
using Taylor Series decomposition with the fact that $h \ll R$ near Earth's surface, thereby making all but the first two components irrelevant.

# Examples
>[!example] (Easy) Diverting an Asteroid
>An asteroid of mass $m$ is flying toward Earth. When it is a distance $r_{0}$ away from Earth’s center, it has a speed $v_{0}$. Superman tries to intercept the asteroid and push it away, so that it will not be attracted by Earth again.
>
>>[!example]- What is the minimal work Superman needs to apply on the asteroid?
>>The total mechanical energy of the asteroid is $E_{i}=K+U=\dfrac{1}{2}mv_{0}^2-G \dfrac{Mm}{r_{0}}$. Since Superman must oppose that energy, $E_{f}=0$. As such, the work that Superman must exert on the asteroid to divert its course is 
>>$$W_{S}=E_{f}-E_{i}=0+G \dfrac{Mm}{r_{0}}-\dfrac{1}{2}mv_{0}^2.$$

>[!example] Satellite Orbit
>A satellite of mass $m$ is orbiting around Earth with a circular orbit of radius $R_{1}$, as shown below.
>
>![[Screenshot 2025-08-03 at 18.35.53.png|200]]
>
>>[!example]- What is the total mechanical energy of the satellite?
>>We have that $E=K+U$. The satellite orbits Earth with velocity $v$, and is pulled by Earth's gravitational force. As such, $$E=\frac{1}{2}mv^2-G \frac{Mm}{R_{1}},$$
>>where $M$ is the mass of Earth.
>>
>>We can simplify this even further by observing that the orbit is circular with radius $R_{1}$. As such, the satellite exhibits uniform circular motion. We then have that $a_{R}=\frac{v^2}{R_{1}}$ and therefore the centripetal force $F_{C}=\frac{mv^2}{R_{1}}$. Since the centripetal force of the satellite is essentially the force of gravity on the satellite by Earth, we have that
>>$$F_{C}=\frac{mv^2}{R_{1}}=G \frac{Mm}{R_{1}^2} \implies v^2=\sqrt{ \frac{GM}{R_{1}} }.$$
>>
>>Knowing $v^2$, the equation we derived earlier can be simplified to $\boxed{E=-G\frac{Mm}{2R_{1}}}$.
>
>>[!example]- What is the least amount of energy required to boost the satellite to the higher orbit with radius $R_{2}$?
>>Using the previous step of the problem, we have that
>>$$E_{i}=-G\frac{Mm}{2R_{1}} \implies E_{f}=-G \frac{Mm}{2R_{2}}.$$
>>Therefore, $\boxed{W=E_{f}-E_{i}=G \frac{Mm(R_{2}-R_{1})}{2R_{1}R_{2}}}$.
