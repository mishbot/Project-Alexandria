>[!note] Insurance
>A person has wealth $w$ and utility of money $u$ with $u' > 0$ and $u'' < 0$. With probability $p$ an accident causes a loss $L$. An insurer sells **coverage**: each unit pays $\$1$ if the accident happens and costs a **premium** of $\$q$ in either case, with $q < 1$. The person buys $\alpha \ge 0$ units. Her wealth is $w - q\alpha$ with no accident and $w - q\alpha - L + \alpha$ after an accident. She solves
>$$\max_{\alpha \ge 0}\ (1-p)\,u(w - q\alpha) + p\,u(w - q\alpha - L + \alpha).$$
>- First-order condition at an interior optimum:
>$$0 = -q(1-p)\,u'(w - q\alpha) + (1-q)\,p\,u'(w - q\alpha - L + \alpha) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{u'(w - q\alpha)}{u'(w - q\alpha - L + \alpha)} = \frac{1-q}{q}\,\frac{p}{1-p}.$$
>- Second-order condition: the second derivative $q^2(1-p)\,u''(\cdot) + (1-q)^2 p\,u''(\cdot)$ is negative, so the objective is strictly concave in $\alpha$. The first-order condition is sufficient and the optimum is unique.
>- **Fair insurance**, $q = p$: the premium equals the expected payout per unit, so the insurer's expected profit $q - p$ per unit is zero. The right side equals $1$, marginal utilities are equal in both states, and the person buys **full insurance**, $\alpha^* = L$. Her wealth is $w - pL$ whether or not the accident happens.
>- **Unfair insurance**, $q > p$, as when the insurer must cover operating costs: the right side is below $1$. Wealth is then higher with no accident, which means **partial insurance**, $\alpha^* < L$.
>- If the markup is large, the person buys nothing. The corner $\alpha^* = 0$ holds exactly when $(1-q)\,p\,u'(w - L) \le q(1-p)\,u'(w)$.
>- Log example with $w = 100$, $L = 50$, $p = 0.1$: $\alpha^* = 50$ at $q = 0.10$, $\alpha^* = 32.2$ at $q = 0.12$, and $\alpha^* = 0$ for $q \ge 2/11$.
>
>The same first-order logic decides the stock share in [[6.6.4 Investment in a Risky Asset]].

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture covered the two cases $q = p$ and $q > p$. In the callout, the corner condition for $\alpha^* = 0$ and the log example go beyond it. So do the two-state diagram below and the Medium and Hard examples.

# Intuition

A [[6.6.2 Risk Aversion|risk-averse]] person values a lottery at less than its mean. She would pay something to turn the lottery into a sure amount. Insurance lets her do this in steps. Each unit of coverage takes $q$ from her in the good state and gives her $1 - q$ in the bad state. It moves money from the state where she is rich to the state where she is poor. With a concave $u$, a dollar is worth more in the poor state. The tension is between this gain from smoothing wealth and the markup $q - p$ that the insurer charges.

The diagram shows wealth with no accident, $W_N$, against wealth after an accident, $W_A$. Without insurance the person sits at $E = (w, w - L)$. Buying coverage moves her up and to the left along a line of slope $-(1-q)/q$. The 45-degree line holds the riskless points. An indifference curve of expected utility has slope $-(1-p)u'(W_N)/(p\,u'(W_A))$, which equals $-(1-p)/p$ on the 45-degree line. At a fair price the budget line has that same slope, so the tangency $F$ sits on the 45-degree line. An unfair price gives a flatter line, and the tangency $P$ sits below the 45-degree line. The diagram uses $u = \ln x$, $w = 100$, $L = 50$, $p = 1/3$, and $q = 0.4$ for the unfair price, so that the slopes are easy to see. The gray curves are the indifference curves through $F$ (solid) and $P$ (dotted).

# Properties

#### *(Why Full Insurance)* At $q = p$ each unit is a free transfer of money from the rich state to the poor state.

One more unit changes no-accident wealth by $-q$ and accident wealth by $1 - q$. Its effect on expected wealth is $-q(1-p) + (1-q)p = p - q$, which is zero at a fair price. While $\alpha < L$, the person is poorer after an accident, so $u'(W_A) > u'(W_N)$ and the transfer raises expected utility. She keeps buying until the two states have equal wealth, which happens at $\alpha = L$. Beyond $L$ the accident state becomes the rich one and further units lower expected utility. The argument uses only $u'' < 0$, so it holds for every risk-averse person.

#### *(When to Buy Nothing)* The person buys some coverage exactly when a dollar after the accident is worth enough more than a dollar without it.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>This property and the two after it go beyond the lecture.

The slope of expected utility at $\alpha = 0$ is $-q(1-p)\,u'(w) + (1-q)\,p\,u'(w - L)$. It is positive when
$$\frac{u'(w - L)}{u'(w)} > \frac{q}{1-q}\,\frac{1-p}{p}.$$
The left side grows with the loss $L$ and with the curvature of $u$. The right side is the price of a dollar in the accident state, in no-accident dollars, relative to the odds. When the inequality fails, the objective is decreasing on all of $\alpha \ge 0$, and the optimum is the [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions|corner]] $\alpha^* = 0$. A fair price always passes the test, since then the right side equals $1$ and the left side exceeds $1$.

#### *(Paying to Hedge)* With $q > p$ coverage has a negative expected return, and a risk-averse person may still buy it.

Each unit is a bet that pays $1 - q$ if the accident happens and $-q$ otherwise. Its expected payoff is $p - q < 0$. With no other risk, a risk-averse person refuses every bet $b$ with $E(b) < 0$, since Jensen's inequality gives $E(u(w + b)) \le u(w + E(b)) < u(w)$. Coverage is different, because it pays exactly in the state where she is poor and so narrows the spread of her wealth. She accepts a small expected loss in exchange for a smaller spread. The risky asset of [[6.6.4 Investment in a Risky Asset]] is the opposite case, a bet unrelated to her other risk, which she takes only when its expected return is positive.

#### *(Coverage and the Markup)* For log utility coverage falls steadily from $L$ to $0$ as $q$ rises, but the general sign of $d\alpha^*/dq$ is not fixed.

The surface shows the gain from buying $\alpha$ units, measured as the rise in the [[6.6.2 Risk Aversion|certainty equivalent]] over no insurance, for $u = \ln x$, $w = 100$, $L = 50$, and $p = 0.1$. Expected utility is strictly concave in $\alpha$, so each slice at fixed $q$ has a single peak. The red ridge marks $\alpha^*(q)$. It starts at full coverage, $\alpha^* = 50$, with a gain of $1.70$ at $q = 0.10$. It reaches the corner $\alpha^* = 0$ at $q = 2/11$, and for higher $q$ every slice falls from $\alpha = 0$. In general a higher $q$ also makes the person poorer. If poorer people are more risk averse, this income effect pushes toward more coverage, so the [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] gives no general sign.

# Derivation

We want the first-order condition, its solution for $q = p$ and for $q > p$, and the second-order condition. Write $W_N = w - q\alpha$ and $W_A = w - q\alpha - L + \alpha$, so $dW_N/d\alpha = -q$ and $dW_A/d\alpha = 1 - q$. By the chain rule the derivative of the objective $EU(\alpha)$ is
$$EU'(\alpha) = -q(1-p)\,u'(W_N) + (1-q)\,p\,u'(W_A).$$
Setting it to zero and dividing gives the ratio form in the callout. Differentiating again,
$$EU''(\alpha) = q^2(1-p)\,u''(W_N) + (1-q)^2\,p\,u''(W_A) < 0,$$
since $u'' < 0$ in both states. So $EU'$ is strictly decreasing. By [[7.1 Unconstrained Optimality Conditions]], an $\alpha$ that solves the first-order condition is the unique maximum, and if $EU'(0) \le 0$ the maximum on $\alpha \ge 0$ is $\alpha^* = 0$.

If $q = p$, the right side of the ratio form is $\frac{1-p}{p}\cdot\frac{p}{1-p} = 1$, so $u'(W_N) = u'(W_A)$. Since $u'$ is strictly decreasing, $W_N = W_A$, which reads $-q\alpha = -q\alpha - L + \alpha$, so $\alpha^* = L > 0$. The constraint $\alpha \ge 0$ holds, so the interior guess was right.

If $q > p$, then $\frac{1-q}{q} < \frac{1-p}{p}$, so the right side is below $1$ and $u'(W_N) < u'(W_A)$. A decreasing $u'$ requires $W_N > W_A$, which reads $0 > \alpha - L$, so $\alpha^* < L$. If instead $EU'(0) \le 0$, the solution is the corner $\alpha^* = 0$, which is also below $L$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Fair or Not
>A risk-averse homeowner can insure her house against fire. In case (a) the insurer charges $q = p$. In case (b) it charges $q > p$ to cover its costs.
>
>>[!example]- In each case, does she buy partial insurance ($0 < \alpha < L$), full insurance ($\alpha = L$), or can we not tell?
>>In case (a) she buys full insurance. The ratio of marginal utilities must equal $1$, which forces equal wealth in both states and $\alpha^* = L$. This holds for any strictly concave $u$.
>>
>>In case (b) she buys less than full insurance, $\alpha^* < L$. She buys partial insurance whenever she buys at all. If the markup is large relative to her risk aversion, she buys nothing, so "partial" is correct only when $EU'(0) > 0$.

>[!example] (Medium) Pricing Above the Odds
>A driver has $u = \ln x$, wealth $w = 100$ (thousand dollars), and a $p = 0.1$ chance of a crash that costs $L = 50$.
>
>>[!example]- Find her coverage, her wealth in each state, and her certainty equivalent when $q = 0.10$ and when $q = 0.12$.
>>With $u'(x) = 1/x$, the first-order condition reads $W_A/W_N = \frac{1-q}{q}\cdot\frac{p}{1-p}$. At $q = 0.10$ the ratio is $1$, so $\alpha^* = 50$ and her wealth is $100 - 5 = 95$ in both states. Her certainty equivalent is $95$. Uninsured, it is $e^{0.9\ln 100 + 0.1\ln 50} = 93.30$, so fair insurance is worth $1.70$ to her.
>>
>>At $q = 0.12$ the ratio is $\frac{0.88 \cdot 0.1}{0.12 \cdot 0.9} = 22/27 = 0.815$. Substituting $W_N = 100 - 0.12\alpha$ and $W_A = 50 + 0.88\alpha$,
>>$$50 + 0.88\alpha = \tfrac{22}{27}(100 - 0.12\alpha) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha^* = \frac{850/27}{44/45} = 32.2 .$$
>>Her wealth is $W_N = 96.14$ and $W_A = 78.33$, so she keeps part of the risk. Her certainty equivalent is $e^{0.9\ln 96.14 + 0.1\ln 78.33} = 94.19$, still above $93.30$. The insurer expects to earn $(0.12 - 0.10)\times 32.2 = 0.64$ from her.

>[!example] (Hard) The Price at Which She Walks Away
>This example uses the driver of the Medium example.
>
>>[!example]- Find the premium $\bar q$ above which she buys no coverage. Check the answer at $q = 0.2$. How does $\bar q$ change if the crash costs $L = 80$?
>>She buys nothing when $EU'(0) \le 0$, that is, when $\frac{u'(w-L)}{u'(w)} \le \frac{q}{1-q}\cdot\frac{1-p}{p}$. With log utility the left side is $\frac{w}{w-L} = \frac{100}{50} = 2$. The cutoff solves $\frac{q}{1-q}\cdot 9 = 2$, so $9q = 2 - 2q$ and $\bar q = 2/11 = 0.182$.
>>
>>At $q = 0.2$, $EU'(0) = -\frac{0.2 \times 0.9}{100} + \frac{0.8 \times 0.1}{50} = -0.0018 + 0.0016 < 0$. Since $EU$ is concave, it falls on all of $\alpha \ge 0$ and $\alpha^* = 0$. Solving the first-order condition as in the Medium example would give $\alpha = -6.25$, which violates $\alpha \ge 0$.
>>
>>With $L = 80$ the left side becomes $\frac{100}{20} = 5$. The cutoff solves $9q = 5(1-q)$, so $\bar q = 5/14 = 0.357$. A larger loss makes the accident state much poorer, so she still buys at a premium more than three times the fair price $p = 0.1$.
