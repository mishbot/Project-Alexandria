>[!note] Labor Supply
>The **labor supply** model applies [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|utility maximization]] to the choice of how many hours to work in a day. A person consumes $c$ units of a good at price $p$ and $l$ hours of **leisure**, meaning all time not spent working. She works $h = 24 - l$ hours at the hourly wage $w$ and also receives **non-labor income** $M$, such as an allowance. Her utility $u(c, l)$ is increasing in both goods. Her spending cannot exceed her income, $pc \le M + w(24 - l)$, which rearranges to
>$$pc + wl \le M + 24w, \qquad 0 \le l \le 24.$$
>Leisure is a good with price $w$, since each hour of leisure gives up an hour of wages, its [[6.5.1 Opportunity Cost|opportunity cost]]. The right side $M + 24w$ is **full income**, the person's money plus her **time endowment** of 24 hours valued at the market wage. The budget line ends at the **endowment point** $E = (24, M/p)$ in the $(l, c)$ plane, where she does not work.
>
>With the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = u(c, l) - \lambda\,(pc + wl - M - 24w)$, the first-order conditions $u'_c = \lambda p$ and $u'_l = \lambda w$ give the tangency condition
>$$\frac{u'_c}{u'_l} = \frac{p}{w}.$$
>The ratio of marginal utilities equals the ratio of the prices of consumption and leisure. For Cobb-Douglas utility $u(c, l) = c^{\alpha} l^{1-\alpha}$ with $0 < \alpha < 1$, the optimal choices are
>$$c^* = \alpha\,\frac{M + 24w}{p}, \qquad l^* = (1-\alpha)\left(24 + \frac{M}{w}\right), \qquad h^* = 24 - l^* = 24\alpha - (1-\alpha)\frac{M}{w}.$$
>The person spends the share $\alpha$ of full income on consumption and the share $1 - \alpha$ on leisure. Both goods are [[6.3.3 Income Changes|normal]]. Unlike standard [[6.4a Cobb-Douglas]] demand, $c^*$ depends on $w$, the price of the other good, because the person is endowed with time as well as money. At an interior solution, hours rise with the wage, $\partial h^*/\partial w = (1-\alpha)M/w^2 \ge 0$, and do not respond to it when $M = 0$. These formulas require $l^* \le 24$. If $(1-\alpha)(24 + M/w) \ge 24$, the person does not work, and the solution is the [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions|corner]] $l^* = 24$, $h^* = 0$, $c^* = M/p$.

# Intuition

A job turns time into money. The person owns 24 hours a day and sells some of them at the wage $w$. Every hour she keeps costs her $w$ in wages. She therefore buys leisure at price $w$, out of a full income that counts her time at its market value. More work buys more consumption and leaves less free time, and the person chooses her hours to balance the two.

The diagram plots leisure $l$ on the horizontal axis and consumption $c$ on the vertical axis. The budget line starts at the endowment point $E$, where the person does not work and consumes $M/p$. Each hour of work moves her one hour to the left and raises consumption by $w/p$, so the line has slope $-w/p$. It reaches $(M + 24w)/p$ on the vertical axis, the consumption she would have if she worked all 24 hours. The optimum $A$ is the point where the budget line touches the highest indifference curve $u^*$. Hours worked are read from the right, as the distance from $l^*$ to $24$.

# Properties

#### *(Full Income Shortcut)* The labor supply problem is a standard two-good problem with income $M + 24w$ and prices $p$ and $w$.

Any demand formula from [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]] applies once income is replaced by full income. For Cobb-Douglas, the fixed-share rule gives $c^* = \alpha(M + 24w)/p$ and $l^* = (1-\alpha)(M + 24w)/w$ at once. Two steps then finish the problem. First, check that $l^* \le 24$, and move to the corner at $E$ if it fails. Second, convert leisure into hours with $h^* = 24 - l^*$. A common error is to use $M$ instead of $M + 24w$ as income, which ignores the value of the person's time.

#### *(Two Effects of a Wage Rise)* A higher wage makes leisure more expensive and also makes the person richer.

The first change is a substitution effect. Each hour of leisure now costs more consumption, so the person moves along her indifference curve toward consumption and works more. The second change is an income effect. The person owns 24 hours, so a higher price of time raises her full income. Leisure is a normal good, so she buys more of it and works less. For a good the consumer only buys, a price rise makes her poorer. Both terms of the [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]] then reduce the demand for a normal good. Here the person sells time, so the income effect of the wage has the opposite sign. For general preferences, the total effect of the wage on hours is ambiguous.

#### *(Why the Allowance Matters)* With Cobb-Douglas utility, hours respond to the wage only through non-labor income.

The person spends $wl^* = (1-\alpha)(M + 24w)$ on leisure. The part $(1-\alpha)\,24w$ grows in proportion to $w$, the price of leisure, so it buys $(1-\alpha)\,24$ hours at every wage. For this part the substitution and income effects cancel. The part $(1-\alpha)M$ is a fixed number of dollars, so it buys $(1-\alpha)M/w$ hours, fewer when $w$ rises. This gives $\partial l^*/\partial w = -(1-\alpha)M/w^2 \le 0$. A person with no allowance works $24\alpha$ hours at any wage. A person with an allowance works more after a raise, because her allowance now buys fewer hours of leisure.

#### *(Corner at the Endowment)* A person with high non-labor income relative to her wage does not work at all.

The tangency formula can return $l^* > 24$, a point beyond the time limit. The budget line stops at $E$, so the best feasible point is then $E$ itself. The diagram shows this case. At $E$ the indifference curve through $E$ is steeper than the budget line. An hour of work would buy $w/p$ units of consumption. The person values an hour of leisure at more than that, so every hour of work lowers her utility. The point $T$ is the tangency that the formula finds, which needs more than 24 hours of leisure. The dashed indifference curve through $T$ is higher than $u(E)$, but no feasible bundle reaches it.

For Cobb-Douglas, the indifference curve at $E$ has slope $-u'_l/u'_c = -(1-\alpha)M/(24\alpha p)$. The person works only if the size of this slope is below $w/p$, that is, if
$$w > \frac{(1-\alpha)M}{24\alpha}.$$
The right side is the lowest wage at which she starts to work, her **reservation wage** for the first hour of work. It rises with her non-labor income $M$ and with her taste for leisure $1 - \alpha$. [[6.5.4 Taste-Based Discrimination]] takes a reservation wage $\bar u$ for each worker as given.

#### *(Slutsky With a Time Endowment)* In the Slutsky equation for leisure, the income term carries hours worked with a positive sign.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture does not decompose the effect of the wage formally. The equation below extends the Slutsky equation to a consumer who owns the good whose price changes.

Let $S < 0$ be the substitution effect, the change in leisure along a fixed indifference curve when $w$ rises. Then
$$\frac{\partial l^*}{\partial w} = S + (24 - l^*)\,\frac{\partial l^*}{\partial M}.$$
The second term is $h^*\,\partial l^*/\partial M$, positive when leisure is normal. A wage rise of $dw$ raises full income by $24\,dw$ and the cost of her current leisure by $l^*\,dw$. Her net gain is $h^*\,dw$, and she spends part of it on leisure. For Cobb-Douglas, $S = -\alpha l^*/w$ and the income term is $(1-\alpha)h^*/w$. Their sum is $-(1-\alpha)M/w^2$, which matches the direct calculation.

# Derivation

We want the Cobb-Douglas solution in the callout. Assume the solution is interior, $0 < l^* < 24$. Utility is strictly increasing in both goods, so the budget binds. The first-order conditions of the Lagrangian, as in [[7.2 Lagrange Multipliers]], are
$$u'_c - \lambda p = 0, \qquad u'_l - \lambda w = 0.$$
Dividing the first condition by the second eliminates $\lambda$. With $u'_c = \alpha c^{\alpha - 1} l^{1-\alpha}$ and $u'_l = (1-\alpha)c^{\alpha} l^{-\alpha}$, the ratio is
$$\frac{u'_c}{u'_l} = \frac{\alpha\, l}{(1-\alpha)\, c} = \frac{p}{w}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad wl = \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,pc.$$
Spending on leisure is a fixed multiple of spending on consumption. Substituting into the binding budget $pc + wl = M + 24w$ gives
$$pc\left(1 + \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\right) = \frac{pc}{\alpha} = M + 24w,$$
so $c^* = \alpha(M + 24w)/p$. Then $wl^* = (1-\alpha)(M + 24w)$, which gives $l^* = (1-\alpha)(24 + M/w)$ and $h^* = 24 - l^*$. Cobb-Douglas indifference curves are convex, so the tangency is a maximum. Differentiating gives $\partial c^*/\partial M = \alpha/p > 0$ and $\partial l^*/\partial M = (1-\alpha)/w > 0$, so both goods are normal. It also gives $\partial c^*/\partial w = 24\alpha/p > 0$ and $\partial l^*/\partial w = -(1-\alpha)M/w^2$, the results stated in the callout.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Campus Job
>A student receives an allowance of $M = \$30$ per day from her parents. A campus library job lets her work any number of hours at $w = \$15$ per hour. She spends all her income on a consumption good with price $p = \$1$. Her utility is $u(c, l) = c^{0.25} l^{0.75}$.
>
>>[!example]- How many hours does she work, and how much does she consume?
>>Full income is $M + 24w = 30 + 24 \times 15 = \$390$. She spends the share $0.25$ on consumption, so $c^* = 0.25 \times 390 = 97.5$. She spends the share $0.75$ on leisure, so $l^* = 0.75 \times 390 / 15 = 19.5$ hours. This is below 24, so the solution is interior. She works $h^* = 24 - 19.5 = 4.5$ hours.
>>
>>As a check, her earnings are $4.5 \times 15 = \$67.50$. Together with the allowance they give $\$97.50$, exactly her consumption. Leisure includes sleep, so 19.5 hours of leisure describes a day with a part-time shift.

>[!example] (Medium) A Raise at the Library
>The library raises the wage from $\$15$ to $\$20$ per hour. Two students have the same utility $u(c, l) = c^{0.25} l^{0.75}$ and face $p = 1$. The first has the $\$30$ daily allowance from the previous example. The second has no allowance.
>
>>[!example]- How do the hours of each student change, and why do they respond differently?
>>For the first student, $l^* = 0.75\,(24 + 30/w)$. At $w = 15$ this is $19.5$, and at $w = 20$ it is $0.75 \times 25.5 = 19.125$. Her hours rise from $4.5$ to $4.875$. Her consumption rises from $97.5$ to $0.25 \times (30 + 480) = 127.5$.
>>
>>For the second student, $M = 0$, so $l^* = 0.75 \times 24 = 18$ at every wage. She works 6 hours before and after the raise. Her consumption rises from $0.25 \times 360 = 90$ to $0.25 \times 480 = 120$, all from higher pay for the same hours.
>>
>>Both students spend $75\%$ of full income on leisure. For the second student, full income is $24w$, which rises in proportion to the price of leisure. The substitution and income effects of the raise therefore cancel. For the first student, the $\$30$ allowance does not grow with $w$. It buys $0.75 \times 30/15 = 1.5$ hours of leisure at the old wage and $0.75 \times 30/20 = 1.125$ hours at the new wage. The difference of $0.375$ hours is her extra work.

>[!example] (Hard) The Student Who Does Not Work
>A third student has the same utility $u(c, l) = c^{0.25} l^{0.75}$ and faces $p = 1$, but her daily allowance is $M = \$150$.
>
>>[!example]- Does she work at $w = 15$? What is the lowest wage at which she works, and how many hours does she work at $w = 20$?
>>The tangency formula gives $l^* = 0.75\,(24 + 150/15) = 25.5$ hours, which exceeds 24. No point on the budget line has more than 24 hours of leisure, so she chooses the corner $E$. She does not work and consumes her allowance, $c^* = 150$.
>>
>>She works only if the wage exceeds her reservation wage,
>>$$w > \frac{(1-\alpha)M}{24\alpha} = \frac{0.75 \times 150}{24 \times 0.25} = 18.75.$$
>>At this wage the formula gives exactly $l^* = 0.75\,(24 + 8) = 24$. At $w = 20$ it gives $l^* = 0.75\,(24 + 7.5) = 23.625$, so she works $0.375$ hours, or 22.5 minutes. Her consumption is $0.25 \times (150 + 480) = 157.5$, which is her allowance plus $0.375 \times 20 = \$7.50$ of earnings. Below $\$18.75$ her hours stay at zero, and above it they rise with the wage, since $\partial h^*/\partial w > 0$ when $M > 0$.
