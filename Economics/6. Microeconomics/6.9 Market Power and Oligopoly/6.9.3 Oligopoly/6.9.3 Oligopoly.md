>[!note] Oligopoly
>An **oligopoly** is a market with a small number $n$ of firms, such as two or five. It lies between the extremes of [[6.7.5 Profit Maximization and Supply|perfect competition]] and [[6.9.1 Monopoly|monopoly]]. Soft drinks (Coke and Pepsi), cell phone carriers, and car dealers are examples. Firm $i$ chooses its output $y_i$ to solve
>$$\max_{y_i}\ p(y_i + y_{-i})\,y_i - c(y_i), \qquad y_{-i} = \sum_{j \neq i} y_j ,$$
>with first-order condition $p'_Y(y_i + y_{-i})\,y_i + p - c'_y(y_i) = 0$, where $p'_Y$ is the slope of inverse demand at total output $Y = y_i + y_{-i}$. The condition cannot be solved until we know $y_{-i}$. The answer depends on what the rivals choose and on who moves when, so oligopoly needs game theory. Three models fix these two features.
>- In [[6.9.3a Cournot Competition|Cournot competition]], firms choose quantities at the same time. The equilibrium is a [[2.2c Nash Equilibrium|Nash equilibrium]], where each quantity is a best response to the others.
>- In [[6.9.3b Bertrand Competition|Bertrand competition]], firms choose prices at the same time and then supply what the market demands. With identical goods and equal constant marginal cost $c$, the unique Nash equilibrium is $p_1 = p_2 = c$.
>- In [[6.9.3c Stackelberg Competition|Stackelberg competition]], firm 1 chooses its quantity first, and firm 2 observes it before choosing. The equilibrium is [[3.2b Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibrium|subgame perfect]] and is found by backward induction.
>
>With two firms, inverse demand $p(Y) = a - bY$ ($a > c > 0$, $b > 0$), and constant marginal cost $c$, total output ranks as
>$$\frac{a-c}{2b} \;<\; \frac23\cdot\frac{a-c}{b} \;<\; \frac34\cdot\frac{a-c}{b} \;<\; \frac{a-c}{b},$$
>for monopoly, Cournot, Stackelberg, and Bertrand (equal to perfect competition). The markup $p - c$ falls in the same order, through $\frac{a-c}{2}$, $\frac{a-c}{3}$, $\frac{a-c}{4}$, and $0$. Industry profit and deadweight loss fall too, and consumer surplus rises.
>- By default, use Cournot for identical goods when firms commit to output or capacity before the price forms. Use Bertrand when firms post prices, the goods are identical, and either firm can serve the whole market at constant marginal cost. Use Stackelberg when one firm's quantity is fixed first and is visible to its rivals before they choose.

# Intuition

Every oligopoly model starts from the same fact. Each firm's output lowers the price that all firms receive. The firms as a group would earn the most by restricting output like a monopolist. Each firm alone gains by expanding output or by undercutting the price, because it keeps the extra sales and shares the cost of the lower price with its rivals. The models differ in how strongly this individual pull works, and two features decide it.

The first feature is the choice variable. When firms choose prices for identical goods, a cut of one cent wins the whole market, so undercutting continues until the price reaches marginal cost. When firms choose quantities, extra output lowers the price on the firm's own units, so each firm stops while the price is still above cost. The second feature is timing. A firm that commits to a quantity first can produce more, because the rival responds by producing less. The figure places each outcome on the demand curve $p = 100 - Y$ with $c = 10$. Moving down the demand curve, output rises, the price falls, and the markup shrinks from $45$ to $30$, $22.5$, and $0$.

# When to Use Which

Start with what the firms commit to. If output or capacity is set before the selling season and the price adjusts to clear the market, use Cournot. Cement plants and oil producers fit this case. If firms post prices and can supply any quantity demanded at those prices, use Bertrand. Two online sellers of the same camera fit this case. Then check the timing. If one firm's quantity is fixed first and its rivals observe it, use Stackelberg. An incumbent with a plant already built, facing a new entrant, fits this case. On an exam the wording decides it: "choose quantities simultaneously" is Cournot, "set prices" is Bertrand, and "firm 1 chooses first and firm 2 observes" is Stackelberg.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The caveats below go beyond the lecture. Unequal costs, capacity limits, and Cournot with $n$ firms were not covered.

Read the Bertrand prediction $p = c$ as an extreme case. It needs identical goods, equal costs, and unlimited capacity. When any of these fails, prices stay above cost, and a model with a positive markup, such as Cournot, fits better. The ranking of the models also holds the number of firms fixed. With $n$ Cournot firms the price is $\frac{a + nc}{n+1}$, which falls toward $c$ as $n$ grows. Collusion is a separate question. Firms that would like to act as a monopolist face a prisoner's dilemma in one-shot play, and only repeated play can sustain the cartel ([[4.1 Infinitely Repeated Game]]).
