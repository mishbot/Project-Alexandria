>[!note] Bayesian Bootstrap
>The **Bayesian bootstrap** (Rubin, 1981) replaces the equal-weight resampling of the [[1.5.1 Classical Bootstrap|classical bootstrap]] with random **Dirichlet-distributed weights** on the observed data points. Given a sample $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$, each Bayesian bootstrap replicate is generated by:
>1. Drawing a random weight vector $\mathbf{w}^{*} = (w_1^*, \dots, w_n^*)$ from a $\text{Dir}(1, 1, \dots, 1)$ distribution (i.e., uniform on the $(n-1)$-simplex).
>2. Computing the statistic using these weights: $T_n^* = g(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{w}^*)$.
>
>For the weighted mean, this gives $\bar{X}^* = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i^* x_i$. The distribution of $T_n^*$ across many draws of $\mathbf{w}^*$ approximates a **posterior distribution** over the parameter of interest.
>
>The Bayesian bootstrap arises from placing a **noninformative prior** on the space of all distributions. If the data-generating distribution $F$ is modeled nonparametrically via a [[2.2c.10 Dirichlet Distribution|Dirichlet process]] prior $F \sim \text{DP}(\alpha)$ with $\alpha \to 0$ (the limiting noninformative case), then the posterior over $F$ given the data assigns random weights to the observed values according to $\text{Dir}(1, \dots, 1)$—which is exactly the Bayesian bootstrap.
>
>>[!warning] Classical vs. Bayesian Bootstrap
>>The classical bootstrap resamples with replacement, producing **integer** counts for each observation (some appear $0, 1, 2, \dots$ times). The Bayesian bootstrap assigns **continuous** weights drawn from a Dirichlet, so every observation contributes to every replicate—just with varying importance. The classical bootstrap estimates the **sampling distribution** of a statistic; the Bayesian bootstrap estimates a **posterior distribution** over the parameter. Despite this philosophical difference, the two methods produce similar intervals in practice for smooth functionals.

# Intuition
The classical bootstrap answers: "how would my estimate vary across repeated samples from $\hat{F}_n$?" The Bayesian bootstrap answers a different question: "given the data I observed, what is my posterior belief about the parameter?"

The key insight is the connection to the [[2.2c.10 Dirichlet Distribution|Dirichlet distribution]]. In a nonparametric Bayesian framework, the unknown distribution $F$ is modeled as a random object. After observing data, the posterior over $F$ concentrates on distributions supported on the observed values, with random weights. Drawing these weights from $\text{Dir}(1, \dots, 1)$—the uniform distribution over all ways to allocate probability mass to $n$ points—corresponds to the most "uninformative" prior belief about $F$.

Each Bayesian bootstrap replicate represents one plausible version of $F$ drawn from the posterior. Computing the statistic under each such $F$ traces out the posterior distribution of the parameter, providing a fully Bayesian uncertainty quantification without requiring any parametric model.

# Properties
#### (*Dirichlet Weights*) The weight vector $\mathbf{w}^* \sim \text{Dir}(1, \dots, 1)$ has $\mathbb{E}[w_i^*] = 1/n$ and $\text{Var}(w_i^*) = \frac{n-1}{n^2(n+1)}$. By the [[2.2c.10 Dirichlet Distribution|Gamma construction]], $w_i^* = G_i / \sum_j G_j$ where $G_i \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ are independent.
#### (*Posterior Mean*) The Bayesian bootstrap mean satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}^* \mid \mathbf{x}] = \bar{x}$ (the sample mean) and $\text{Var}(\bar{X}^* \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{n+1}\hat\sigma^2$, where $\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i - \bar{x})^2$. This is close to but not identical to the classical bootstrap variance $\hat\sigma^2/n$.
#### (*Asymptotic Equivalence*) For smooth functionals (means, quantiles, regression coefficients), the classical and Bayesian bootstrap are asymptotically equivalent—both approximate the same limiting distribution as $n \to \infty$.
#### (*Credible Intervals*) The $(1-\alpha)$ Bayesian bootstrap interval is the $[\alpha/2, 1-\alpha/2]$ quantile range of $T_n^*$ over the Dirichlet replicates. Unlike frequentist confidence intervals, these can be interpreted directly as posterior probability statements (under the nonparametric prior).
#### (*Weighted Likelihood*) For parametric models, the Bayesian bootstrap generalizes to **weighted likelihood bootstrap**: maximize $\sum_{i=1}^n w_i^* \log f(x_i; \theta)$ for each draw of $\mathbf{w}^*$. The resulting distribution of $\hat\theta^*$ approximates the Bayesian posterior under a flat prior.

# Examples
>[!example] Bayesian Bootstrap of the Mean
>A sample of $n = 5$ observations is $\mathbf{x} = (2.1, 3.4, 2.8, 4.0, 3.2)$ with sample mean $\bar{x} = 3.1$.
>>[!example]- Procedure and Posterior
>>For each of $B = 10{,}000$ replicates, draw $(w_1^*, \dots, w_5^*) \sim \text{Dir}(1, 1, 1, 1, 1)$ and compute $\bar{X}^* = \sum_{i=1}^5 w_i^* x_i$. The posterior mean is $\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}^*] = 3.1$ and the posterior variance is
>>$$\text{Var}(\bar{X}^* \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{5}\sum_{i=1}^5 (x_i - 3.1)^2 = \frac{1}{6} \cdot 0.412 \approx 0.069,$$
>>giving a posterior standard deviation of $\approx 0.263$. A $95\%$ credible interval from the Dirichlet replicates might be approximately $[2.55, 3.60]$.

>[!example] Comparing Classical and Bayesian Bootstrap
>Given $n = 100$ observations from an unknown distribution with sample mean $\bar{x} = 50$ and sample standard deviation $s = 10$.
>>[!example]- Variance Comparison
>>The classical bootstrap variance of $\bar{X}^*$ is approximately $s^2/n = 100/100 = 1.0$. The Bayesian bootstrap variance is $\hat\sigma^2/(n+1) = 100/101 \approx 0.99$. For $n = 100$, the two are nearly identical. The practical difference only matters for very small samples, where the Bayesian bootstrap produces slightly narrower intervals reflecting the Dirichlet prior's regularization.
