>[!note] Binomial Distribution
>A discrete random variable $X \geq 0$ follows the **Binomial distribution** $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$ if it represents the number of **successes** in $n$ independent [[2.5b.3 Bernoulli Distribution|Bernoulli trials]], where each trial has a probability $p$ of success. Formally, the Binomial distribution of a random variable is given by the PMF,
>$$p_{X}(x)=\binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{n-x},$$
>where $\binom{n}{x}=\frac{n!}{x!(n-x)!}$ is the binomial coefficient, $p^{k}$ is the probability of exactly $k$ successes, and $(1-p)^{n-x}$ is the probability of the remaining trials being failures.


# Properties
#### (*Mean*) $\mathbb{E}[X \sim \text{Bin}(n,p)]=np.$
#### (*Variance*) $\text{Var}[X \sim \text{Bin}(n,p)]=np(1-p)$.

# Examples
>[!example] Packet Routing
>Consider a system with $n$ inputs and $n$ outputs. At each input, a packet appears independently with probability $p$. If a packet appears, it is destined for one of the $n$ outputs uniformly randomly, independently of the other packets.
>
>
>>[!example]- Let $X$ denote the number of packets destined for the first output. What is the distribution of $X$?
>>Let $A$ be the event that the packet appears, and let $B_{i}$ be the event that the packet hits the $i$-th output. There are $n$ independent Bernoulli trials, and each trial has the same probability of success $\frac{p}{n}$.
