
>[!note] Exponential Distribution
>A non-negative continuous random variable $X \geq 0$ follows the **Exponential distribution** $X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$ with parameter $\lambda > 0$, defined by the [[2.2c.1 Probability Density Function|probability density function]]
>$$f_{X}(x)=\begin{cases}\lambda e^{-\lambda x} &\text{if} \enspace  x \geq 0 \\ 0 &\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$
>The CDF for the Exponential distribution is $F_{X}(x)=1-e^{-\lambda x}$.
>
>The Exponential distribution represents the waiting time until the first occurrence of an event in a process where events occur continuously, independently, and at a constant average rate $\lambda$.
>
>Additionally, akin to the discrete [[2.2b.5 Geometric Distribution|Geometric distribution]], the exponential distribution is the only continuous PDF that has the **memoryless property**, which states that the probability of needing more than $m$ additional trials to get the first success is independent of the $k$ trials that have already been conducted, such that
>$$\mathbb{P}[X > k + m \mid X > k] = \mathbb{P}[X > m],$$
>showing that whether or not you start fresh or you have already had $k$ failures, the chance of needing more than $m$ additional trials remains the same.

# Properties
#### (*Mean*) $\mathbb{E}(X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda))= \frac{1}{\lambda}$.
#### (*Variance*) $\text{Var}(X \sim \operatorname{Exp}(\lambda))=\frac{1}{\lambda^2}$.
#### (*Discretization*) If $X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$, $\lfloor X \rfloor + 1 \sim \operatorname{Geom}(1-e^{-\lambda})$.
We know that the CDF of $W$ is $F_{W}(w)=1-e^{-\lambda w}$. Then, $F_{X}(x)=1-e^{-\lambda x}$ for $x \in \mathbb{N}$. We are trying to find the probability of W falling in the interval $[x, x+1)$ (since this continuous interval represents the floor of values of $x$). As such,
$$\begin{align}
f_{X}(x)&=F_{X}(x+1)-F_{X}(x) \\
&=1-e^{-\lambda(x+1)}-1+e^{-\lambda x} \\
&=e^{-\lambda x}(1-e^{-\lambda})\\
&=(e^{-\lambda})^x(1-e^{-\lambda}),
\end{align}$$
which represents a geometric distribution with $p=1-e^{-\lambda}$ by definition.

# Examples
>[!example] Exponential Order Statistics
>Let $X_{1}, \ldots, X_{n} \sim \operatorname{Exp}(\lambda_{i})$ be mutually independent. 
>>[!example]- What is the distribution of $\min(X_{1}, \ldots, X_{n})$?
>>Observe that since each variable $X_{i}$ denotes the time at which the $i$-th event occurs, then $\min(X_{1}, \ldots, X_{n})$ denotes the time until an event occurs. As such, the minimum will be much smaller than just a single $X_{i}$.
>>
>>Think about this in survival time terms. The survival time of $\min(X_{1}, \ldots, X_{n})$ is
>>$$\begin{align}
>>P(\min(X_{1}, \ldots, X_{n}) > t) &= P(X_{1} > t, \ldots, X_{n} > t) \\
>>&= P(X_{1}>t) \times \ldots \times P(X_{n} > t) \\
>>&= \prod_{i=1}^{n}e^{-\lambda_{i} t} \\
>>&= e^{-t \sum^{n}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}},
>>\end{align}$$
>>and therefore $\min(X_{1}, \ldots, X_{n}) \sim \operatorname{Exp}\left( \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_{i} \right)$.
>>
>>Think of it like $n$ independent phone lines, each getting random calls at their own rate $\lambda_i$. The time until the very first phone rings is faster because all the lines are “competing”–together, they get calls at the combined rate $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \dots + \lambda_n$.
