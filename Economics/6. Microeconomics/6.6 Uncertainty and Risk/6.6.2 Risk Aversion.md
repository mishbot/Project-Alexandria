>[!note] Risk Aversion
>A person with utility of money $u$, where $u' > 0$, is **risk averse** if for every lottery $x$ she weakly prefers the sure amount $E(x)$ to the lottery: $u(E(x)) \ge E(u(x))$. She is **risk neutral** if equality always holds, and **risk loving** if the reverse inequality always holds.
>- By **Jensen's inequality**, if $f$ is concave, then $f(E(x)) \ge E(f(x))$. If $f$ is strictly concave, the inequality is strict for every lottery with two or more distinct outcomes. [[2.5a Expectation]] states the same result for convex functions, with the inequality reversed.
>- A concave $u$ ($u'' \le 0$) therefore makes a person risk averse. Conversely, a person who is risk averse over every lottery has a concave $u$. Risk aversion is the same as diminishing marginal utility of money.
>- The **Arrow-Pratt measures** say how risk averse a person is at wealth $x$:
>$$r_A(x) = -\frac{u''(x)}{u'(x)}, \qquad r_R(x) = -\frac{u''(x)}{u'(x)}\,x = x\,r_A(x).$$
>- $r_A$ is **absolute risk aversion**, measured per dollar. $r_R$ is **relative risk aversion** and has no units. Both are positive where $u'' < 0$, zero for a linear $u$, and negative where $u'' > 0$. Both stay the same when $u$ is replaced by $a + bu$ with $b > 0$.
>- The **certainty equivalent** $\text{CE}$ is the sure amount with the same utility as the lottery, $u(\text{CE}) = E(u(x))$. The **risk premium** $\text{RP} = E(x) - \text{CE}$ is the expected money the person gives up to remove the risk. $\text{RP} > 0$ when $u$ is strictly concave and the lottery has two or more distinct outcomes. For a small risk, $\text{RP} \approx \tfrac12\, r_A(E(x))\, V(x)$.
>- Standard cases: $u = \ln x$ gives $r_A = 1/x$ and $r_R = 1$. $u = \sqrt x$ gives $r_A = 1/(2x)$ and $r_R = 1/2$. $u = -e^{-ax}$ with $a > 0$ gives $r_A = a$ and $r_R = ax$.
>- Standard example: a person with a strictly concave $u$ prefers a sure $25$ to $20$ or $30$ with equal odds, since $\tfrac12 u(20) + \tfrac12 u(30) < u(25)$.

>The lecture defined $r_A$ and $r_R$ and left examples to the problem set. In the callout, the invariance of $r_A$ and $r_R$ under $a + bu$, the certainty equivalent, the risk premium, and the approximation $\text{RP} \approx \tfrac12 r_A V$ are extensions. So are the Medium and Hard examples.

# Intuition

People buy insurance, hold bonds that pay less than stocks on average, and turn down fair coin flips for large stakes. Expected utility explains all three with one assumption: each extra dollar adds less utility than the dollar before. Compare a sure $25$ with a lottery that pays $20$ or $30$ with equal odds. Moving down to $20$ costs more utility than moving up to $30$ adds. The lottery is therefore worth less to the person than its mean.

In the diagram the chord joins the two outcomes of a fifty-fifty lottery. Its midpoint has height $E(u(x))$, which lies below $u(E(x))$ on the curve. Reading the height $E(u(x))$ back to the curve gives the certainty equivalent, the sure amount the person values as much as the lottery. The horizontal gap between $\text{CE}$ and $E(x)$ is the risk premium. A more curved $u$ bends further above the chord and makes the gap wider. The Arrow-Pratt measures put a number on this curvature.

# Properties

#### *(Direction of Jensen's Inequality)* Jensen's inequality turns concavity into risk aversion, and the definition of concavity turns risk aversion back into concavity.

Jensen's inequality starts from a concave $u$ and concludes $u(E(x)) \ge E(u(x))$ for every lottery. For the converse, pick wealth levels $x_1$ and $x_2$ and a probability $t \in (0, 1)$. The lottery that pays $x_1$ with probability $t$ and $x_2$ otherwise has mean $t x_1 + (1-t) x_2$. Risk aversion over this lottery reads $u(t x_1 + (1-t) x_2) \ge t\,u(x_1) + (1-t)\,u(x_2)$, the definition of a [[2.2 Convex Functions|concave function]]. This holds for every $x_1$, $x_2$, and $t$, so $u$ is concave, and $u'' \le 0$ when $u$ is twice differentiable. A person who turns down only some lotteries need not have a concave $u$.

>[!warning] Lecture Wording: Jensen Implies Concavity
>The lecture says that a person who dislikes uncertainty has $U(E(x)) \ge E(U(x))$, and that "Jensen's inequality then implies $U$ concave". Jensen's inequality goes from concavity to the inequality. The step from the inequality to concavity uses the two-outcome argument above.

#### *(Diminishing Marginal Utility)* With falling marginal utility, a loss of $h$ dollars costs more utility than a gain of $h$ dollars adds.

For a fifty-fifty gamble of $\pm h$ around wealth $x$,
$$u(x) - E(u) = \tfrac12\big[(u(x) - u(x-h)) - (u(x+h) - u(x))\big].$$
The first difference is the utility lost on the way down. The second is the utility gained on the way up. Each is an integral of $u'$ over an interval of length $h$. When $u'$ is decreasing, $u'$ is higher on the lower interval, so the loss exceeds the gain and the gamble lowers expected utility. Risk aversion and diminishing marginal utility of money are therefore the same property.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>This property and the two after it go beyond the lecture, which only defined $r_A$ and $r_R$.

#### *(Why Divide by $u'$)* $u''$ alone is not a measure of risk aversion, because it changes when $u$ is rescaled.

Replacing $u$ with $bu$ multiplies $u''$ by $b$, while [[6.6.1 Expected Utility|preferences over lotteries]] stay the same. Dividing by $u'$ cancels $b$, and adding a constant $a$ changes neither derivative. The measures also have a direct reading. $r_A(x) = -\frac{d}{dx}\ln u'(x)$ is the proportional rate at which marginal utility falls per extra dollar. $r_R(x) = -\frac{d \ln u'(x)}{d \ln x}$ is the percentage fall in marginal utility when wealth rises by one percent, an elasticity in the sense of [[6.8.3 Elasticities]].

#### *(Fixed Versus Proportional Stakes)* $r_A$ prices a gamble of fixed dollar size, and $r_R$ prices a gamble of fixed share of wealth.

By the small-risk approximation, a fifty-fifty gamble of $\pm h$ dollars has $\text{RP} \approx \tfrac12 r_A(x)\,h^2$. A gamble of $\pm s$ times wealth has $\text{RP}/x \approx \tfrac12 r_R(x)\,s^2$. With $u = \ln x$, $r_A = 1/x$ falls with wealth, so a richer person pays less to avoid a fixed $\$10$ gamble. Her $r_R = 1$ is constant, so she pays the same share of wealth to avoid a $\pm 40\%$ gamble at any wealth. With $u = -e^{-ax}$, $r_A = a$ is constant and the dollar premium for a fixed gamble does not depend on wealth. The Hard example computes both cases. [[6.6.4 Investment in a Risky Asset]] shows the same split for the share held in stocks.

#### *(Quadratic Risk Premium)* Doubling the size of a small gamble roughly quadruples the risk premium.

The variance of a $\pm h$ gamble is $h^2$, so $\text{RP} \approx \tfrac12 r_A h^2$ grows with $h^2$. In the animation $u = \ln x$, $E(x) = 25$, and $r_A(25) = 0.04$, so the approximation is $h^2/50$. As $h$ goes from $5$ to $20$, the chord drops away from the curve and the premium grows from $0.51$ to $10$. The approximation is close for small $h$ and too low for large $h$. For a tiny gamble the premium is of order $h^2$ while the stake is of order $h$, so a risk-averse person is nearly risk neutral over small risks.

# Derivation

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture did not derive or state this approximation.

We want the small-risk approximation $\text{RP} \approx \tfrac12 r_A(\mu)\,\sigma^2$. Write the lottery as $x = \mu + z$, where $\mu = E(x)$, $E(z) = 0$, and $V(z) = \sigma^2$. A second-order Taylor expansion of $u$ around $\mu$, with [[2.5a Expectation|linearity of expectation]], gives
$$E(u(x)) \approx u(\mu) + u'(\mu)\,E(z) + \tfrac12 u''(\mu)\,E(z^2) = u(\mu) + \tfrac12 u''(\mu)\,\sigma^2.$$
The certainty equivalent is $\mu - \text{RP}$. A first-order expansion gives $u(\mu - \text{RP}) \approx u(\mu) - u'(\mu)\,\text{RP}$. One order is enough on this side, because $\text{RP}$ is itself of order $\sigma^2$. Setting the two expressions equal, as $u(\text{CE}) = E(u(x))$ requires,
$$-u'(\mu)\,\text{RP} \approx \tfrac12 u''(\mu)\,\sigma^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{RP} \approx -\frac{u''(\mu)}{u'(\mu)}\,\frac{\sigma^2}{2} = \tfrac12\, r_A(\mu)\,\sigma^2 .$$

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Computing the Measures
>Four people have the utility functions $u = \ln x$, $u = \sqrt x$, $u = -e^{-ax}$ with $a > 0$, and $u = 7 + 3\ln x$.
>
>>[!example]- Find $r_A$ and $r_R$ for each person. Which person is most risk averse at $x = 100$?
>>For $\ln x$: $u' = 1/x$, $u'' = -1/x^2$, so $r_A = 1/x$ and $r_R = 1$. For $\sqrt x$: $u' = \tfrac12 x^{-1/2}$, $u'' = -\tfrac14 x^{-3/2}$, so $r_A = \tfrac{1}{2x}$ and $r_R = \tfrac12$. For $-e^{-ax}$: $u' = ae^{-ax} > 0$, $u'' = -a^2 e^{-ax}$, so $r_A = a$ and $r_R = ax$. For $7 + 3\ln x$ both derivatives triple, so $r_A = 1/x$ and $r_R = 1$, the same as $\ln x$.
>>
>>At $x = 100$ the values of $r_A$ are $0.01$, $0.005$, $a$, and $0.01$. The exponential person is the most risk averse whenever $a > 0.01$. A single number such as $r_A$ ranks people only at a given wealth.

>[!example] (Medium) A Fifty-Fifty Income Lottery
>Income is $20$ or $30$ with equal odds.
>
>>[!example]- Check that $\tfrac12 u(20) + \tfrac12 u(30) < u(25)$ for $u = \ln x$ and $u = \sqrt x$. Find each person's certainty equivalent and risk premium, and compare with $\tfrac12 r_A(25)\,V(x)$.
>>With $\ln x$: $E(u) = \tfrac12(2.9957 + 3.4012) = 3.1985 < \ln 25 = 3.2189$. Then $\text{CE} = e^{3.1985} = \sqrt{20 \cdot 30} = 24.49$ and $\text{RP} = 0.51$. With $\sqrt x$: $E(u) = \tfrac12(4.4721 + 5.4772) = 4.9747 < \sqrt{25} = 5$. Then $\text{CE} = 4.9747^2 = 24.75$ and $\text{RP} = 0.25$.
>>
>>The variance is $V(x) = \tfrac12 \cdot 25 + \tfrac12 \cdot 25 = 25$. The approximation gives $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{1}{25} \cdot 25 = 0.50$ for $\ln x$ and $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{1}{50} \cdot 25 = 0.25$ for $\sqrt x$. The log person has twice the relative risk aversion ($1$ against $\tfrac12$) and pays twice the premium.

>[!example] (Hard) Rich and Poor, Fixed and Proportional Stakes
>Consider two people, each at wealth $w = 25$ and again at $w = 100$. Lena has $u = \ln x$. Omar has $u = -e^{-0.1x}$. Each faces gamble G1, a fifty-fifty $\pm\$10$, and gamble G2, a fifty-fifty $\pm 40\%$ of wealth.
>
>>[!example]- Find each risk premium and explain the pattern with $r_A$ and $r_R$.
>>For Lena, $\text{CE}$ of a fifty-fifty gamble is the geometric mean of the outcomes. G1 at $w = 25$: $\text{CE} = \sqrt{15 \cdot 35} = 22.91$, so $\text{RP} = 2.09$. G1 at $w = 100$: $\text{CE} = \sqrt{90 \cdot 110} = 99.50$, so $\text{RP} = 0.50$. G2 at $w = 100$: $\text{CE} = \sqrt{60 \cdot 140} = 91.65$, so $\text{RP} = 8.35$. At $w = 25$, G2 is G1, with $\text{RP} = 2.09$. Both are $8.35\%$ of wealth.
>>
>>For Omar, $E(u) = -\tfrac12 e^{-0.1(w-h)} - \tfrac12 e^{-0.1(w+h)} = -e^{-0.1w}\cosh(0.1h)$. Solving $-e^{-0.1\,\text{CE}} = E(u)$ gives $\text{RP} = 10\ln\cosh(0.1h)$, with no $w$ in it. G1 costs him $10\ln\cosh 1 = 4.34$ at both wealths. G2 is $h = 10$ at $w = 25$ and $h = 40$ at $w = 100$, so it costs $4.34$ ($17\%$ of wealth) and $10\ln\cosh 4 = 33.07$ ($33\%$ of wealth).
>>
>>Lena's $r_A = 1/x$ falls with wealth and her $r_R = 1$ is constant. Her dollar premium for G1 falls as she gets richer, and her premium for G2 is a constant share. Omar's $r_A = 0.1$ is constant and his $r_R = 0.1x$ rises. His dollar premium for G1 is fixed, and his premium for G2 grows faster than his wealth.
