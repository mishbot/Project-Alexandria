>[!note] Probability Space
>A **probability space** is a triple $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ used to model random experiments and the associated outcomes. It consists of
>1. A **sample space** $\Upomega$, which represents the set of all possible single outcomes (**sample points**) $\omega \in \Omega$ of a random experiment. An **event** $A \subseteq \Omega$ is a subset of the sample space, and consists of one or more mutually exclusive sample points.
>2. A 
>3. A **probability measure** $\mathbb{P}: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ for each sample point (outcome) $\upomega \in \Upomega$, which follows the following axioms:
>	- (***Non-Negativity***) $0 \leq \mathbb{P}[\upomega] \leq 1$ for all $\upomega \in \Upomega$.
>	- (***Sum to One***) $\sum^{}_{\upomega \in \Upomega}\mathbb{P}[\upomega]=1$. 
>
>>[!warning] Completeness (Sum to One)
>The sum to one condition also means that a probability space must be collectively exhaustive so that no matter the outcome $\omega$ of the experiment, it will always be an element of the sample space. In other words, $P[\Omega] = 1$.

# Properties
#### (*Subsets*) If $A \subset B$, then $\mathbb{P}[A] < \mathbb{P}[B]$.

# Examples
>[!example] Birthday Paradox
>We want to calculate the probability of at least two people sharing the same birthday in a group of n people. We start by saying that $S=\set{1,...,365}$ since there are $365$ days to sample $n$ from. Then, $|\Upomega|=365^n$ (combinatorial sequence), since for each additional sample point, there are 365 available choices.
>
>We let A be the event that at least two people share the same birthday. Then we need to calculate $\mathbb{P}[A]$. However, it is easier to do so using the complement of A, $\mathbb{P}[\bar{A}]$, since it only takes into account instances where everyone has a different birthday. Since this is the case, $|\bar{A}|=\frac{365!}{(365-n)!}$ (FRC w/o replacement) since each person in the group has to have a different birthday. Therefore,
>$$\mathbb{P}[A]=1-\mathbb{P}[\bar{A}]=1-\frac{|\bar{A}|}{|\Upomega|}=1-\frac{365!}{(365-n)! \times 365^n}$$
>This means that in a group of 60 people, there is a 99% chance of two or more people sharing the same birthday.
