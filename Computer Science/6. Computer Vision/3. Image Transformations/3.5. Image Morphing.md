>[!note] Image Morphing
>**Image morphing** turns an image $I_0$ into an image $I_1$ through a sequence of frames indexed by $t \in [0, 1]$. Its goal is an image of the average object. Averaging the pixels of the two images does not give one. Morphing is warping plus cross-dissolving: the warp handles shape (geometry) and the cross-dissolve handles color (photometry).
>
>Both steps use the **affine combination** $aP + bQ$ with $a + b = 1$, usually written $(1 - t)P + tQ$. It interpolates for $t \in [0, 1]$ and extrapolates outside that range. $P$ and $Q$ can be points, colors, or whole images. A **cross-dissolve** alone, $(1 - t)I_0 + tI_1$, shows two faint overlapping objects (ghosting) when the objects are not aligned.
>
>The morph needs corresponding points $\mathbf{p}_k$ in $I_0$ and $\mathbf{p}'_k$ in $I_1$, for $k = 1, \dots, n$, and one triangulation of them, used in both images. For each $t$:
>1. Compute the intermediate shape $\mathbf{p}_k(t) = (1 - t)\,\mathbf{p}_k + t\,\mathbf{p}'_k$.
>2. For each triangle of the intermediate shape, find the [[3.1. Affine Transformations|affine maps]] $A_0$ and $A_1$ that send it to the matching triangles in $I_0$ and $I_1$.
>3. For each pixel $\mathbf{q}$ in that triangle, set $M_t(\mathbf{q}) = (1 - t)\,I_0(A_0\mathbf{q}) + t\,I_1(A_1\mathbf{q})$, with [[3.3. Image Interpolation|interpolation]] at the non-integer points $A_0\mathbf{q}$ and $A_1\mathbf{q}$. This [[3.2. Image Warping|inverse-warps]] both images to the intermediate shape and cross-dissolves them.
>4. Save $M_t$ as the next frame.
>
>A **mean face** of $N$ faces warps each face to the mean shape $\bar{\mathbf{p}}_k = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \mathbf{p}^{(j)}_k$ and then averages the pixels. A **caricature** pushes a face's shape away from the mean, to $\bar{\mathbf{p}}_k + \alpha(\mathbf{p}_k - \bar{\mathbf{p}}_k)$ with $\alpha > 1$. That is the morph from the mean shape to the face, evaluated at $t = \alpha$ outside $[0, 1]$.

# Intuition

A cross-dissolve fades one picture into the other. When the two objects sit in different places, the middle frame shows both of them, half transparent. Aligning the images first with one global warp fixes this when the objects differ by a global transformation, such as a shift or a rotation. Two different dogs, or two faces, also differ in proportions. No single affine map puts nose on nose and tail on tail at the same time. Corresponding points state which parts should match. A triangle mesh over those points fills in all pixels between them, and each triangle gets its own affine map. The result is a piecewise affine warp, flexible enough to follow the points and simple enough to compute per triangle.

The figure shows one triangle at $t = \tfrac{1}{4}$ from the first example below. The output pixel $\mathbf{q}$ lies in the intermediate triangle. It pulls a color from each image through the triangle's two affine maps, and the two colors are blended with weights $\tfrac{3}{4}$ and $\tfrac{1}{4}$.

# Properties

#### *(No Cracks Between Triangles)* Neighboring triangles agree on their shared edge, so the piecewise affine warp is continuous.

An affine map sends a line segment to the segment between the images of its endpoints. Two triangles that share an edge also share its two endpoints, and both maps send those endpoints to the same corresponding points. So both maps agree along the entire edge, and the warped image has no gaps or overlaps there. The warp is not smooth across edges: straight lines in the image can bend at triangle borders. The warp breaks down if a triangle flips orientation, which happens when one corresponding point crosses an edge of its triangle. The intermediate triangle then collapses to a segment at some $t$, and its affine map has no inverse. The third example below shows a case.

#### *(One Triangulation for All Frames)* The same triangles, listed by vertex index, are used in $I_0$, $I_1$ and every intermediate shape.

Triangle-to-triangle correspondence only exists if both meshes use the same index triples. Triangulating $I_0$ and $I_1$ separately can produce different triples. A common choice is to compute the triangulation once, with [[3.6. Delaunay Triangulation|Delaunay triangulation]], on the mean shape $\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{p}_k + \mathbf{p}'_k)$. Triangles that are well shaped there tend to stay reasonable in both images. Adding the four image corners as fixed corresponding points makes the mesh cover the full frame, including the background.

#### *(Morph Loop in Code)* The lecture's "life-hack" needs only two nested loops, one over $t$ and one over triangles.

Inside the triangle loop, all pixels of the triangle are handled at once: compute their source coordinates in both images and interpolate in one call. In the sketch below, `affine_from_triangles(src, dst)` returns $T_2T_1^{-1}$ from the triangle-to-triangle property of [[3.1. Affine Transformations]].

```python
tri = delaunay((pts0 + pts1) / 2)          # index triples, computed once
for t in linspace(0, 1, num_frames):
    pts_t = (1 - t) * pts0 + t * pts1      # 1. intermediate shape
    frame = zeros_like(im0)
    for idx in tri:                        # idx = [i, j, k], one triangle
        A0 = affine_from_triangles(pts_t[idx], pts0[idx])  # 2. shape t -> I0
        A1 = affine_from_triangles(pts_t[idx], pts1[idx])  #    shape t -> I1
        rows, cols = pixels_inside(pts_t[idx])        # e.g. skimage.draw.polygon
        q = stack([cols, rows, ones_like(rows)])      # homogeneous (x, y, 1)
        w0 = interpolate(im0, A0 @ q)                 # 3. inverse warp of I0
        w1 = interpolate(im1, A1 @ q)                 #    inverse warp of I1
        frame[rows, cols] = (1 - t) * w0 + t * w1     #    cross-dissolve
    save(frame)                                       # 4. next frame
```

The lecture uses the same $t$ for the shape and the color. Some implementations use a separate warp fraction and dissolve fraction, for example to change the shape early and the color late.

#### *(Shape and Appearance Vectors)* A face has a short shape vector and a long appearance vector, and warping to a common shape makes appearance vectors comparable.

The lecture's example uses $43$ annotated points, so the shape vector has $43 \times 2 = 86$ numbers. A $200 \times 150$ RGB photo is an appearance vector of $200 \times 150 \times 3 = 90{,}000$ numbers. Annotation is required, and in return it provides alignment. Averaging photos directly blurs every feature that sits in different places. Warping each photo to the mean shape first puts every eye on the same pixels, so the pixel average gives a sharp mean face. Averaging only the faces in a group gives subpopulation means, such as average male and female faces, happy faces, children, or people wearing glasses. Shape vectors are also the natural input for linear models of face shape, such as [[7.1. Principal Component Analysis|PCA]].

#### *(Extrapolation)* Weights outside $[0, 1]$ go past an endpoint, which exaggerates a difference instead of blending it.

The lecture's point example $P + 1.5(Q - P) = -0.5P + 1.5Q$ still has weights that sum to $1$, so it is a valid affine combination. With $P$ the mean shape and $Q$ one face, the result is a caricature. Every difference between the face and the mean grows by half. Applied to colors, the same rule can push values outside $[0, 255]$, so results must be clipped. The lecture's slides show extrapolation for points and mean faces for groups. Caricatures combine the two.

# Examples

>[!example] (Medium) One Pixel of a Face Morph
>A triangle of a face mesh has corners $(96, 104)$, $(132, 100)$, $(100, 148)$ in $I_0$ and the matching corners $(112, 88)$, $(164, 100)$, $(100, 116)$ in $I_1$, in pixel coordinates. Consider the frame at $t = \tfrac{1}{4}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the intermediate triangle. For the output pixel $\mathbf{q} = (110, 120)$, find $A_0\mathbf{q}$ and $A_1\mathbf{q}$. If $I_0$ there is $80$ and $I_1$ there is $160$, what is the output value?
>>The intermediate corners are $\tfrac{3}{4}\mathbf{p} + \tfrac{1}{4}\mathbf{p}'$, which gives $(100, 100)$, $(140, 100)$ and $(100, 140)$. Its unit-triangle matrix has edge columns $(40, 0)$ and $(0, 40)$ and origin $(100, 100)$. So $\mathbf{q}$ has unit-triangle coordinates $\beta = 10/40 = 0.25$ and $\gamma = 20/40 = 0.5$.
>>
>>The map to $I_0$ is
>>$$A_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 & -4 \\ -0.1 & 1.1 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A_0\mathbf{q} = (107, 125).$$
>>The map to $I_1$ is
>>$$A_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1.3 & -0.3 & 12 \\ 0.3 & 0.7 & -12 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A_1\mathbf{q} = (119, 105).$$
>>Both points have the same barycentric weights $(0.25, 0.25, 0.5)$ in their own triangles. As a check, $\tfrac{3}{4}(107, 125) + \tfrac{1}{4}(119, 105) = (110, 120) = \mathbf{q}$. Here both source points land on integer pixels. In general they land between pixels and need interpolation.
>>
>>The output value is $\tfrac{3}{4} \cdot 80 + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 160 = 100$. At $t = \tfrac{1}{4}$ the frame still looks mostly like $I_0$, in both shape and color.

>[!example] (Medium) Mean Eyes and a Caricature
>Three aligned face photos have their eye centers on one row, at these $x$-coordinates: face A at $60$ and $140$, face B at $70$ and $130$, face C at $65$ and $135$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the mean shape. What goes wrong if the three photos are averaged without warping? Find a caricature of face A with $\alpha = 1.5$.
>>The mean eye positions are $(60 + 70 + 65)/3 = 65$ and $(140 + 130 + 135)/3 = 135$, so the mean eye distance is $70$.
>>
>>Averaging the unwarped photos puts dark eye pixels at $60$, $65$ and $70$ on the left and at $130$, $135$ and $140$ on the right. Each is one third as dark, so each side shows a blurry smear of three faint eyes. Warping every face so that its eyes move to $65$ and $135$ first, then averaging, gives one sharp pair of eyes.
>>
>>Face A has eyes $5$ pixels farther out than the mean on each side. The caricature puts them at $65 + 1.5(60 - 65) = 57.5$ and $135 + 1.5(140 - 135) = 142.5$. The eye distance grows from $80$ to $85$, while the mean's is $70$. The offset from the mean grows from $10$ to $15$, so face A's wide-set eyes become more noticeable. The same numbers come from the morph between the mean and face A at $t = 1.5$.

>[!example] (Hard) A Triangle That Folds
>A user clicks the points of a triangle in both images: $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (10, 0)$, $C = (4, 6)$ in $I_0$. In $I_1$ the left and right mouth corners are clicked in the opposite order, so $B' = (4, 6)$ and $C' = (10, 0)$, with $A' = (0, 0)$.
>
>>[!example]- Track the signed area of the intermediate triangle. At which $t$ does the per-triangle warp fail?
>>The intermediate corners are $B(t) = (10 - 6t,\ 6t)$ and $C(t) = (4 + 6t,\ 6 - 6t)$, with $A$ fixed at the origin. The signed area is
>>$$\tfrac{1}{2}\left[(10 - 6t)(6 - 6t) - 6t(4 + 6t)\right] = 30 - 60t.$$
>>It is $30$ at $t = 0$, $15$ at $t = 0.25$, $0$ at $t = 0.5$, and $-30$ at $t = 1$. At $t = 0.5$ both $B(t)$ and $C(t)$ equal $(7, 3)$, so the triangle collapses to a segment. Its unit-triangle matrix is singular, and the affine maps $A_0$ and $A_1$ cannot be built. For $t > 0.5$ the triangle is mirrored, and it overlaps its neighbors in the mesh. The fix is to click the points in the same order in both images.
