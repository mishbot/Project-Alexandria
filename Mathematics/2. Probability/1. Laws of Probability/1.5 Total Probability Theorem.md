>[!note] Total Probability Theorem
>For a finite set of [[1.2 Mutually Exclusive Events|mutually exclusive events]] $A_1, \ldots, A_{N} \subseteq \Omega$ ($A_{i} \cap A_{j} = \emptyset$ for $i \neq j$) that form a partition of the sample space (i.e. every possible outcome $\omega \in \Omega$ is included in only one event $A_{i}$ for all $i \in [1, N]$), the **total probability theorem** states that if $\mathbb{P}[A_{i}] > 0$ for all $i$, the probability of any event $B \subseteq \Omega$ can be expressed as the sum of the joint probability of $B$ and every $A_i$, so that
>$$\mathbb{P}[B]=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\mathbb{P}[A_{i} \cap B],$$
>or as the sum of the [[1.3 Conditional Probability|conditional probabilities]] of $B$ given $A_i$, weighted by $\mathbb{P}[A_i]$, and vice-versa, such that
>$$\mathbb{P}[B]=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\mathbb{P}[B \mid A_i]\mathbb{P}[A_i]=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\mathbb{P}[A_{i} \mid B]\mathbb{P}[B].$$
# Intuition
Consider events $A_{1},A_{2}$ that form a partition of the sample space, such that $A_{1} \cup A_{2} = \Omega$. Consider another event $B$, which intersects with parts of $A_{1}$ and $A_2$ in the following figure:

Since $A_1 \cap A_{2} = \emptyset$ (i.e. $A_1$ and $A_2$ are mutually exclusive), so are $A_1 \cap B$ and $A_{2} \cap B$. Furthermore, these two intersections form a partition of $B$, such that $B = (A_{1} \cap B) \cup (A_{2} \cap B)$. Therefore, by the additivity axiom, $\mathbb{P}[B]=\mathbb{P}[A_{1} \cap B]+\mathbb{P}[A_{2} \cap B]$. Likewise, since $\mathbb{P}[B \mid A_{i}]$ is contained within a sample space $\Omega_{2}=A_{i}$, we must weigh it by $\mathbb{P}[A_{i}]$, as seen in 1.3.

# Examples
>[!example] Candies in a Jar
>You are taking out candies one by one from a jar that contains 10 red candies, 20 blue candies, and 30 green candies. 
>>[!example]- What is the probability that there are at least 1 blue candy and 1 green candy left in the jar when you have taken out all the red candies?
>>This problem requires a little bit more creative thinking. Let $r_{\max}$, $g_{\max}$, and $b_{\max}$ be the positions of the last red, green and blue candy in the sequence of 60 candies. Then, we are solving for the problem of 
>>$$\mathbb{P}(r_{\max} < g_{\max} \cap r_{\max} < b_{\max})=\mathbb{P}(r_{\max}<g_{\max}<b_{\max})+\mathbb{P}(r_{\max}<b_{\max}<g_{\max}),$$ since the two events are mutually exclusive.
>>
>>We abstract from the 60-candy sequence, and take a look at the two events. Since taking out each of the 60 candies is equally likely, and the blue candy is the last one, we can transform the first factor into
>>$$\mathbb{P}(r_{\max} < g_{\max} < b_{\max}) = \mathbb{P}(b_{\max}=60) \cdot \mathbb{P}(r_{\max} < g_{\max} \mid b_{\max} = 60) = \frac{20}{60} \cdot \frac{30}{40}.$$
>>Note that here $\mathbb{P}(r_{\max} < g_{\max} \mid b_{\max} = 60)=\mathbb{P}(r_{\max} < g_{\max})$, since knowing that a blue candy occupies slot 60 tells you nothing about how the red and green candies are interleaved among the remaining slots.
>>
>>Similarly, the second factor can be written as
>>$$\mathbb{P}(r_{\max} < b_{\max} < g_{\max}) = \mathbb{P}(g_{\max}=60) \cdot \mathbb{P}(r_{\max} < b_{\max} \mid g_{\max} = 60) = \frac{30}{60} \cdot \frac{20}{30}.$$
>>
>>Therefore, our final answer is
>>$$\mathbb{P}(r_{\max} < g_{\max} \cap r_{\max} < b_{\max})=\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{3}=\frac{7}{12}.$$
