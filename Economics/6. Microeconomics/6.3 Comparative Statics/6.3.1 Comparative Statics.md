>[!note] Comparative Statics
>**Comparative statics** studies how the solution of an optimization problem changes when one parameter changes and all other parameters stay fixed. An agent chooses $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_s)$ to maximize an objective $f(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{p})$, with or without constraints. The vector $\mathbf{p} = (p_1, \dots, p_n)$ collects the **parameters**, the quantities the agent takes as given, such as prices, income, wages, or tariffs. The problem has two outputs. The **optimal choice** is $\mathbf{x}^*(\mathbf{p})$, for example the [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|Marshallian demand]] $x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)$. The **optimal value** is $F(\mathbf{p}) = f(\mathbf{x}^*(\mathbf{p}); \mathbf{p})$, for example the [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function|indirect utility]] $v(\mathbf{p}, M)$. Each output has its own tool.
>
>For the choice, write the conditions that determine the optimum as a system $\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{p}) = \mathbf{0}$ of as many equations as unknowns. Without constraints these are the first-order conditions $G_k = \partial f / \partial x_k = 0$. Suppose $\mathbf{G}$ is continuously differentiable and the Jacobian $\partial \mathbf{G} / \partial \mathbf{x}$ has a nonzero determinant at the optimum. Then the [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] gives, by Cramer's rule,
>$$\frac{\partial x_i^*}{\partial p_k} = -\frac{\det\left(\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{G}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} \text{ with column } i \text{ replaced by } \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{G}}{\partial p_k}\right)}{\det \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{G}}{\partial \mathbf{x}}}.$$
>Here $\partial \mathbf{G}/\partial p_k$ is the column of derivatives of each equation with respect to the parameter $p_k$. With one choice variable $x$ and one parameter $p$, the formula reduces to $dx^*/dp = -f''_{x,p} / f''_{x,x}$.
>
>For the value, the [[7.3 Envelope Theorem|envelope theorem]] says that only the direct effect of the parameter counts. Without constraints, and with constraints $h_j(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{p}) = 0$ and multipliers $\lambda_j$,
>$$\frac{dF}{dp_k} = \frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x}^*(\mathbf{p}); \mathbf{p})}{\partial p_k}, \qquad \frac{dF}{dp_k} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_k} - \sum_{j=1}^{m} \lambda_j \frac{\partial h_j}{\partial p_k}.$$
>All derivatives are evaluated at the optimum. Both tools are local. They need an interior optimum, smooth functions, and a nonsingular Jacobian. At a point that satisfies the [[7.1 Unconstrained Optimality Conditions|second-order condition]], that condition fixes the sign of the Jacobian's determinant, so signing $\partial x_i^* / \partial p_k$ only requires signing the numerator. We apply both tools to the consumer's problem in the rest of this section.

# Intuition

Most questions in economics are comparative statics questions. A consumer's decision to buy a car depends on the price of oil and on the consumer's income. A car maker's output depends on the minimum wage and on the tariff on imported Japanese cars. Comparative statics asks how each decision changes when one of these parameters moves. In each case the agent solves a problem, one parameter changes, and we compare the new optimum with the old one. We do not study the path from one optimum to the other, which is where the word "statics" comes from.

The optimal choice is usually defined only implicitly, as the solution of the first-order conditions. Solving those conditions in closed form is often impossible. Even when a closed form exists, the sign of its derivative can be hard to read. With the implicit function theorem, we differentiate the first-order conditions at the optimum and never solve them. For the optimal value, the envelope theorem requires even less. We do not need to know how the choice moves, because at the optimum a small change in the choice has no first-order effect on the value.

The diagram shows a car plant that hires $L$ workers, produces $f(L)$ cars, sells each car at price $p$, and pays wage $w$. The first-order condition $pf'(L) = w$ says the plant hires until one more worker adds exactly $w$ dollars of revenue. A higher minimum wage moves the horizontal line up from $w$ to $w'$. The optimum slides up along the falling curve to a lower $L^*$. The size of the response depends on the slope of the curve. A flat curve, with $f''$ close to zero, gives a large fall in hiring. This is the formula $dL^*/dw = 1/(pf''(L^*))$ from the Medium example.

# Properties

#### *(Exam Recipe)* A derivative of the optimal choice can be signed in four steps, without solving for the choice.

First, write every condition that determines the optimum as an equation $G_k(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{p}) = 0$. With constraints, the Lagrange multipliers join the unknowns and the constraints join the equations. Second, compute the Jacobian $\partial \mathbf{G}/\partial \mathbf{x}$ with respect to all unknowns and check that its determinant is nonzero at the optimum. Third, replace the column of the unknown of interest with $\partial \mathbf{G}/\partial p_k$ and take the determinant. This column is mostly zeros, because a parameter usually enters only one or two equations. Fourth, sign the ratio. The denominator's sign comes from the second-order condition. The numerator's sign comes from the assumptions of the problem, such as $f'' < 0$ or the sign of a cross partial.

#### *(Sign of the Denominator)* At a point that satisfies the second-order condition, the sign of $\det(\partial \mathbf{G}/\partial \mathbf{x})$ is known from the second-order condition.

Without constraints the Jacobian of the first-order conditions is the Hessian $H$ of $f$. With one variable it is $f''_{x,x} < 0$, so $dx^*/dp$ has the sign of the cross partial $f''_{x,p}$. With two variables the Sylvester criterion requires $f''_{x_1x_1}f''_{x_2x_2} - (f''_{x_1x_2})^2 > 0$, so the denominator is positive. With $s$ variables, $\det H$ has the sign of $(-1)^s$. For two choices and one equality constraint, order the unknowns as $(\lambda, x_1, x_2)$ and the equations as $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\lambda = 0$, $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial x_1 = 0$, $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial x_2 = 0$, with $\mathcal{L} = u - \lambda(p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 - M)$. Writing the budget equation as $p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 - M = 0$ instead flips the sign of the determinant. With this ordering the Jacobian is the bordered Hessian of [[7.2 Lagrange Multipliers]], and its determinant is positive at a constrained maximum. [[6.3.3 Income Changes]] uses this to sign $\partial x_1^*/\partial M$.

#### *(Choice Versus Value)* The envelope theorem holds because the first-order conditions set the indirect effects to zero.

By the chain rule, $dF/dp_k = \sum_i (\partial f/\partial x_i)(\partial x_i^*/\partial p_k) + \partial f/\partial p_k$. At an interior optimum each $\partial f/\partial x_i$ is zero, so the sum drops out. The agent does re-optimize after the change. For a small change $dp_k$, the gain from re-optimizing is of order $(dp_k)^2$, so it vanishes relative to $dp_k$. The value derivative therefore needs no Jacobian and no determinant. It cannot answer a choice question, though. "How much better off is the consumer" is a value question. "How much more does the consumer buy" is a choice question. [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function]] uses the value tool to get $\partial v/\partial M = \lambda$, and [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]] uses it to get $\partial e/\partial p_i = h_i$.

#### *(Where the Tools Fail)* The formulas do not apply at corners, at kinks, or when the Jacobian is singular.

At a [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions|corner solution]] the first-order condition does not hold with equality, so the implicit function theorem does not apply. A consumer who buys none of a good often keeps buying none after a small price change. With [[6.4b Perfect Substitutes|perfect substitutes]] the second derivatives of $u$ are zero, so the bordered Hessian determinant is zero. Demand then jumps from one corner to the other when $p_1/p_2$ crosses $\alpha/\beta$. With [[6.4c Perfect Complements|perfect complements]] the utility has a kink at the optimum, so the derivatives in the formula do not exist. The results are also local: a large change can move the agent to a different optimum. In all these cases, solve the problem before and after the change and compare the two solutions directly.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Bakery Facing a Higher Price
>A bakery sells bread at a price $p$ per loaf that it takes as given. Baking $q$ loaves a day costs $q^2/100$ dollars. The bakery chooses $q$ to maximize profit $f(q; p) = pq - q^2/100$. The current price is $p = 3$.
>
>>[!example]- Find $dq^*/dp$ with the implicit function theorem and $d\pi^*/dp$ with the envelope theorem. Check both against the explicit solution.
>>The first-order condition is $G(q; p) = p - q/50 = 0$. Its derivatives are $G'_q = -1/50$ and $G'_p = 1$. The implicit function theorem gives $dq^*/dp = -G'_p/G'_q = 50$. Each extra dollar on the price leads the bakery to bake $50$ more loaves.
>>
>>The optimal profit is $\pi^*(p) = f(q^*(p); p)$. The envelope theorem keeps only the direct effect, $\partial f/\partial p = q$, evaluated at the optimum. At $p = 3$ the condition gives $q^* = 150$ loaves, so $d\pi^*/dp = 150$.
>>
>>The explicit solution confirms both answers. The condition gives $q^* = 50p$, whose derivative is $50$. Profit is $\pi^*(p) = 50p^2 - 25p^2 = 25p^2$, whose derivative at $p = 3$ is $50 \cdot 3 = 150$. The envelope answer needed only the value of $q^*$ at the current price. It did not need the whole function $q^*(p)$.

>[!example] (Medium) A Minimum Wage at a Car Plant
>A car plant hires $L$ workers and produces $f(L)$ cars, with $f' > 0$ and $f'' < 0$. It sells each car at a given price $p$ and pays each worker the wage $w$, so profit is $pf(L) - wL$. A new minimum wage raises $w$.
>
>>[!example]- Sign $dL^*/dw$ and $d\pi^*/dw$ without solving. Then take $f(L) = 20\sqrt{L}$, $p = 5$, $w = 10$, and compare the envelope estimate of the profit loss from a one-dollar wage rise with the exact loss.
>>The first-order condition is $G(L; w) = pf'(L) - w = 0$, with $G'_L = pf''(L)$ and $G'_w = -1$. So
>>$$\frac{dL^*}{dw} = -\frac{-1}{pf''(L^*)} = \frac{1}{pf''(L^*)} < 0.$$
>>The denominator is negative by the second-order condition. The plant hires fewer workers. By the envelope theorem, $d\pi^*/dw = \partial(pf(L) - wL)/\partial w = -L^*$. Profit falls by about $L^*$ dollars per dollar of wage increase.
>>
>>With the numbers, $f'(L) = 10/\sqrt{L}$ and the condition $50/\sqrt{L} = 10$ gives $L^* = 25$. Profit is $\pi^* = 5 \cdot 20 \cdot 5 - 10 \cdot 25 = 250$. Since $f''(25) = -5 \cdot 25^{-3/2} = -0.04$, the response is $dL^*/dw = 1/(5 \cdot (-0.04)) = -5$ workers per dollar. The envelope estimate of the loss from $w = 10$ to $w = 11$ is $L^* \cdot 1 = 25$.
>>
>>The exact solution is $L^* = 2500/w^2$ and $\pi^* = 2500/w$. Profit falls from $250$ to $227.27$, a loss of $22.73$. The estimate is slightly too large, because it holds hiring at $25$. After the rise the plant cuts hiring, and that adjustment can only raise its profit.

>[!example] (Hard) Robots and the Minimum Wage
>A car plant uses workers $L$ and robots $K$ to produce $F(L, K)$ cars. It sells cars at price $p$, pays wage $w$ per worker, and pays rent $r$ per robot. Profit is $pF(L, K) - wL - rK$. The optimum is interior and satisfies the second-order conditions $F''_{LL} < 0$ and $D = F''_{LL}F''_{KK} - (F''_{LK})^2 > 0$. These two conditions together also force $F''_{KK} < 0$. The minimum wage rises.
>
>>[!example]- Does the plant rent more or fewer robots? Answer first when workers operate the robots, so $F''_{LK} > 0$, and then when robots replace workers, so $F''_{LK} < 0$.
>>The first-order conditions are $G_1 = pF'_L - w = 0$ and $G_2 = pF'_K - r = 0$. The Jacobian with respect to $(L, K)$ and its determinant are
>>$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{G}}{\partial (L, K)} = \begin{pmatrix} pF''_{LL} & pF''_{LK} \\ pF''_{LK} & pF''_{KK} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det = p^2 D > 0.$$
>>The wage enters only the first condition, so $\partial \mathbf{G}/\partial w = (-1, 0)$. Replacing the $K$ column with this vector gives
>>$$\frac{\partial K^*}{\partial w} = -\frac{1}{p^2 D}\det\begin{pmatrix} pF''_{LL} & -1 \\ pF''_{LK} & 0 \end{pmatrix} = -\frac{pF''_{LK}}{p^2 D} = -\frac{F''_{LK}}{pD}.$$
>>The denominator is positive by the second-order condition. When workers operate the robots, $F''_{LK} > 0$ and the plant rents fewer robots. The wage rise cuts hiring, and with fewer workers each robot produces less.
>>
>>When robots replace workers, $F''_{LK} < 0$ and the plant rents more robots. Replacing the $L$ column instead gives $\partial L^*/\partial w = F''_{KK}/(pD) < 0$, so hiring falls in both cases. The robot answer depends only on the sign of $F''_{LK}$. No explicit solution was needed. A numerical check with $F = L^{0.3}K^{0.3}$, $p = 10$, $w = 2$, $r = 3$ gives $\partial K^*/\partial w = -0.51$, matching the formula.
