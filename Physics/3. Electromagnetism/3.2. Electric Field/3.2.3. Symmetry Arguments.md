>[!note] Symmetry Arguments
>A **symmetry** of a charge distribution is a move that leaves the charges looking exactly the same. The move can be a rotation, a reflection, or a shift. If the charges look the same after the move, the [[3.2.1. Electric Field|field]] they make must also look the same. This fixes the direction of the field, and sometimes what its size can depend on, before any calculation. The common cases:
>- **Spherical symmetry** belongs to a point charge or a ball whose density depends only on $r$. The field points radially, $\vec E = E(r)\,\hat r$. Its size depends only on the distance $r$ from the center.
>- **Cylindrical symmetry** belongs to an infinite uniform line. It also belongs to a cylinder whose density depends only on the distance from its axis. The field points straight away from or toward the axis. Its size depends only on the distance from the axis. This needs both rotation about the axis and shifts along it.
>- **Mirror symmetry** applies at a point on a mirror plane of the charges. There the field component perpendicular to the plane is zero. Each piece of charge has a mirror piece whose perpendicular contribution cancels its own.
>- **Mirror symmetry with a sign flip** applies when a reflection swaps each charge with an equal and opposite one. A dipole is the standard case. On the mirror plane, the components along the plane cancel, and the perpendicular components add.
>
>A symmetry counts only if both the **geometry** and the **charge distribution** have it. A uniformly charged line has mirror symmetry about every perpendicular plane. A line with positive charge on one half and negative charge on the other half has the same shape. It does not have the symmetry.
>
>Symmetry sets components to zero and makes magnitudes equal. It never gives the size of the field. That still comes from [[3.1.4. Coulomb's Law|Coulomb's law]], an integral, or [[3.3.2. Gauss's Law|Gauss's law]].

# Intuition

A field cannot prefer a direction that the charges do not prefer. Take a single point charge. A point has no shape, so up, down, left, and right all look the same from it. Suppose the field leaned slightly to the left. Rotating the picture would make it lean right, while the charge stayed unchanged. The only field that survives every rotation points straight outward. Its strength is the same at every point of a sphere around the charge.

Symmetry is the most useful tool in electrostatics. It turns integrals into algebra and cuts the work in half or more. In a sum over many charges, mirror pairs cancel one component before any number is computed. A component claimed to vanish by symmetry must vanish exactly. If the claim is wrong, half of the problem is lost.

The animation pairs pieces of a long line above and below a point P. With uniform charge, the components along the line cancel, and the sum points straight out. With the lower half negative, the outward components cancel instead. The sum then points along the line.

# Properties

#### *(Geometry Is Not Enough)* A symmetric shape carrying an unsymmetric charge has no symmetry.

A ring looks the same after any rotation about its axis. With charge spread evenly, the sideways components on the axis cancel. Suppose one half of the ring carries more charge than the other half. The shape is unchanged, but the sideways components no longer cancel. The field on the axis tilts away from the more positive half. Every symmetry argument must check the charge as well as the shape.

#### *(Pairs Remove an Integral)* Pairing each piece of charge with its mirror image removes one component from the calculation.

In a [[3.2.4. Electric Field of a Continuous Charge Distribution|slice-and-dice integral]], every piece $dq$ adds a small field $d\vec E$ with several components. When the distribution has a mirror plane through the field point, the components across that plane cancel in pairs. Only the components in the plane need integrating. For a ring of charge and a point on its axis, rotation symmetry removes both sideways components. One integral remains instead of three.

#### *(Size Depends on Fewer Variables)* Symmetry also limits what the field's size can depend on.

A field that depends on one variable instead of three makes Gauss's law usable. That is the main reason symmetry matters in [[3.3.2. Gauss's Law]]. For that use the direction must be known exactly everywhere on a surface, not only at a few points.

#### *(Draw the Field Lines)* Sketching the field lines is the fastest test of whether a symmetry is real.

Draw lines out of the positive charges and into the negative ones, using only what the arrangement allows. If the sketch forces the lines straight out from a center, an axis, or a plane, the symmetry is real. If some lines must curve in a way the sketch cannot pin down, the direction there is unknown. A sketch catches mistakes that a quick look at the shape alone misses. For a finite charged rod, rotation about the rod's axis fixes the lines on the axis. The mirror plane through the rod's middle fixes them on that plane. Everywhere else the lines curve, and only a calculation gives their direction.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Four Corners
>Four equal charges $+q$ sit at the corners of a square.
>
>>[!example]- What is the field at the center of the square?
>>The arrangement looks the same after a quarter turn about the center. Suppose the field at the center pointed somewhere. A quarter turn would make it point elsewhere, while the charges stayed the same. The only field that survives is zero. The four fields cancel in opposite pairs.

>[!example] (Medium) One Sign Flipped
>Two charges sit at $(\pm a, 0)$. Point P is at $(0, y)$ on the $y$-axis.
>
>>[!example]- Which component of the field at P vanishes when both charges are $+Q$? Which vanishes when the charge at $(-a, 0)$ is changed to $-Q$?
>>With two charges $+Q$, the $y$-axis is a mirror line of the charges. The $x$-components cancel, and the field points along the $y$-axis. Now put $+Q$ on the right and $-Q$ on the left. The mirror image of the right charge has the wrong sign. The field of $+Q$ points up and to the left, away from it. The field of $-Q$ points down and to the left, toward it. Now the $y$-components cancel and the $x$-components add, so the field points along $-x$.

>[!example] (Hard) Is the Direction Known Everywhere?
>Four charge distributions:
>- (a) a uniformly charged rod of finite length;
>- (b) a sphere with positive charge on its top half and equal negative charge on its bottom half;
>- (c) an infinite line with $\lambda > 0$ above a point O and $\lambda < 0$ below it;
>- (d) an infinite cylinder whose charge density depends only on the distance from its axis.
>
>>[!example]- For which ones does symmetry give the field direction at every point?
>>Draw the field lines in each case.
>>- (a) No. Rotation about the rod's axis fixes the lines on the axis. The mirror plane through the middle fixes them on that plane. Everywhere else the lines curve, because one end is closer than the other.
>>- (b) No. Lines leave the positive top half and curve around to the negative bottom half. Their exact shape is unknown.
>>- (c) No. The shape is symmetric, but the charge is not. Lines leave the positive half and curve to the negative half.
>>- (d) Yes. The charge looks the same after any rotation about the axis and any shift along it. The lines must point straight out from the axis everywhere.
>>Only (d) has a symmetry of both the shape and the charge that pins down every line. [[3.3.2b Cylindrical Symmetry]] finds its field with Gauss's law.
