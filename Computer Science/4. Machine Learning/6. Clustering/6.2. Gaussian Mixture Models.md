>[!note] Gaussian Mixture Models
>A **Gaussian mixture model** (GMM) describes data as draws from $K$ Gaussian components. Each point $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ has a hidden label $z_i \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ that names the component that produced it:
>$$z_i \sim \mathrm{Categorical}(\pi_1, \dots, \pi_K), \qquad \mathbf{x}_i \mid z_i = k \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\mu_k, \boldsymbol\Sigma_k).$$
>Here the mixing weights satisfy $\pi_k \ge 0$ and $\sum_k \pi_k = 1$, and $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ and $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k$ are the mean and covariance of component $k$. Summing over the hidden label gives the density of one point and the log-likelihood of the data:
>$$p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol\mu_k, \boldsymbol\Sigma_k), \qquad \ell(\boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol\mu_k, \boldsymbol\Sigma_k).$$
>Here $\boldsymbol\theta$ collects all weights, means and covariances. The sum inside the logarithm leaves $\ell$ with no closed-form maximizer. [[1.6 Bayes' Rule|Bayes' rule]] gives the **responsibility** of component $k$ for point $i$, the probability that $k$ produced the point:
>$$r_{ik} = P(z_i = k \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \frac{\pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol\mu_k, \boldsymbol\Sigma_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_j\, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol\mu_j, \boldsymbol\Sigma_j)}.$$
>The numbers $r_{i1}, \dots, r_{iK}$ sum to $1$ and form a **soft assignment** of point $i$. The [[3.11.5 EM Algorithm|Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm]] fits the model. It starts from initial parameters, often a k-means solution, and alternates two steps:
>
>- **E-step**: compute every $r_{ik}$ from the current parameters.
>- **M-step**: refit each component to all points, weighting point $i$ by $r_{ik}$.
>
>With the soft counts $n_k = \sum_i r_{ik}$, the M-step sets
>$$\pi_k = \frac{n_k}{n}, \qquad \boldsymbol\mu_k = \frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i=1}^{n} r_{ik}\,\mathbf{x}_i, \qquad \boldsymbol\Sigma_k = \frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i=1}^{n} r_{ik}\,(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)^T.$$
>The covariance update uses the new $\boldsymbol\mu_k$, and each term $(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)^T$ is a $d \times d$ outer product. The log-likelihood $\ell$ never decreases from one iteration to the next. EM stops when $\ell$ improves by less than a tolerance, at a stationary point that is usually a local maximum.
>
>Four covariance structures are common. They differ in cluster shape and in the number of covariance parameters:
>
>- **Full**: any ellipse, with $d(d+1)/2$ parameters per component.
>- **Diagonal**: ellipses aligned with the axes, with $d$ parameters per component.
>- **Spherical**: round clusters, $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k = \sigma_k^2\mathbf{I}$, with $1$ parameter per component.
>- **Tied**: one full covariance shared by all components, with $d(d+1)/2$ parameters in total.
>
>The likelihood is unbounded. A component that shrinks onto a single point drives $\ell \to \infty$, so implementations add a small $\epsilon\mathbf{I}$ to each $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k$. The number of components is chosen with the **Bayesian information criterion**,
>$$\mathrm{BIC} = -2\,\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}) + m \log n,$$
>where $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$ is the fitted model and $m$ counts its free parameters. With full covariances, $m = (K - 1) + Kd + Kd(d+1)/2$, and the $K$ with the smallest BIC wins. [[6.1. K-Means|K-means]] is the limit of EM with equal weights and $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}$ as $\sigma \to 0$. A GMM adds elliptical clusters of different sizes, soft assignments and a density.

# Intuition

The name lists the parts. A **mixture** draws each point from one of several sources, and here every source is a Gaussian. Picture the wait times at a café, pooled over quiet hours and rush hours. Each regime has its own bell curve, and the pooled histogram shows two humps. Nobody recorded which regime each customer came from. A GMM recovers both bell curves and the share of customers in each regime.

EM handles two unknowns that depend on each other. If the labels were known, each Gaussian would be the mean and covariance of its own points. If the Gaussians were known, Bayes' rule would give each label's probability. EM alternates between these two easy problems. It uses probabilities instead of hard labels, so a point between two clusters counts partly toward both.

# Properties

#### *(Weighted Sample Statistics)* Each M-step update is the usual estimate for one Gaussian, with every point weighted by its responsibility.

If every $r_{ik}$ were $0$ or $1$, the updates would give each cluster's share, mean and covariance. The covariance divides by $n_k$ and not $n_k - 1$, since it is a maximum likelihood estimate. The soft count $n_k$ acts as the number of points in cluster $k$, and it need not be an integer. Each outer product in the covariance update has rank one. The inner product $(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)^T(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)$ is a scalar instead. The weighted sum of inner products recovers only $n_k \operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol\Sigma_k$, the total variance, and loses every correlation.

#### *(Likelihood Never Decreases)* Each EM iteration raises $\ell$ or leaves it unchanged, which gives a built-in check on an implementation.

The proof in [[3.11.5 EM Algorithm]] uses a lower bound on $\ell$. The E-step makes the bound equal to $\ell$ at the current parameters. The M-step maximizes the bound in closed form. A drop in $\ell$ between iterations therefore signals a bug. The usual causes are errors in the covariance update or in the normalization of $r_{ik}$. The guarantee covers only convergence to a stationary point. Two components can end up sharing one true cluster while another cluster gets no component. EM cannot leave that state by itself. It also slows down when components overlap, since the responsibilities then change little between iterations. Practitioners run several starts and keep the best non-degenerate fit. Responsibilities are computed in log space, because Gaussian densities underflow when $d$ is large.

#### *(Collapsing Components)* The maximum likelihood estimate does not exist without constraints, because one component can shrink onto a single point.

Set $\boldsymbol\mu_k = \mathbf{x}_1$ and $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k = s^2\mathbf{I}$, and let the other components cover the remaining points. The density of component $k$ at $\mathbf{x}_1$ is $(2\pi s^2)^{-d/2}$, which grows without bound as $s \to 0$. Every other point keeps a positive density from the other components, so $\ell \to \infty$. The same failure occurs when a component's points span fewer than $d$ dimensions. This happens whenever it has $d$ or fewer points with real weight. Its covariance is then singular, with determinant zero. Scikit-learn adds $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ to every diagonal entry after each M-step. A prior on the covariances turns EM into [[1.2.2 Maximum a Posteriori Estimation|MAP estimation]] and removes the singularity. Diagonal or tied covariances need fewer points per component. A component whose weight or determinant collapses can also be restarted at a random point.

#### *(Covariance Shapes the Boundaries)* Tied covariances give flat boundaries between clusters, and separate full covariances give curved ones.

Compare $\log \pi_j\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol\mu_j, \boldsymbol\Sigma_j)$ for two components. With a shared $\boldsymbol\Sigma$, the quadratic term $\mathbf{x}^T\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x}$ cancels, and the boundary is a hyperplane. This is the algebra of linear discriminant analysis in [[4.2. Linear Discriminants]], with the labels unknown. Separate covariances keep the quadratic term, so the boundaries are quadrics, as in QDA. Fewer covariance parameters give a more stable fit on small data. More parameters allow flexible shapes but raise the risk of collapse. With $d = 50$, a full covariance has $1{,}275$ parameters per component, while a diagonal one has $50$.

#### *(Reading BIC)* BIC charges $\log n$ per parameter, so it never picks more components than AIC once $n \ge 8$.

The fitted $\ell$ rises as $K$ grows, since a larger mixture contains every smaller one. BIC approximates $-2$ times the log marginal likelihood of the model (Schwarz 1978). The Akaike information criterion (AIC) charges $2m$ instead of $m \log n$, and $\log n > 2$ once $n \ge 8$. BIC can still choose more components than there are visible groups. A skewed group may need two Gaussians to fit its shape. The components then describe pieces of a density. A separate merging step must recover the groups a person would see.

# Derivation

The E-step fixes the responsibilities. [[3.11.5 EM Algorithm]] shows that the M-step should then maximize the expected complete-data log-likelihood,
$$Q(\boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K} r_{ik}\left[\log \pi_k + \log \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol\mu_k, \boldsymbol\Sigma_k)\right].$$
Each Gaussian log-density is
$$\log \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma) = -\frac{d}{2}\log 2\pi - \frac{1}{2}\log\det\boldsymbol\Sigma - \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol\mu)^T\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol\mu).$$
The gradient of $Q$ with respect to $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ is $\sum_i r_{ik}\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)$. Setting it to zero and multiplying by $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k$ gives the weighted mean. The weights appear in $Q$ only through $\sum_k n_k \log \pi_k$, maximized subject to $\sum_k \pi_k = 1$. A [[4.2. Lagrangian Duality|Lagrange multiplier]] $\lambda$ gives $n_k/\pi_k = \lambda$ for every $k$. Summing $\pi_k = n_k/\lambda$ over $k$ gives $\lambda = n$, so $\pi_k = n_k/n$.

For the covariance, work with the precision $\boldsymbol\Lambda_k = \boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}$. A scalar equals its own trace, so $(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol\mu)^T\boldsymbol\Lambda(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol\mu) = \operatorname{tr}\big(\boldsymbol\Lambda(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol\mu)(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol\mu)^T\big)$. The terms of $Q$ that involve $\boldsymbol\Lambda_k$ are then
$$\frac{n_k}{2}\log\det\boldsymbol\Lambda_k - \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol\Lambda_k\mathbf{S}_k), \qquad \mathbf{S}_k = \sum_{i=1}^{n} r_{ik}(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)^T.$$
The gradient of $\log\det\boldsymbol\Lambda$ is $\boldsymbol\Lambda^{-1}$, and the gradient of $\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf{S})$ is $\mathbf{S}$ for symmetric $\mathbf{S}$. Setting $\frac{n_k}{2}\boldsymbol\Lambda_k^{-1} - \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{S}_k = \mathbf{0}$ gives $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k = \mathbf{S}_k/n_k$. The outer product enters through the trace identity.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Was It Rush Hour?
>Wait times at a café follow a two-component mixture. Quiet hours give $\mathcal{N}(3, 1^2)$ minutes with weight $0.7$. Rush hours give $\mathcal{N}(8, 2^2)$ minutes with weight $0.3$. One customer waits $5$ minutes.
>
>>[!example]- What is the density of a $5$-minute wait, and how likely is it that the customer came during rush hour?
>>The quiet density at $5$ is $e^{-2}/\sqrt{2\pi} \approx 0.0540$. The rush density is $e^{-9/8}/(2\sqrt{2\pi}) \approx 0.0648$. Weighting gives $0.7 \cdot 0.0540 = 0.0378$ and $0.3 \cdot 0.0648 = 0.0194$. The mixture density is their sum, $p(5) \approx 0.0572$. The rush responsibility is $0.0194/0.0572 \approx 0.34$. The rush curve alone gives $5$ more density. A $5$-minute wait is $1.5$ rush standard deviations from $8$ and $2$ quiet ones from $3$. The larger quiet weight still makes quiet hours the likelier source. K-means would assign the customer to the quiet center with certainty, since $2 < 3$.

>[!example] (Medium) One EM Iteration
>A smart meter records daily energy use of $2$, $3$, $4$, $8$ and $9$ kWh. A two-component GMM starts from $\pi = (0.5, 0.5)$, $\mu = (4, 6)$ and $\sigma^2 = (1, 1)$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the responsibilities, one M-step and the change in $\ell$.
>>With equal weights and variances, $r_{i2} = 1/\big(1 + \exp(-[(x_i - 4)^2 - (x_i - 6)^2]/2)\big)$. The five readings give $r_{i2} \approx 0.0025$, $0.0180$, $0.1192$, $0.9975$ and $0.9997$. The soft counts are $n_1 \approx 2.863$ and $n_2 \approx 2.137$, so $\pi \approx (0.573, 0.427)$. The weighted means are $\mu_1 \approx 2.964$ and $\mu_2 \approx 8.196$. The weighted variances are $\sigma_1^2 \approx 0.681$ and $\sigma_2^2 \approx 1.574$. The log-likelihood rises from $-16.91$ to $-9.53$. Further iterations converge to $\pi = (0.6, 0.4)$, $\mu = (3, 8.5)$ and $\sigma^2 = (2/3, 0.25)$, with $\ell \approx -8.47$. These are the share, mean and variance of the groups $\{2, 3, 4\}$ and $\{8, 9\}$.

>[!example] (Medium) How Many Delivery Zones?
>A courier fits GMMs with full covariances to $n = 500$ drop-off locations in $d = 2$ dimensions. The fitted log-likelihoods are $-2100$ for $K = 1$, $-1980$ for $K = 2$ and $-1968$ for $K = 3$.
>
>>[!example]- Which $K$ do BIC and AIC choose?
>>With $d = 2$, each component has $1$ weight, $2$ mean entries and $3$ covariance entries. One weight is fixed by the others, so $m = 6K - 1$, giving $5$, $11$ and $17$. With $\log 500 \approx 6.215$, BIC is $4231.1$, $4028.4$ and $4041.6$, so BIC picks $K = 2$. AIC is $4210$, $3982$ and $3970$, so AIC picks $K = 3$. The third component lowers $-2\ell$ by $24$. It adds $6$ parameters, which cost $6 \cdot 6.215 \approx 37.3$ under BIC and $12$ under AIC.

>[!example] (Hard) A Component That Collapses
>Return to the five meter readings. Component $1$ is the single Gaussian fit to all of them, with mean $5.2$, variance $7.76$ and weight $0.8$. Component $2$ sits on the reading $9$ with standard deviation $s$ and weight $0.2$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute $\ell$ for $s = 1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001$ and compare it with the sensible fit from the previous example. What rules out the collapse?
>>The log-likelihoods are $-11.78$, $-10.41$, $-8.15$ and $-5.86$. Below $s = 0.1$, each tenfold drop in $s$ adds about $\log 10 \approx 2.30$, from the reading at $9$ alone. Near $s \approx 0.014$, this degenerate model passes the sensible fit's $-8.47$, and below that it wins on likelihood. The likelihood alone prefers the degenerate model, so the fix must come from outside it. Suppose the meter reports to the nearest $0.1$ kWh. A spread below that resolution has no meaning, so a floor of $s \ge 0.1$ is justified. That floor caps this model at $\ell = -10.41$, below the sensible fit. A default floor such as $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ allows $s = 0.001$ and does not prevent the collapse here.
