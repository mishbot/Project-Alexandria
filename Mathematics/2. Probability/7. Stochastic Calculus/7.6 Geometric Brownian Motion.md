>[!note] Geometric Brownian Motion
>**Geometric Brownian motion** (GBM) is a model of a price whose small percentage changes are random. Their expected rate and their volatility stay constant. A stock price $S$ follows GBM with drift $\mu$ and volatility $\sigma$ when
>$$dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t, \qquad \text{or equivalently} \qquad \frac{dS}{S} = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t.$$
>Here $W_t$ is a [[7.1 Generalized Wiener Process|Wiener process]], $\mu$ is the expected rate of return per year, and $\sigma$ is the annualized standard deviation of returns. Both are constants, and the stock pays no dividends. The solution is
>$$S_t = S_0\exp\left(\left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t + \sigma W_t\right).$$
>The log price is normal, $\ln S_t \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\ln S_0 + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)t,\ \sigma^2 t\big)$. So $S_t$ has a [[2.2c.11 Lognormal Distribution|lognormal distribution]] and is always positive. Its mean and variance are
>$$\mathbb{E}[S_t] = S_0e^{\mu t}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_t) = S_0^2e^{2\mu t}\big(e^{\sigma^2 t} - 1\big).$$
>The continuously compounded return $x$, defined by $S_t = S_0e^{xt}$, is $x = \frac{1}{t}\ln(S_t/S_0) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu - \sigma^2/2,\ \sigma^2/t)$. The model assumes small moves in small time intervals, so there are no jumps. It also assumes a symmetric, normal distribution of log returns, so there are no fat tails (extreme moves that occur far more often than a normal distribution allows). GBM is the stock model behind the [[2.3.3e Black-Scholes-Merton Equation]] and the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula]]. The [[2.3.3c Binomial Tree|binomial tree]] with $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ and $d = 1/u$ is its discrete-time counterpart. Unlike the mean-reverting [[7.7 Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process|Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process]], GBM has no pull toward a long-run level, so the variance of $\ln S_t$ grows without bound.

# Intuition

A modeler needs a process for a stock price over the next ten years. The price must stay positive, and a typical dollar move should grow with the price. A [[7.1 Generalized Wiener Process|generalized Wiener process]] $dS = a\,dt + b\,dW_t$ fails both tests. Take $S_0 = \$100$, a drift of $a = \$10$ per year, and $b = 30$, so the standard deviation of a one-year change is $\$30$. After ten years the price is normal with mean $\$200$ and standard deviation $\$94.87$. It is below zero with probability $0.0175$. Its typical yearly move is also $\$30$ at every price level, the problem that [[7.3 Ito Process]] solves by letting both coefficients grow with the price.

The fix is to make the random part a percentage of the price, $dS/S = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t$. Each short step then multiplies the price by a factor close to $1$, so dollar moves grow with $S$. A product of positive factors stays positive. Taking logs turns the product into a sum, so the log price moves like a generalized Wiener process.

The log price drifts more slowly than $\mu$. A rise of $10\%$ followed by a fall of $10\%$ takes $\$100$ to $\$110$ and then to $\$99$. The two percentage moves average $0$, but the two log changes, $0.0953$ and $-0.1054$, average $-0.0050$. Each step loses about half its squared percentage move, here $0.10^2/2 = 0.005$. Over a year the squared percentage moves add up to about $\sigma^2$ ([[7.2 Quadratic Variation]]), so the log price loses $\sigma^2/2$ per year. This loss is the convexity term of [[7.5 Ito's Lemma|Itô's lemma]] applied to $\ln S$.

The figure shows four simulated paths with $S_0 = 100$, $\mu = 0.15$, and $\sigma = 0.40$ over one year, each in $60$ steps. No path comes near zero. The dashed curve is the mean $S_0e^{\mu t}$, which ends at $116.18$. The dotted curve is the median $S_0e^{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)t}$, which grows at the slower rate of the log price and ends at $107.25$. The mean lies above the median because a few paths rise far, while no path can fall below zero.

In the name, "geometric" refers to growth by multiplication, as in a geometric sequence. "Brownian motion" refers to the Wiener process that drives the log price. In the callout, the factor $S$ in $\sigma S\,dW_t$ comes from the percentage step. The exponential form of $S_t$ comes from taking logs, and it keeps the price positive. The $-\sigma^2/2$ in the exponent is the loss from moving up and down by the same percentage.

# Properties

#### *(Log Returns)* Log returns over separate time intervals are independent and normal, with parameters proportional to the interval length.

From the solution, the log return from $t$ to $t + \Delta t$ is
$$\ln\frac{S_{t+\Delta t}}{S_t} = \left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)\Delta t + \sigma\,(W_{t+\Delta t} - W_t) \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)\Delta t,\ \sigma^2\Delta t\right).$$
Increments of $W$ over separate intervals are independent, so the log returns are independent too. [[3.1f Historical Volatility]] relies on this. The sample standard deviation of returns over steps of length $\Delta t$, divided by $\sqrt{\Delta t}$, estimates $\sigma$. With daily data the factor $1/\sqrt{\Delta t}$ is $\sqrt{250}$. Over a day, simple returns and log returns differ very little, so either can be used. The dollar change $\Delta S$ is not normal, and its spread grows with the price level.

#### *(Mean Versus Median)* The mean price grows at rate $\mu$, but the median price grows at the lower rate $\mu - \sigma^2/2$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules give the mean and the distribution of the return $x$. The median and the probability of beating the mean are not stated.

The median of $S_t$ is $S_0e^{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)t}$, the exponential of the mean of $\ln S_t$. Half of the paths end above it and half below. The mean is higher because a small number of paths end with large gains. The price ends above its mean when $\sigma W_t > \sigma^2t/2$, so
$$\mathbb{P}\big[S_t > S_0e^{\mu t}\big] = N\left(-\frac{\sigma\sqrt{t}}{2}\right).$$
Here $N$ is the standard normal CDF, written $\Phi$ in [[2.2c.3 Gaussian Distribution]]. This probability is below $1/2$ and falls as $t$ grows. With $\mu = 0.10$ and $\sigma = 0.30$, the median grows at $5.5\%$ per year. The probability of ending above the mean is $0.4404$ after one year and $0.2266$ after $25$ years. The gap $\sigma^2/2$ between the two growth rates is the convexity term of [[7.5 Ito's Lemma]].

#### *(Long Horizons)* The annualized return becomes more certain over long horizons, while the total log return becomes less certain.

The return $x = \frac{1}{t}\ln(S_t/S_0)$ has standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{t}$, which shrinks as $t$ grows. The total log return $xt$ has standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{t}$, which grows. Take $\mu = 0.10$ and $\sigma = 0.30$. A $95\%$ interval for $x$ runs from $-53.3\%$ to $64.3\%$ over one year. Over $25$ years it runs from $-6.3\%$ to $17.3\%$. Both intervals are centered at $\mu - \sigma^2/2 = 5.5\%$. An investor who reads $\mu$ as the expected compounded annual return overstates it by $\sigma^2/2$.

#### *(Model Assumptions)* GBM rules out jumps and fat tails, and real prices show both.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules list the assumptions. The empirical evidence below is not covered.

The paths of $W_t$ are continuous, so a GBM price moves by small amounts in small intervals and never jumps. Log returns are normal, so they are symmetric, and moves of many standard deviations are extremely rare. The drift and the volatility are constant. Real prices break each of these assumptions. On 19 October 1987 the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell $22.6\%$ in one day. With $\sigma = 0.20$ per year, the daily standard deviation is $0.20/\sqrt{250} = 1.26\%$. The log return of that day, $\ln(1 - 0.226) = -0.256$, is about $20$ standard deviations below zero. A normal model gives such a day a probability near $10^{-91}$. Prices also jump on news, and volatility changes over time. The [[2.3.3e Black-Scholes-Merton Equation]] is derived under the GBM assumptions, because they make a continuously adjusted hedge riskless. Traders measure the gap between the model and option prices with the [[2.3.5b Volatility Surface|volatility surface]].

#### *(Risk-Neutral Version)* For option pricing, the same model is used with $\mu$ replaced by the risk-free rate $r$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules show that the risk-neutral expectation and the Black-Scholes-Merton equation give the same formula. The general reason, the Feynman-Kac formula, is not covered.

Under the risk-neutral probabilities of [[2.3.3b Risk-Neutral Valuation]], every asset earns the risk-free rate $r$ on average. The stock then follows GBM with drift $r$ and the same $\sigma$:
$$\ln S_T \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\ln S_t + \left(r - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)(T - t),\ \sigma^2(T - t)\right).$$
The volatility stays the same. This matches the binomial tree, where $u$ and $d$ depend on $\sigma$ but not on $\mu$. The [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula]] is the discounted expected payoff under this distribution. In that formula, $N(d_2)$ is the risk-neutral probability that $S_T > K$. The same price solves the [[2.3.3e Black-Scholes-Merton Equation]], a PDE in which $\mu$ does not appear. The [[7.8 Feynman-Kac Formula]] explains why a discounted expectation under this risk-neutral GBM solves that PDE.

# Derivation

We want to solve $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t$ for $S_t$. Without randomness ($\sigma = 0$) the equation is $dS/S = \mu\,dt$. Then $d(\ln S) = dS/S = \mu\,dt$, and integrating gives $S_t = S_0e^{\mu t}$.

With randomness, a first guess adds $\sigma W_t$ to the exponent: $G = S_0e^{\mu t + \sigma W_t}$. Apply [[7.5 Ito's Lemma|Itô's lemma]] with $x = W$, $a = 0$, and $b = 1$. The derivatives are $G_t = \mu G$, $G_x = \sigma G$, and $G_{xx} = \sigma^2G$, so
$$dG = \left(\mu + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)G\,dt + \sigma G\,dW_t.$$
The guess has the right volatility, but its drift is too high by $\sigma^2/2$. This excess is the extra term of Itô calculus. Subtract it in the exponent and try
$$S_t = S_0\exp\left(\left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t + \sigma W_t\right).$$
Now $G_t = (\mu - \sigma^2/2)G$, and the lemma gives $dS = (\mu - \sigma^2/2 + \sigma^2/2)S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t$. So this guess solves the equation.

Taking logs gives $\ln S_t = \ln S_0 + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)t + \sigma W_t$. Since $W_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t)$, the log price is normal with the mean and variance in the callout. Write $m = \ln S_0 + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)t$ and $s = \sigma\sqrt{t}$. For a level $Y > 0$, substituting $u = \ln x$ in the normal integral for $\ln S_t$ gives
$$\mathbb{P}[S_t \le Y] = \int_0^Y \frac{1}{s\,x\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\exp\left(-\frac{(\ln x - m)^2}{2s^2}\right)dx.$$
The integrand is the lognormal density with parameters $(m, s)$. The lognormal mean $e^{m + s^2/2}$ equals $S_0e^{\mu t}$. The lognormal variance $(e^{s^2} - 1)e^{2m + s^2}$ equals $S_0^2e^{2\mu t}(e^{\sigma^2 t} - 1)$. Finally, $x = \frac{1}{t}\ln(S_t/S_0) = \mu - \sigma^2/2 + \sigma W_t/t$. This is normal with mean $\mu - \sigma^2/2$ and variance $\sigma^2t/t^2 = \sigma^2/t$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Ending Above a Target
>A stock trades at $\$60$ and follows GBM with $\mu = 0.12$ and $\sigma = 0.25$ per year.
>
>>[!example]- Find the probability that the price is above $\$65$ in six months.
>>With $t = 0.5$, the log price is normal with mean $\ln 60 + (0.12 - 0.03125)(0.5) = 4.0943 + 0.0444 = 4.1387$. Its standard deviation is $0.25\sqrt{0.5} = 0.1768$. Then
>>$$\mathbb{P}[S_{0.5} > 65] = 1 - N\left(\frac{\ln 65 - 4.1387}{0.1768}\right) = 1 - N\left(\frac{4.1744 - 4.1387}{0.1768}\right) = 1 - N(0.2018) = 0.4200.$$
>>The expected price is $60e^{0.06} = \$63.71$, which is below the target. The chance of ending above $\$65$ is still $0.42$, because the spread of prices over six months is wide.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The next example uses the median and the probability of beating the mean from the Mean Versus Median property.

>[!example] (Medium) Mean and Median Over Three Years
>A growth stock trades at $\$100$ and follows GBM with $\mu = 0.15$ and $\sigma = 0.40$ per year.
>
>>[!example]- Find the mean and the median price in three years, the probability of ending above the mean, and the probability of a loss.
>>The mean is $100e^{0.15 \times 3} = 100e^{0.45} = \$156.83$. The median is $100e^{(0.15 - 0.08) \times 3} = 100e^{0.21} = \$123.37$. The log price has standard deviation $0.40\sqrt{3} = 0.6928$. The probability of ending above the mean is
>>$$N\left(-\frac{0.6928}{2}\right) = N(-0.3464) = 0.3645.$$
>>A loss means $S_3 < 100$, that is $\ln(S_3/100) < 0$. The log return has mean $0.21$, so
>>$$\mathbb{P}[S_3 < 100] = N\left(-\frac{0.21}{0.6928}\right) = N(-0.3031) = 0.3809.$$
>>The expected gain is $56.8\%$. Even so, the probability of a loss is $0.38$, and the probability of ending below the expected price is $0.64$.

>[!example] (Hard) An Interval for the Price
>A stock trades at $\$75$ and follows GBM with $\mu = 0.09$ and $\sigma = 0.28$ per year.
>
>>[!example]- Find a $95\%$ interval for the price in two years and for the annualized return $x$. Compare the price interval with the mean plus or minus $1.96$ standard deviations.
>>The log price has mean $\ln 75 + (0.09 - 0.0392)(2) = 4.3175 + 0.1016 = 4.4191$ and standard deviation $0.28\sqrt{2} = 0.3960$. A $95\%$ interval for $\ln S_2$ is $4.4191 \pm 1.96 \times 0.3960$, which is $[3.6430, 5.1952]$. The exponential keeps the probability, so
>>$$\mathbb{P}\big[\$38.21 < S_2 < \$180.41\big] = 0.95.$$
>>The interval is not symmetric around $e^{4.4191} = \$83.02$, the exponential of the mean log price. It reaches $\$44.82$ below that value and $\$97.39$ above it.
>>
>>The return $x$ is normal with mean $0.09 - 0.0392 = 0.0508$ and standard deviation $0.28/\sqrt{2} = 0.1980$. Its $95\%$ interval is $0.0508 \pm 1.96 \times 0.1980$, or $-33.7\%$ to $43.9\%$ per year.
>>
>>The mean of $S_2$ is $75e^{0.18} = \$89.79$. The standard deviation is $75e^{0.18}\sqrt{e^{0.1568} - 1} = \$37.00$. The interval $89.79 \pm 1.96 \times 37.00$ is $[\$17.28, \$162.30]$. Its total probability is close to $0.95$, but its two tails are very different. The probability below $\$17.28$ is $0.0000$, and the probability above $\$162.30$ is $0.0452$. The lognormal interval puts $0.025$ in each tail.
