>[!note] Current Density
>The charges that carry a current are called **charge carriers**. In a metal they are free electrons. In salt water they are positive and negative ions. Let each carrier have charge $q$, and let the **carrier density** $n$ be the number of carriers per unit volume. A field $\vec E$ inside the material pushes each carrier with the force $q\vec E$. The carriers keep colliding with atoms, so their average velocity stays constant. This average velocity is the **drift velocity** $\vec v_d$. It points along the force $q\vec E$, and its size is proportional to the field.
>
>When carriers drift at right angles across a cross-section of area $A$, the current through it has size
>$$I = n|q|v_dA.$$
>Here $n$ is the carrier density, $|q|$ is the size of each carrier's charge, $v_d$ is the drift speed, and $A$ is the area.
>
>The **current density** $\vec J$ is the current per unit area of cross-section. Its unit is $\text{A/m}^2$. As a vector it is
>$$\vec J = nq\vec v_d.$$
>Here $q$ keeps its sign, and $\vec v_d$ is the drift velocity. For a flow spread evenly over an area $A$, the size is $J = I/A$. In general $\vec J$ changes from point to point. The current through a surface is then the flux of $\vec J$ through it:
>$$I = \int \vec J\cdot d\vec A.$$
>Here $d\vec A$ is the area vector of a small piece of the surface. Its direction is the chosen positive direction for the current.
>
>In most materials the current density is proportional to the field:
>$$\vec J = \sigma\vec E.$$
>The constant $\sigma$ is the **conductivity** of the material. Here $\sigma$ means conductivity, not surface charge density. It measures how easily the material carries a current.

# Intuition

A density is an amount per unit size. Mass density is mass per unit volume. Current density is current per unit area of cross-section. A thick wire and a thin wire can carry the same current. The thin wire then has the larger current density, because the same current passes through less area.

To drift means to move slowly in one direction while other motion goes on. Each carrier bounces between atoms in random directions. The field adds a small steady motion along one direction. The random motion averages to zero, so only the steady part moves charge from one end to the other.

Two electrons make the same random flights between collisions, one with no field and one in a field. Only the second drifts, and it drifts against $\vec E$. Then a cylinder of length $v_d\,dt$ shows where $I = n|q|v_dA$ comes from.

# Derivation

Draw a flat surface of area $A$ across the conductor, at right angles to the drift. In a short time $dt$, each carrier moves a distance $v_d\,dt$. Every carrier within that distance of the surface, on the side the carriers come from, crosses it during $dt$. These carriers fill a cylinder with base $A$ and length $v_d\,dt$. The cylinder holds $nAv_d\,dt$ carriers. The charge they carry across has size
$$dQ = n|q|\,Av_d\,dt.$$
Here $n|q|$ is the size of the carrier charge per unit volume, and $Av_d\,dt$ is the cylinder's volume. Dividing by $dt$ gives the current $I = n|q|v_dA$. Dividing by $A$ gives the size of the current density, $J = n|q|v_d$.

The direction of $\vec J$ follows from the force. The drift velocity points along the force on each carrier, so $\vec v_d$ is proportional to $q\vec E$. Substituting into $\vec J = nq\vec v_d$ gives
$$\vec J \propto nq\,(q\vec E) = nq^2\vec E.$$
Here $q^2$ is positive for either sign of $q$. The current density therefore points along $\vec E$ whether the carriers are positive or negative. The constant of proportionality collects $n$, $q^2$, and the effect of the collisions. Naming it $\sigma$ gives $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$.

# Properties

#### *(Current Density Follows the Field)* The current density points along $\vec E$ for positive and negative carriers alike.

Electrons in a metal drift against the field, because their charge is negative. Their current density $nq\vec v_d$ still points along the field. Both $q$ and $\vec v_d$ have the opposite sign from those of a positive carrier, and the two signs cancel. The current is the flux of $\vec J$, so its direction also follows $\vec E$. This is the physical reason for the convention in [[3.6.1. Electric Current]]. Inside a conductor that carries a current, the field points from the higher-potential end to the lower one. The current therefore flows from high potential to low potential. Outside a supply, it leaves the positive terminal and returns to the negative one.

#### *(Opposite Carriers Add)* When positive and negative carriers drift in opposite directions, their currents add.

In a classroom demonstration, a battery, a switch, and a lamp formed one loop with a gap between two metal contacts. With air in the gap, the lamp stayed dark. Tap water in the gap did not light it either. The tap water was soft and held few dissolved ions. Pure water holds even fewer, because only a tiny fraction of its molecules split into ions. Pouring table salt into the water added sodium ions $\text{Na}^+$ and chloride ions $\text{Cl}^-$, and the lamp lit. More salt made it brighter, because a larger carrier density carries a larger current. In the field between the contacts, the sodium ions drift along $\vec E$ and the chloride ions drift against it. By the property above, each kind of ion has a current density along $\vec E$. The two current densities point the same way and add, although the ions move in opposite directions.

#### *(Current Is a Flux)* The current through a surface is the flux of $\vec J$, as [[3.3.1. Electric Flux|electric flux]] is the flux of $\vec E$.

The integral $\int \vec J\cdot d\vec A$ counts only the part of $\vec J$ that crosses the surface at right angles. A surface parallel to the flow has no current through it. The surface is usually open, such as a cross-section of a wire. The direction of $d\vec A$ is then a choice, and reversing it flips the sign of $I$. When $\vec J$ is uniform and crosses an area $A$ at right angles, the integral gives $I = JA$. When $J$ varies across the area, $I = JA$ holds only with the average value of $J$. The integral gives that average, as in the Hard example.

#### *(Conductivity Is a Material Property)* The conductivity depends on the material and its temperature, not on the object's size or shape.

The constant $\sigma$ collects the carrier density, the carrier charge, and the collisions. None of these depend on the length or thickness of the object. Its unit follows from $\sigma = J/E$, which gives $(\text{A/m}^2)/(\text{V/m}) = \text{A}/(\text{V}\cdot\text{m})$. [[3.6.3. Resistivity]] writes this unit with the ohm. In some crystals the conductivity differs from one direction to another, and $\sigma$ becomes a matrix. These notes treat $\sigma$ as one number. The current density is not a material property, because it depends on the applied field.

#### *(Drift Is Slow)* In a metal wire, the drift speed is a fraction of a millimeter per second.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture gave no numerical drift speeds and did not explain why a lamp lights as soon as its switch closes. Giancoli section 25.8 covers both.

Copper has about $8.5 \times 10^{28}$ free electrons per cubic meter. With so many carriers, a small drift speed carries a large current, as the Easy example shows. A lamp still lights almost at once when its switch closes. The field spreads along the wire at a large fraction of the speed of light. Electrons all along the wire then start to drift at nearly the same moment. No single electron has to travel from the switch to the lamp.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Drift Speed in a Copper Wire
>A copper wire of diameter $1.0\ \text{mm}$ carries a current of $2.0\ \text{A}$. Copper has $n = 8.5 \times 10^{28}$ free electrons per cubic meter.
>
>>[!example]- Find the current density and the drift speed.
>>The cross-sectional area is $A = \pi(0.50 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m})^2 = 7.85 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^2$. The current density is
>>$$J = \frac{I}{A} = \frac{2.0}{7.85 \times 10^{-7}} = 2.55 \times 10^{6}\ \text{A/m}^2.$$
>>Here $I$ is the current and $A$ the area of the cross-section. From $J = n|q|v_d$ with $|q| = e$, the drift speed is
>>$$v_d = \frac{J}{ne} = \frac{2.55 \times 10^{6}}{(8.5 \times 10^{28})(1.602 \times 10^{-19})} = 1.87 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m/s}.$$
>>Here $n$ is the electron density and $e$ the electron charge. That is about $0.19\ \text{mm/s}$. An electron needs about $90$ minutes to drift one meter along the wire.

>[!example] (Medium) A Wire That Narrows
>A copper wire of diameter $2.0\ \text{mm}$ is joined to a copper wire of diameter $1.0\ \text{mm}$. A steady current of $3.0\ \text{A}$ flows through both. Copper has $n = 8.5 \times 10^{28}\ \text{m}^{-3}$ and $\sigma = 5.95 \times 10^{7}\ \text{A}/(\text{V}\cdot\text{m})$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the current density, the drift speed, and the field in each wire.
>>In a steady state the same current crosses every cross-section, as in [[3.6.1. Electric Current]]. Both wires carry $3.0\ \text{A}$. The areas are $3.14 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2$ for the thick wire and $7.85 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^2$ for the thin one. The current densities are
>>$$J_1 = \frac{3.0}{3.14 \times 10^{-6}} = 9.55 \times 10^{5}\ \text{A/m}^2, \qquad J_2 = \frac{3.0}{7.85 \times 10^{-7}} = 3.82 \times 10^{6}\ \text{A/m}^2.$$
>>Here subscript 1 marks the thick wire and subscript 2 the thin wire. The drift speed is $v_d = J/(ne)$, with $ne = 1.36 \times 10^{10}\ \text{C/m}^3$. It is $7.01 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{m/s}$ in the thick wire and $2.81 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m/s}$ in the thin one. The field is $E = J/\sigma$. It is $0.0160\ \text{V/m}$ in the thick wire and $0.0642\ \text{V/m}$ in the thin one.
>>
>>Half the diameter means a quarter of the area. The thin wire therefore has four times the current density, the drift speed, and the field. The electrons drift faster where the wire narrows, so the same charge passes each second.

>[!example] (Hard) A Rod With Uneven Conductivity
>A rod of radius $a = 1.5\ \text{mm}$ has a conductivity that is largest on its axis and falls to zero at its surface. A uniform field along the axis drives a current density along the axis. Its size depends on the distance $r$ from the axis:
>$$J(r) = J_0\left(1 - \frac{r^2}{a^2}\right),$$
>with $J_0 = 2.0 \times 10^{6}\ \text{A/m}^2$. Here $J_0$ is the current density on the axis, and $J$ falls to zero at the surface.
>
>>[!example]- Find the current, the average current density, and the radius inside which half the current flows.
>>The current density varies across the area, so the current needs the flux integral. Slice the cross-section into thin rings, as in [[3.2.4. Electric Field of a Continuous Charge Distribution|slice and dice]]. A ring of radius $r$ and width $dr$ has area $dA = 2\pi r\,dr$, and the current through it is $J(r)\,dA$. Adding the rings gives
>>$$I = \int_0^a J_0\left(1 - \frac{r^2}{a^2}\right)2\pi r\,dr = 2\pi J_0\left(\frac{a^2}{2} - \frac{a^2}{4}\right) = \frac{\pi J_0a^2}{2}.$$
>>Here the first term in the bracket comes from the $1$, and the second from the $r^2/a^2$. With the numbers, $I = \pi(2.0 \times 10^{6})(1.5 \times 10^{-3})^2/2 = 7.07\ \text{A}$. The average current density is $I/(\pi a^2) = J_0/2 = 1.0 \times 10^{6}\ \text{A/m}^2$. Multiplying $J_0$ by the area would give twice the current.
>>
>>The current inside a radius $r_h$ follows from the same integral with upper limit $r_h$:
>>$$I(r_h) = \pi J_0\left(r_h^2 - \frac{r_h^4}{2a^2}\right).$$
>>Here $I(r_h)$ is the current through the disk of radius $r_h$. Set it equal to half the total, $\pi J_0a^2/4$, and write $x = r_h^2/a^2$. This gives $x - x^2/2 = 1/4$, or $x^2 - 2x + \tfrac12 = 0$. The root below $1$ is $x = 1 - 1/\sqrt2 = 0.293$. So $r_h = a\sqrt{0.293} = 0.541a = 0.81\ \text{mm}$. Half the current flows inside $0.81\ \text{mm}$, a disk that covers only $29\%$ of the area.
