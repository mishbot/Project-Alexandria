>[!note] Standing Wave
>A standing wave is a wave pattern that results from the constructive interference (superposition) of two waves of the same frequency and amplitude traveling in opposite directions. Unlike traveling waves, standing waves appear stationary, with certain points (called nodes) remaining at rest, while other points (called antinodes) oscillate with maximum amplitude. The vertical displacement of a standing wave at distance $x$ and time $t$ is represented by the function
>$$D(x, t) = 2A\sin(kx)\cos(\omega_{n}t),$$
>where $k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$ is the wave's wavenumber ($\lambda$ is the wave's wavelength), $\omega_{n}=\frac{2\pi}{T}$ is the wave's natural frequency ($T$ is its period), and $A$ is its peak $y$-axis displacement (amplitude).
>
>A standing wave contains two types of points:
>1. **Nodes**, where the wave displacement is always $0$ due to destructive interference.
>2. Antinodes, where the wave displacement is always $D$ due to constructive interference.
>
>Furthermore, a standing wave can occur at multiple frequencies $f_{n}$, known as **resonant frequences**. More specifically,
>$$f_{n} = \frac{v}{2\ell}n = nf_{1}$$
>where $n \in \mathbb{N}$, guides the frequencies at which a standing wave occurs. Here, $f_{1}=\frac{v}{2\ell}$ represents the **first harmonic** of the wave, whereas frequencies of $n>1$ are known as $n$-th **harmonics**, or $(n-1)$-th **overtones**.
# Intuition
Since the only thing that distinguishes the two waves from one another is their direction, and we have
$$D(x, t)=A\sin(kx \pm  \omega_{n} t + \phi),$$
by the definition of the transverse wave, we have the superimposed wave
$$D_{3}(x, t)=D_{1}(x, t)+D_{2}(x,t),$$
where $D_{1}(x,t)=A\sin(kx -  \omega_{n} t + \phi),$ is the wave traveling to the right of the $x$-axis, and $D_{2}(x,t)=A\sin(kx +  \omega_{n} t + \phi)$ is the wave traveling to the left. Then,
$$\begin{align}
D_{3}(x, t) &= A\sin(kx -  \omega_{n} t + \phi) + A\sin(kx + \omega_{n} t + \phi) \\
&= 2A\sin(kx)\cos(\omega_{n}t).
\end{align}$$
