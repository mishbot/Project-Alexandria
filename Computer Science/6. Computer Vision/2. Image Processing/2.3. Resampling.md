>[!note] Resampling
>A digital image stores **samples**, the values of a continuous image function $f(x, y)$ at the points of a grid. **Reconstruction** turns the samples back into a continuous function, which amounts to guessing what $f$ did between them. **Resampling** moves the image to a new grid. We reconstruct $f$ and sample it again, more sparsely (**downsampling**) or more densely (**upsampling**).
>
>Downsampling by a factor of $2$ keeps every other row and every other column. For an $H \times W$ image $X$ indexed from $0$, the operation is
>$$\operatorname{down}_2(X)[i, j] = X[2i,\, 2j].$$
>The output has $\lceil H/2 \rceil \times \lceil W/2 \rceil$ pixels, about one quarter as many. In NumPy it is `X[::2, ::2]`. A $256 \times 256$ image becomes $128 \times 128$, then $64 \times 64$.
>
>Upsampling by $2$, written $\operatorname{expand}_2(X)$, places the old samples at the even positions of a grid with twice the height and width. It fills the new positions by [[3.3. Image Interpolation|interpolation]]. Nearest-neighbor interpolation copies each pixel into a $2 \times 2$ block. Linear interpolation (bilinear in 2D) sets each new sample to the average of its nearest old samples.
>
>The two operations are not inverses. With this alignment $\operatorname{down}_2(\operatorname{expand}_2(X)) = X$, because the old samples survive at the even positions. In general $\operatorname{expand}_2(\operatorname{down}_2(X)) \neq X$. Downsampling discards three quarters of the samples, and interpolation cannot recover detail that fell between the kept ones. Downsampling without a blur also causes aliasing, so the correct shrink is $\operatorname{down}_2(\operatorname{blur}(X))$, as explained in [[2.4. Anti-Aliasing]]. Repeating blur-and-shrink builds the Gaussian levels of an [[2.5. Image Pyramids|image pyramid]].

>[!warning] Downsample Versus Subsample
>The lecture says "filter the image, then subsample", so there **subsample** means keeping every other pixel with no filtering. Discussion 4 defines $\operatorname{down}_2$ the same way, as a pure subsample. Some libraries and texts use "downsample" for the filtered version. In these notes $\operatorname{down}_2$ never blurs, and every anti-aliasing blur is written out explicitly.

# Intuition

Many tasks need the same picture at another size. A thumbnail needs fewer pixels. Coarse-to-fine alignment in Project 1 searches a small copy first. Blending works on a whole ladder of sizes. The name describes the operation. We sample the same underlying scene function a second time, on a different grid. Digital audio shows the 1D version. Recording writes down samples of a sound wave, and playback must fill in the gaps between them before a speaker can play it.

The diagram shows the asymmetry. Shrinking throws away samples. Whatever lived only in the white cells is gone, or worse, it shows up later as a false pattern. Growing adds samples but no information. Every light cell on the right is a fixed mix of the dark cells, so an upsampled image looks soft. A grid can always be made coarser safely after a blur. It can never be made finer without guessing.

# Properties

#### *(Averaging Is Blur Plus Subsample)* Averaging each $2 \times 2$ block is a $2 \times 2$ box filter followed by $\operatorname{down}_2$.

Discussion 1 writes the vectorized version as `(X[0::2, 0::2] + X[0::2, 1::2] + X[1::2, 0::2] + X[1::2, 1::2]) / 4`. It is the cheapest downsample that blurs before it subsamples, and it removes the finest checkerboard pattern exactly. The box is a weak low-pass filter, since its spectrum is a sinc with side lobes ([[2.1.3. Blurring and Sharpening]]). A Gaussian blur before $\operatorname{down}_2$ suppresses aliasing more evenly, which is why pyramids use it.

#### *(Upsampling Adds No Detail)* An upsampled image has four times the pixels and no new information.

Each new pixel of $\operatorname{expand}_2(X)$ is a fixed weighted sum of old pixels, so the image carries the same information in more numbers. Nearest-neighbor copies create sharp block edges. Those edges are artifacts set by the old samples, and they hold no scene detail. With smooth interpolation, almost all of the content stays below the highest frequency the small grid could hold. That is why $\operatorname{expand}_2(G_{k+1})$ looks blurry next to $G_k$. The difference $G_k - \operatorname{expand}_2(G_{k+1})$ is therefore the fine detail, and it becomes a level of the Laplacian pyramid in [[2.5. Image Pyramids]].

#### *(Odd Sizes)* Slicing rounds sizes up, so expanding does not always return the original shape.

`X[::2]` on $255$ rows keeps $128$ rows, and $\operatorname{expand}_2$ turns those into $256$ rows. Pyramid code that subtracts $\operatorname{expand}_2(G_{k+1})$ from $G_k$ must crop or pad to match shapes. Cropping the input to a multiple of $2^N$ before building $N$ levels avoids the problem.

#### *(Float Before Arithmetic)* Resampling arithmetic on `uint8` arrays wraps around and truncates.

NumPy adds `uint8` arrays modulo $256$. With pixels $200$ and $100$, `(a + b) / 2` returns $22$, since $200 + 100$ wraps to $44$. Converting first with `img.astype(np.float32) / 255` gives the intended $150/255$. Casting back with `astype(np.uint8)` truncates, so $255.2$ becomes $255$ and $0.6$ becomes $0$. Discussion 1 shows that an out-of-range $256.0$ can wrap to $0$. Clip to the valid range before any cast.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) The Finest Checkerboard
>A $4 \times 4$ image $C$ is a checkerboard of black ($0$) and white ($1$) pixels, with $C[0, 0] = 0$. Its mean brightness is $0.5$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute $\operatorname{down}_2(C)$ and the $2 \times 2$ block average. Then apply nearest-neighbor $\operatorname{expand}_2$ to each. Which result better represents the image?
>>Every kept pixel $C[2i, 2j]$ has an even coordinate sum, so it is black. Hence $\operatorname{down}_2(C)$ is a $2 \times 2$ image of zeros. If the grid started one column to the right (`C[::2, 1::2]`), every kept pixel would be white. A one-pixel shift of the scene flips the result from black to white. Either way the mean is wrong ($0$ or $1$ instead of $0.5$). This is an alias at frequency zero.
>>
>>Each $2 \times 2$ block holds two black and two white pixels, so the block average is $0.5$ everywhere. The pattern is gone, but the brightness is right.
>>
>>Nearest-neighbor $\operatorname{expand}_2$ turns either result into a constant $4 \times 4$ image ($0$, $1$, or $0.5$). No interpolation brings the checkerboard back. A $2 \times 2$ grid cannot hold a pattern that changes every pixel, so losing it is unavoidable. Showing a false solid color is avoidable, by averaging (blurring) first.

>[!example] (Medium) A Round Trip on One Row
>Take the row $\mathbf{x} = [10, 40, 50, 20, 30, 60]$ and use linear $\operatorname{expand}_2$. Old samples go to the even positions. Each new sample in between is the average of its two neighbors, and the last new sample copies its left neighbor.
>
>>[!example]- Compute $\operatorname{down}_2(\operatorname{expand}_2(\mathbf{x}))$ and $\operatorname{expand}_2(\operatorname{down}_2(\mathbf{x}))$. Where is the second one wrong, and why there?
>>Expanding first gives $[10, 25, 40, 45, 50, 35, 20, 25, 30, 45, 60, 60]$. Keeping the even positions returns $[10, 40, 50, 20, 30, 60] = \mathbf{x}$ exactly.
>>
>>Shrinking first keeps $[10, 50, 30]$. Expanding gives $[10, 30, 50, 40, 30, 30]$. The error $\mathbf{x} - \operatorname{expand}_2(\operatorname{down}_2(\mathbf{x}))$ is
>>$$[0,\ 10,\ 0,\ -20,\ 0,\ 30].$$
>>The kept positions are exact, and all the error sits at the discarded positions. Linear interpolation assumes the row moves in a straight line between kept samples. The row rose from $10$ to $50$ unevenly, so the guess at position $1$ is off by $10$. It dipped to $20$ between $50$ and $30$, so the guess there is off by $20$. The final $60$ has no kept sample to its right, so the end rule copies $30$ and misses by $30$. Any wiggle shorter than two pixels is invisible after the shrink.
