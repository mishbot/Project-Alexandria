>[!note] Short Selling
>**Short selling** is the sale of an asset the seller does not own. The short seller borrows the asset, usually shares lent through her broker, and sells it at today's price $S_0$. Later she buys the asset back and returns it to the lender. Buying back is called **covering**. The position gains when the price falls. A short sale of $N$ shares has three sets of cash flows.
>- At the start, the short seller sells the borrowed shares and receives $NS_0$.
>- While the position is open, she pays the lender every dividend ($D$ per share) and a **borrow fee**.
>- At the close, she buys $N$ shares at the market price $S_T$ and returns them.
>
>Let $b$ be the annual fee rate, charged in this note as simple interest on the day-0 value $NS_0$. Let $T$ be the holding time in years. The short seller's profit and loss (P&L) is
>$$\Pi = N(S_0 - S_T) - ND - bTNS_0.$$
>The gain is at most $NS_0$ before dividends and the fee, reached if the price falls to zero. The loss has no upper limit, since $S_T$ can rise without bound.
>
>The broker keeps the sale proceeds in a **margin account**. The short seller adds an initial margin deposit $m_INS_0$. With no interest on the account, its cash stays at $A = (1 + m_I)NS_0$. At price $S$ she owes shares worth $NS$, so her equity and her margin ratio are
>$$\text{equity} = A - NS, \qquad \text{margin ratio} = \frac{A - NS}{NS}.$$
>The equity is what the account would keep after buying the shares back. When the ratio falls below the maintenance margin $m_M < m_I$, the broker makes a margin call. The short seller must add cash, or the broker buys the shares back and closes the position. In this note a stock margin call restores the ratio to $m_M$. The deposit due at price $S$ is then $(1 + m_M)NS - A$. A futures margin call restores the initial margin instead ([[2.2.3 Futures]]). The first call comes once the price rises above the trigger price
>$$S^* = \frac{(1 + m_I)\,S_0}{1 + m_M}.$$
>Here $m_I$ and $m_M$ are fractions of the value of the shares owed. The deposit is collateral that the broker returns at the close, so it is not a cost. The lender can also recall the shares at any time, which can force an early close. A short sale has the same exposure to the price as a short [[2.2.2a Forward Contract|forward contract]]. It also lets arbitrageurs sell an overpriced asset they do not hold ([[4.2.2a Arbitrage]]).

# Intuition

Owning an asset pays off only when its price rises. An investor who expects a fall needs a way to sell first and buy later. Short selling allows that order by borrowing the asset in the meantime. The lender keeps the benefits of owning the shares. He still receives every dividend, plus a fee for the loan. The broker carries the risk that the short seller cannot pay. If the price climbs, buying the shares back costs more each day. So the broker holds the proceeds plus an extra deposit, and asks for more cash before that deposit runs out.

# Properties

#### *(Dividends)* A cash dividend costs the short seller nothing on net, because the share price falls by the amount she pays.

Two parties expect the dividend on a borrowed share. The buyer of the share now owns it, so the firm pays the dividend to the buyer. The lender also expects it, because lending did not end his ownership. The short seller pays the lender $D$ per share from her own cash. Right after the payment, the share price drops by $D$, since the firm has paid out that cash. Her short position gains that drop. The term $-ND$ in $\Pi$ therefore offsets the part of $N(S_0 - S_T)$ that comes from the dividend drop. A dividend modeled as a continuous yield $q$ is repaid in shares instead of cash. A trader who borrows $x$ shares at time $0$ returns $xe^{qT}$ shares at $T$. That is the holding the lender would have built by reinvesting the dividends. Adding the P&L of a long holder, $N(S_T - S_0) + ND$, to $\Pi$ gives $-bTNS_0$. The fee is the only amount that leaves the pair, and the lender keeps it.

#### *(Repeated Calls)* After a call restores the maintenance level, any further rise in the price brings another call.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules require a margin account for a short sale but give no maintenance rule. The maintenance margin and the trigger price follow Hull. A call that restores only the maintenance level is the convention used here.

A call at price $S$ asks for $(1 + m_M)NS - A$. After the deposit, the account cash is exactly $(1 + m_M)NS$. Applying the trigger formula to the new cash gives the next trigger price,
$$S^*_{\text{new}} = \frac{(1 + m_M)NS}{N(1 + m_M)} = S.$$
The new trigger equals the price at the call. The account sits exactly on the trigger, so each further rise of $\$1$ brings a call of $(1 + m_M)N$ dollars. A short seller who wants room deposits more than the call asks. A futures call restores the initial margin instead, which leaves a buffer before the next call. If the short seller cannot pay, the broker buys the shares back at the market price. The paper loss then becomes a realized loss.

#### *(Unlimited Loss)* The account's cash covers the shares owed only while the price stays below $(1 + m_I)S_0$.

The equity $A - NS$ reaches zero at $S = A/N = (1 + m_I)S_0$. With $m_I = 50\%$, a $50\%$ rise in the price leaves no equity. Above that price, the broker would lose its own money if the short seller failed to pay. For this reason the broker calls at $S^*$, well before the equity runs out. The gain and the loss are not symmetric. The gain is capped at $NS_0$, which is $1/m_I$ times the initial deposit. At $m_I = 50\%$ the largest gain is twice the deposit. The loss has no cap. A doubling of the price costs $NS_0$, and a second doubling costs another $2NS_0$. A long position has the reverse shape, with the loss capped at $NS_0$ and the gain unlimited.

#### *(Recall Risk)* A stock loan has no fixed end date, so the short seller cannot know how long she can stay short.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules do not discuss recalls, changes in the borrow fee, or short squeezes.

When the lender recalls the shares, the broker tries to borrow them from someone else. If no other lender exists, the short seller must buy back at that day's market price. The forced close can lock in a loss even if the price later falls as she expected. Many brokers can also raise the borrow fee while the position is open. When many short sellers must buy at once, their buying pushes the price up. The higher price brings more margin calls and more forced buying. This feedback is a **short squeeze**. Recalls and squeezes limit how long an arbitrageur can hold a short position ([[4.2.2a Arbitrage]]).

#### *(Short Sales in Arbitrage)* Short sales enforce the lower side of many no-arbitrage prices, and each cost of shorting lowers that side.

Without short sales, only the current owners of an expensive asset could sell it. The reverse cash-and-carry trade shorts the asset and buys it back through a cheap forward ([[2.2.4 Cash-and-Carry Arbitrage]]). Each cost of the short sale lowers the profit of this trade. In the no-arbitrage band for a forward price, the fee enters as a continuously compounded rate $b$. It lowers the growth of the invested proceeds to $e^{(r_l - b)T}$, where $r_l$ is the lending rate. For small $bT$ this charge is close to the simple fee $bTNS_0$ used in this note. Short-sale restrictions, or an asset that is hard to borrow, remove the trade. Many commodities are hard to borrow. For them only a long-term owner can enforce the lower bound. The owner sells from inventory and buys back through the forward.

# Derivation

We want the short seller's P&L and the trigger price.

Take $N$ shares sold short at $0$, a dividend $D$ per share paid at $t_D$, and the close at $T$. The margin account pays no interest, and no margin call occurs. The fee is paid at the close. The table lists the short seller's cash flows.

| Cash flow | $0$ | $t_D$ | $T$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sell $N$ borrowed shares | $+NS_0$ | | |
| Place proceeds and deposit in the margin account | $-(1 + m_I)NS_0$ | | $+(1 + m_I)NS_0$ |
| Pay the dividend to the lender | | $-ND$ | |
| Pay the borrow fee | | | $-bTNS_0$ |
| Buy back $N$ shares and return them | | | $-NS_T$ |
| Total | $-m_INS_0$ | $-ND$ | $(1 + m_I)NS_0 - bTNS_0 - NS_T$ |

With no interest, the P&L is the sum of the three totals. The deposit $m_INS_0$ leaves at $0$ and returns at $T$, so it cancels. The sum is $N(S_0 - S_T) - ND - bTNS_0$, which is $\Pi$ in the callout.

The account cash stays at $A = (1 + m_I)NS_0$ while the price moves. A call starts when the margin ratio reaches $m_M$. Setting the ratio equal to $m_M$ at the price $S^*$ gives
$$\frac{(1 + m_I)NS_0 - NS^*}{NS^*} = m_M \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (1 + m_I)NS_0 = (1 + m_M)NS^*.$$
The left equation states that the ratio has fallen to the maintenance level. Dividing the right equation by $N(1 + m_M)$ gives $S^* = (1 + m_I)S_0/(1 + m_M)$. The ratio falls as $S$ rises, so every price above $S^*$ brings a call.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Short Sale With a Dividend
>An investor shorts 250 shares at \$38.40 and covers four months later at \$33.10. The stock pays a dividend of \$0.35 per share while the position is open. The borrow fee is $3\%$ per year, charged as simple interest on the day-0 value. The margin account pays no interest.
>
>>[!example]- Find the short seller's P&L, and compare it with that of a holder of 250 shares over the same dates.
>>The short sale raises $250 \times 38.40 = \$9{,}600$. The dividends paid to the lender are $250 \times 0.35 = \$87.50$. The fee is $0.03 \times \frac{4}{12} \times 9{,}600 = \$96.00$. Covering costs $250 \times 33.10 = \$8{,}275$. The P&L is
>>$$\Pi = 9{,}600 - 87.50 - 96.00 - 8{,}275 = \$1{,}141.50.$$
>>The four terms are the proceeds, the dividends, the fee, and the cost of covering. The long investor has $250 \times (33.10 - 38.40 + 0.35) = -\$1{,}237.50$. The two P&Ls add up to $-\$96.00$, the fee. The lender keeps that amount.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The next two examples use the maintenance margin and the trigger price. Both come from Hull.

>[!example] (Medium) Calls on Every Rise
>An investor shorts 600 shares at \$52.00. The initial margin is $50\%$ and the maintenance margin is $25\%$. A margin call restores the margin ratio to $25\%$. The margin account pays no interest. The price later rises to \$65.00, and then to \$68.00.
>
>>[!example]- Find the trigger price, the deposit due at \$65.00, the new trigger price, and the deposit due at \$68.00.
>>The initial deposit is $0.5 \times 600 \times 52 = \$15{,}600$, so the account holds $A = \$46{,}800$. The trigger price is
>>$$S^* = \frac{1.5 \times 52}{1.25} = \$62.40.$$
>>Here $1.5 = 1 + m_I$ and $1.25 = 1 + m_M$. At \$65.00 the shares owed are worth $\$39{,}000$. The equity is $46{,}800 - 39{,}000 = \$7{,}800$, and the ratio is $0.20$. The broker needs equity of $0.25 \times 39{,}000 = \$9{,}750$, so the deposit due is \$1,950.
>>
>>The account now holds \$48,750. The new trigger is $48{,}750 / (600 \times 1.25) = \$65.00$, the price at the call. At \$68.00 the shares owed are worth \$40,800, and the ratio is $7{,}950 / 40{,}800 = 0.1949$. The deposit due is $1.25 \times 40{,}800 - 48{,}750 = \$2{,}250$. That is $\$750$ for each dollar of the rise, or $(1 + m_M)N$.

>[!example] (Hard) A Recall After a Margin Call
>An investor shorts 1,200 shares at \$25.00. The initial margin is $50\%$ and the maintenance margin is $30\%$. A margin call restores the ratio to $30\%$. The borrow fee is $4\%$ per year, simple interest on the day-0 value, paid at the close. The margin account pays no interest, and dividends are paid from a separate cash account. After one month the price is \$31.00. In month two the stock pays \$0.40 per share, and the price ends the month at \$29.00. At the end of month three the lender recalls the shares, and no other lender exists. The investor covers at \$27.50.
>
>>[!example]- Find the margin call, the P&L, and the return on the cash deposited, and compare with holding to month six.
>>The initial deposit is \$15,000, so $A = \$45{,}000$. The trigger is $1.5 \times 25 / 1.3 = \$28.85$, so \$31.00 brings a call. The shares owed are worth \$37,200, the equity is \$7,800, and the ratio is $0.2097$. The deposit due is $1.3 \times 37{,}200 - 45{,}000 = \$3{,}360$, which brings the account to \$48,360.
>>
>>After the call the equity would reach zero at $48{,}360 / 1{,}200 = \$40.30$. At \$29.00 the ratio is $(48{,}360 - 34{,}800)/34{,}800 = 0.3897$, so month two brings no call. At the close, the dividends cost $1{,}200 \times 0.40 = \$480$ and the fee is $0.04 \times 0.25 \times 30{,}000 = \$300$. The P&L is
>>$$\Pi = 1{,}200 \times (25.00 - 27.50) - 480 - 300 = -\$3{,}780.$$
>>The three terms are the price loss, the dividends, and the fee. As a check, the account returns $48{,}360 - 33{,}000 = \$15{,}360$ after covering. The investor deposited $15{,}000 + 3{,}360 = \$18{,}360$. The net is $15{,}360 - 18{,}360 - 480 - 300 = -\$3{,}780$. The return on the cash deposited is $-3{,}780 / 18{,}360 = -20.59\%$.
>>
>>Suppose instead the loan had lasted to month six, with no further calls, and the price had fallen to \$20.00. The fee would be \$600, and the P&L would be $1{,}200 \times 5 - 480 - 600 = \$4{,}920$. The recall turned a gain of \$4,920 into a loss of \$3,780, although her view on the price was right.
