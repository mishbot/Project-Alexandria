>[!note] Ring of Charge
>A **ring of charge** is a thin circular loop of radius $a$. It carries a total charge $Q$ spread evenly around it. Put the ring in the $xy$-plane, centered on the origin. At a point on its axis with coordinate $z$, negative below the ring, the field points along the axis with
>$$E_z = \frac{kQz}{(a^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}.$$
>For $Q > 0$ the field points away from the ring on both sides. It points along $+z$ above the ring and along $-z$ below it. The sideways components cancel, because the ring looks the same after any rotation about its axis.
>
>The formula has three checks:
>- At the center, $z = 0$, the field is zero. Opposite pieces of the ring cancel each other.
>- Far away, $z \gg a$, the field becomes $kQ/z^2$, the field of a point charge $Q$.
>- The field grows from zero at the center, peaks at a distance comparable to $a$, and then falls off.
>
>This formula holds only on the axis. Off the axis the cancellation fails, and no elementary integral gives the field.

# Intuition

Every piece of the ring sits at the same distance $\sqrt{a^2 + z^2}$ from a point on the axis. Each piece also points its field at the same angle to the axis. The pieces differ only in which side they push from. Going around the ring, the sideways pushes point in every direction and add to zero. The pushes along the axis are all the same, so they add up directly. That makes the ring integral one of the easiest.

The animation shows the pieces of the ring one at a time. Their sideways pushes cancel around the ring, and their pushes along the axis add. Then P moves along the axis. The graph traces $E_z$ from zero at the center, through its peak, toward the point-charge curve $kQ/z^2$.

At the center, each piece pushes a test charge toward the opposite piece, and opposite pieces cancel. Far away, the ring's size is negligible next to the distance, and the field becomes that of a point charge. In between, the field rises and then falls.

# Derivation

Cut the ring into small pieces of charge $dq$. A piece and the field point P on the axis form a right triangle with legs $a$ and $z$. The distance between them is $\sqrt{a^2 + z^2}$, so the piece's field has the size
$$dE = k\,\frac{dq}{a^2 + z^2}.$$
Symmetry cancels the components in the $xy$-plane, since the charge is spread evenly around the ring. Only the $z$-component survives. The angle $\theta$ between $d\vec E$ and the axis has $\cos\theta = z/\sqrt{a^2 + z^2}$. Shrinking the pieces and adding them,
$$E_z = \int k\,\frac{dq}{a^2 + z^2}\cdot\frac{z}{\sqrt{a^2 + z^2}} = \frac{kz}{(a^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}\int dq.$$
Here $k$, $z$, and $a$ do not change from piece to piece, so they come outside. The remaining integral adds up all the charge on the ring, $\int dq = Q$. This gives $E_z = kQz/(a^2 + z^2)^{3/2}$.

# Properties

#### *(Far Away Looks Like a Point)* For $z \gg a$ the ring's field approaches the point-charge field $kQ/z^2$.

When $z$ is much larger than $a$, the term $a^2$ in the denominator is small next to $z^2$. The denominator becomes $(z^2)^{3/2} = z^3$, and $E_z \approx kQz/z^3 = kQ/z^2$. At $z = 10a$ the exact field is $1.5\%$ below the point-charge value. Any charged object looks like a point charge from far enough away.

#### *(Strongest at $a/\sqrt 2$)* On the axis, the field is largest at $z = \pm a/\sqrt 2$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not locate the maximum. It follows from setting the derivative of $E_z$ to zero.

Differentiate $E_z = kQz(a^2 + z^2)^{-3/2}$ with respect to $z$:
$$\frac{dE_z}{dz} = kQ\,\frac{a^2 - 2z^2}{(a^2 + z^2)^{5/2}}.$$
The derivative is zero when $z^2 = a^2/2$. The peak value is $E_{\max} = 2kQ/(3\sqrt 3\,a^2) \approx 0.385\,kQ/a^2$. Inside $|z| < a/\sqrt 2$, the field grows with distance from the center. Outside, it falls off toward $kQ/z^2$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Field on the Axis
>A ring of radius $10\ \text{cm}$ carries $20\ \text{nC}$ spread evenly.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field on the axis $10\ \text{cm}$ from the center.
>>Here $a = z = 0.10\ \text{m}$, so $a^2 + z^2 = 0.020\ \text{m}^2$ and $(0.020)^{3/2} = 2.83 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3$. Then
>>$$E_z = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(20 \times 10^{-9})(0.10)}{2.83 \times 10^{-3}} = 6.4 \times 10^{3}\ \text{N/C},$$
>>pointing along the axis, away from the ring.

>[!example] (Medium) How Far Is Far?
>Use the same ring and move the point to $z = 1.0\ \text{m}$.
>
>>[!example]- Compare the exact field with the point-charge field $kQ/z^2$.
>>The exact field is
>>$$E_z = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(20 \times 10^{-9})(1.0)}{(0.01 + 1.0)^{3/2}} = \frac{179.8}{1.015} = 177\ \text{N/C}.$$
>>A point charge gives $kQ/z^2 = 179.8\ \text{N/C}$. The two differ by $1.5\%$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture set up the integral in the next example and suggested two methods. It did not finish the integral.

>[!example] (Hard) A Sphere Built From Rings
>A thin spherical shell of radius $a$ carries a constant surface charge density $\sigma$, for a total charge $Q = 4\pi a^2\sigma$. Point P lies a distance $z$ from the center.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field at P by slicing the shell into rings, for P outside ($z > a$) and inside ($z < a$).
>>Choose the $z$-axis through P. Slice the shell into rings centered on this axis. Label each ring by its polar angle $\theta$, from $0$ at the top to $\pi$ at the bottom. Translate the ring formula to one slice:
>>- The ring's radius is $a\sin\theta$.
>>- Its center sits at $a\cos\theta$ on the axis. P sits a signed distance $z - a\cos\theta$ from that center, along the axis.
>>- Its width is the arc length $a\,d\theta$, so its area is $2\pi a\sin\theta \cdot a\,d\theta$ and its charge is $dq = 2\pi\sigma a^2\sin\theta\,d\theta$.
>>
>>The ring formula then gives
>>$$dE = \frac{k\,dq\,(z - a\cos\theta)}{\left[a^2\sin^2\theta + (z - a\cos\theta)^2\right]^{3/2}} = \frac{2\pi k\sigma a^2\,(z - a\cos\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta}{(a^2 + z^2 - 2az\cos\theta)^{3/2}},$$
>>using $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$. Substitute $u = \cos\theta$, so $du = -\sin\theta\,d\theta$, and $\theta$ from $0$ to $\pi$ becomes $u$ from $1$ to $-1$:
>>$$E = 2\pi k\sigma a^2\int_{-1}^{1} \frac{(z - au)\,du}{(a^2 + z^2 - 2azu)^{3/2}}.$$
>>One route splits the numerator into two integrals and substitutes $w = a^2 + z^2 - 2azu$ in each. A shorter route uses a derivative. The integrand is minus the derivative of $(a^2 + z^2 - 2azu)^{-1/2}$ with respect to $z$. The derivative can come outside the integral:
>>$$E = -2\pi k\sigma a^2\,\frac{d}{dz}\int_{-1}^{1} \frac{du}{\sqrt{a^2 + z^2 - 2azu}} = -2\pi k\sigma a^2\,\frac{d}{dz}\left[\frac{(z + a) - |z - a|}{az}\right].$$
>>Outside, $z > a$, the bracket equals $2/z$. Its derivative is $-2/z^2$, so
>>$$E = \frac{4\pi k\sigma a^2}{z^2} = \frac{kQ}{z^2}.$$
>>Inside, $z < a$, the bracket equals $2/a$, a constant. Its derivative is zero, so $E = 0$. A uniform shell acts on outside points as if all its charge sat at the center. Inside, its field is zero everywhere. [[3.3.2a Spherical Symmetry]] gets both results from Gauss's law in two lines.
