>[!note] Mutual Independence
>Events $A_{1}, \ldots, A_{N} \subseteq \Omega$ are said to be **mutually independent** if, for every single combination $S$ of the interval $[1, N]$, such that  $S \subseteq \{ 1, 2, \ldots, N \}$ and $|S| \geq 2$, the joint probability of all events $A_{i}$ for $i \in S$ is equal to the product of their marginal probabilities. Mathematically,
>$$\mathbb{P}\Big[\bigcap_{i \in S}A_i\Big]=\prod^{}_{i \in S}\mathbb{P}[A_i].$$

# Intuition
Mutual Independence is simply an extension of the [[1.6a Independence of Two Events|pairwise independence principle]]. Consider for simplification the events $A_{1}, A_{2}, A_{3}$. 

# Examples
>[!example] Pairwise Independent but Not Mutually Independent
>Suppose you flip a fair coin twice. Let three events be:
>- A: The first flip is heads.
>- B: The second flip is heads.
>- C: Both flips are the same (HH or TT).
>
>The first and the second events are independent. So are the first and the third, since $\mathbb{P}[A \cap C]=\mathbb{P}[A]\mathbb{P}[C]=\frac{1}{4}$.
>
>However, these events are not mutually independent: if we are given that A and B occurred, the probability of C occurring is 1. These events are independent by pairs, but not mutually.
