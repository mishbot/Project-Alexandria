>[!note] Multiple Linear Regression
>**Multiple linear regression** generalizes [[1.2. Linear Regression|simple linear regression]] from one predictor to $p - 1$ predictors per observation. With observations $\{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ where each $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{p-1}$ holds the predictor values for observation $i$, the model is
>$$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \cdots + \beta_{p-1} x_{i,p-1} + \varepsilon_i,$$
>or equivalently $y_i = \mathbf{x}_i^\top \boldsymbol\beta + \varepsilon_i$ with $\mathbf{x}_i = (1, x_{i1}, \ldots, x_{i,p-1})^\top$ absorbing the intercept and $\boldsymbol\beta = (\beta_0, \beta_1, \ldots, \beta_{p-1})^\top \in \mathbb{R}^p$. Stacking all $n$ observations into matrix form,
>$$\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon,$$
>where $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ is the **design matrix** whose row $i$ is $\mathbf{x}_i^\top$, and $\boldsymbol\varepsilon \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the vector of noise terms.
>
>The OLS estimator is the closed-form minimizer of $\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2$:
>$$\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} \;=\; (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}.$$
>The inverse exists when the columns of $\mathbf{X}$ are linearly independent, which requires $p \leq n$ at minimum. The geometric interpretation is that $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ is the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{y}$ onto the column space of $\mathbf{X}$, with the residual vector $\mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}$ perpendicular to every predictor.
>
>>[!warning] Multicollinearity
>>When predictors are highly correlated, $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$ is close to singular and the OLS estimator becomes numerically unstable: small changes in $\mathbf{y}$ produce large swings in $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$, individual coefficient standard errors blow up, and individual coefficients can flip sign run-to-run while the joint fit remains stable. The standard remedies are dropping correlated predictors, pre-processing with PCA, or moving to [[1.5. Regularization|ridge regression]] which adds $\lambda \mathbf{I}$ to the diagonal of $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$ and stabilizes the inverse.

# Intuition

Multiple linear regression is the same idea as simple regression, scaled up: find the coefficient vector that makes the linear combination of predictors closest to the response in squared-error sense. The matrix formula $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$ is the multidimensional version of the slope formula from simple regression, with $(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}$ playing the role of $1/\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(x)$ and $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$ playing the role of $\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(x, y)$.

The geometric picture is even cleaner. The fitted vector $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ lies in the column space of $\mathbf{X}$ (it is a linear combination of the predictors), and the residual vector $\mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is the part of $\mathbf{y}$ that the predictors cannot explain. The OLS criterion picks the unique $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ in the column space that minimizes the residual length, which by elementary geometry is the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{y}$ onto the column space. The orthogonality condition $\mathbf{X}^\top (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) = \mathbf{0}$ is exactly the matrix equation that produces the closed-form solution.

# Properties

#### *(OLS Solution)* The closed-form estimator is $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$ when $\mathbf{X}$ has full column rank.

The derivation comes from the orthogonality of the residual vector to the column space of $\mathbf{X}$. Setting $\mathbf{X}^\top (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta) = \mathbf{0}$ gives the **normal equations** $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$, and inverting the Gram matrix $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$ produces the solution. The Gram matrix is invertible exactly when the predictor columns are linearly independent, which requires $\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X}) = p$ and consequently $n \geq p$. When $n < p$ or columns are collinear, the inverse fails to exist and the problem is ill-posed without regularization.

#### *(Hat Matrix)* The fitted values are a linear projection of the response.

Defining the **hat matrix** $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top$, the fitted values are $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{y}$, justifying the name (it puts the "hat" on $\mathbf{y}$). The hat matrix is the orthogonal projector onto the column space of $\mathbf{X}$: it is symmetric, idempotent ($\mathbf{H}^2 = \mathbf{H}$), and its trace equals $p$ (the number of fitted parameters, equal to the rank of the projection). The diagonal entries $h_{ii}$ are the **leverages** of each observation, measuring how much observation $i$'s own response influences its own fitted value, and large leverages identify points whose removal would significantly change the fit.

#### *(Gauss-Markov Theorem)* OLS is the BLUE under uncorrelated, constant-variance noise.

If $\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol\varepsilon] = \mathbf{0}$ and $\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}$, then among all linear unbiased estimators of $\boldsymbol\beta$, the OLS estimator has the smallest variance — it is the **best linear unbiased estimator** (BLUE). The result requires no distributional assumption beyond the first two moments. When the noise covariance is not scalar, the BLUE becomes the **generalized least squares** estimator $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\text{GLS}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{y}$, which weights observations by the inverse noise covariance.

#### *(Residual Variance)* $\hat\sigma^2 = \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\|^2 / (n - p)$ is the unbiased estimator of $\sigma^2$.

The denominator is the **degrees of freedom** remaining after fitting $p$ parameters. The MLE under Gaussian noise is the corresponding biased estimator that divides by $n$; dividing by $n - p$ corrects for the lost degrees of freedom and produces an unbiased estimator. This is the variance estimator that goes into standard errors and $t$-statistics.

#### *(Adjusted $R^2$)* Penalizes added predictors that do not improve fit enough.

The ordinary $R^2 = 1 - \mathrm{SSE}/\mathrm{SST}$ can only increase as predictors are added, since the optimization has more freedom. The **adjusted** version
$$R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{SSE}/(n - p)}{\mathrm{SST}/(n - 1)} = 1 - \frac{n - 1}{n - p}(1 - R^2)$$
divides each sum of squares by its degrees of freedom, so adding a predictor that does not reduce SSE proportionally enough actually decreases $R^2_{\text{adj}}$. This makes it a more honest metric for comparing models of different sizes.

#### *(Inference Under Normality)* The coefficient vector is jointly Gaussian.

Under $\boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})$, the estimator is exactly Gaussian: $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\beta, \sigma^2 (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1})$. Individual coefficients can be tested via $t_j = \hat\beta_j / \mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_j) \sim t_{n-p}$ under $H_0: \beta_j = 0$, and groups of coefficients via $F$-tests on nested models. Without Gaussianity, the same statistics are approximately Normal by the central limit theorem when $n$ is large.

#### *(Spurious Regression)* Regressing one trending variable on another can produce false significance.

When two unrelated variables both trend over time (or both have unit-root behavior), an OLS regression of one on the other often produces a high $R^2$ and small $p$-values even though the variables share no real dependence — the common trend is mistaken for a relationship. This is why blindly regressing time-series quantities is dangerous; the standard remedies are differencing the series, including a trend term explicitly, or testing for **cointegration**. See [[2.4 Time Series Regression]] for the time-series-specific treatment.

#### *(Iterative Computation by Gradient Descent)* When $n$ or $p$ is too large for the closed form, gradient descent solves the same optimization.

For very large $n$ (millions of rows) or very large $p$ (thousands of features), the $O(np^2 + p^3)$ cost of forming and inverting $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$ becomes prohibitive. Iterative methods such as gradient descent
$$\boldsymbol\beta^{(t+1)} = \boldsymbol\beta^{(t)} - \alpha\, \mathbf{X}^\top (\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta^{(t)} - \mathbf{y})$$
converge to the same OLS minimum at $O(np)$ per step and avoid the matrix inversion entirely. This is the algorithm of choice when fitting linear models on big data, and it is the same machinery (with a different loss) that drives logistic regression and most of deep learning.

# Examples

>[!example] Predicting house price from multiple features
>$y_i$ is sale price; predictors are square footage, number of bedrooms, age, and distance to downtown ($p = 5$ including intercept). Fit OLS on $n = 1000$ houses.
>>[!example]- Reading the fit
>>The coefficient vector $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ has five entries, each interpreted as the marginal effect of a one-unit change in that predictor *holding all others fixed*. So $\hat\beta_{\text{bedrooms}}$ is the predicted price increase per added bedroom, conditional on square footage, age, and distance staying constant. The standard error of each coefficient depends on $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$; if bedrooms and square footage are highly correlated, both standard errors will be large because the data cannot cleanly attribute price increases to one or the other. The adjusted $R^2$ then tells you what fraction of price variance is explained, fairly accounting for the larger model.

>[!example] Polynomial regression as a special case
>Fit $y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \beta_2 x_i^2 + \beta_3 x_i^3 + \varepsilon_i$ to a curved scatter plot.
>>[!example]- Why this is still linear regression
>>Although the *function* of $x$ is non-linear, the *parameters* $(\beta_0, \beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3)$ enter linearly. Setting up the design matrix with columns $(1, x_i, x_i^2, x_i^3)$ recovers a multiple linear regression problem with $p = 4$. The OLS solution and all the inferential machinery apply unchanged. This is the standard trick for fitting non-linear shapes with linear regression: pick a basis (polynomials, splines, sinusoids) and treat the basis evaluations as new predictors.

>[!example] Geometric derivation of the normal equations
>Show that $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ being the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{y}$ onto $\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{X})$ implies $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$.
>>[!example]- Solution
>>The orthogonal projection is the unique vector in $\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{X})$ such that $\mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is perpendicular to every column of $\mathbf{X}$, i.e., $\mathbf{X}^\top (\mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}) = \mathbf{0}$. Substituting $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ gives the **normal equations** $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$, and inverting the Gram matrix produces $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$. The geometric story is therefore not separate from the algebraic one: the orthogonality condition *is* the matrix equation that defines the OLS solution.
