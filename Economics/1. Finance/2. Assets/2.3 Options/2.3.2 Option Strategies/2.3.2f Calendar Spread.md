>[!note] Calendar Spread
>A **calendar spread** sells a European call with expiry $T_1$ and buys a European call with a later expiry $T_2 > T_1$. Both calls are on the same stock and have the same strike $K$. Let $c(T_1)$ and $c(T_2)$ be their premiums today. On a stock with no dividends the longer call costs more, so the trader pays the net premium $c(T_2) - c(T_1) > 0$. The premium from the short call pays for part of the long one. At $T_1$ the short call expires, and the long call still has time $\tau = T_2 - T_1$ to run. Let $c_\tau(S_{T_1})$ be the price of that remaining call when the stock is at $S_{T_1}$. The value of the spread at $T_1$ is
>$$V(S_{T_1}) = c_\tau(S_{T_1}) - \max(S_{T_1} - K, 0).$$
>The first term is the long call, now with time $\tau$ left, and the second is the payoff of the expiring short call. The value is not piecewise linear, because $c_\tau$ still contains time value. It peaks at $S_{T_1} = K$, where the short call pays nothing and the long call has the most time value. Ignoring interest, the profit at $T_1$ is $V - [c(T_2) - c(T_1)]$. The maximum loss is the net premium, approached when $S_{T_1}$ falls far below $K$. Far above $K$ the value tends to $K(1 - e^{-r\tau})$, so the loss there is slightly smaller. The maximum gain depends on the price of the remaining call, which depends on the volatility the market expects after $T_1$. Like a [[2.3.2e Butterfly Spread|butterfly spread]], a calendar spread bets that the stock stays near $K$, here until $T_1$. It is also a bet on the **volatility term structure** (how the volatility implied by option prices changes with expiry).

# Intuition

An option loses its time value as expiry approaches, and a short-dated option loses it faster. A trader who sells the near call and buys the far call collects this faster loss. If the stock is still near $K$ at $T_1$, the near call expires worthless. The far call still has months to run and keeps much of its value. The trade works best when the market also keeps pricing the far call at a high volatility. The name refers to the two expiry dates on the calendar. Option chains are often laid out with strikes in rows and expiries in columns. The two legs then sit in the same row, so the trade is also called a horizontal spread.

# Properties

#### *(Never Below Zero)* At $T_1$ the spread is never worth less than zero, so the net premium is the most the trader can lose.

The remaining call satisfies the lower bound of [[2.3.1b Option Price Bounds]], $c_\tau(S) \ge \max(S - Ke^{-r\tau}, 0)$. For $r \ge 0$ this is at least $\max(S - K, 0)$, the payoff of the expiring call. So $V(S_{T_1}) \ge 0$ in every state. A position that is never worth less than zero at $T_1$ cannot cost less than zero today, so $c(T_2) \ge c(T_1)$. The argument needs a stock with no dividends. A large dividend paid between $T_1$ and $T_2$ lowers the long call and can reverse the order. Above $K$ the same bound gives $V \ge K(1 - e^{-r\tau}) > 0$, the interest on $K$ over the remaining time.

#### *(Time Decay)* If the stock stays at the strike, the short call loses all its time value by $T_1$ and the long call only part.

Take the prices in the plot, with the stock still at $\$50$ at $T_1$. The three-month call falls from $\$3.29$ to zero, so the short leg gains $\$3.29$. The six-month call becomes a three-month call and falls from $\$4.82$ to $\$3.29$, so the long leg loses $\$1.53$. The profit is $3.29 - 1.53 = \$1.76$. The six-month call costs less than two three-month calls, $\$4.82$ against $\$6.58$, so time value grows more slowly than time. Of the six-month call's $\$4.82$, only $\$1.53$ is lost in the first three months and $\$3.29$ in the last three.

#### *(Volatility Exposure)* The value at $T_1$ rises with the volatility the market uses at that date to price the remaining call.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules name the volatility term structure as the calendar spread's target, but they do not define implied volatility.

The **implied volatility** of an option is the volatility that makes the Black-Scholes-Merton formula match its market price ([[2.3.5a Implied Volatility]]). The spread holds a call that is still alive at $T_1$, and that call is worth more at a higher implied volatility. With the stock at $\$50$ at $T_1$, the remaining call is worth $\$2.31$ at $20\%$, $\$3.29$ at $30\%$, and $\$4.28$ at $40\%$ implied volatility. The profit is then $\$0.78$, $\$1.76$, or $\$2.75$. The short call has expired, so only the long leg responds. A trader who thinks short-dated options are expensive relative to long-dated ones sells the first and buys the second. The trade gains if the long-dated volatility holds up while the short-dated option decays.

#### *(Diagonal Spread)* A diagonal spread changes the strike as well as the expiry, so its profit at $T_1$ favors a fall or a rise.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture defines only the calendar spread. The homework asks for the profit of a diagonal spread at $T_1$.

A **diagonal spread** sells a call with strike $K_1$ and expiry $T_1$, and buys a call with strike $K_2$ and expiry $T_2 > T_1$. Its value at $T_1$ is $c_\tau(S_{T_1}; K_2) - \max(S_{T_1} - K_1, 0)$, where $c_\tau(\cdot\,; K_2)$ is the remaining call with strike $K_2$. For large $S_{T_1}$ this tends to $K_1 - K_2e^{-r\tau}$. On a large fall both calls end nearly worthless, so the profit is close to the net premium with its sign changed. When $K_2$ is far enough above $K_1$ that $K_2e^{-r\tau} > K_1$, the limit for a large rise is negative, and the trade does worst on a large rise. When $K_2 < K_1$ the limit is positive, so the value never falls below zero. The trade then does worst on a large fall, where it loses the net premium. The plot uses the parameters above with $K_1 = 50$ and $K_2 = 55$ or $45$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Cost of a Calendar Spread
>A stock trades at $\$50$ and pays no dividends. A three-month European call with strike $\$50$ costs $\$3.29$, and a six-month call with the same strike costs $\$4.82$. A trader sells the three-month call and buys the six-month call. Three months later the stock has fallen to $\$35$, and the remaining call is quoted at $\$0.02$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the net premium, the share of the long call paid for by the short call, and the profit at the first expiry.
>>The net premium is $4.82 - 3.29 = \$1.53$. The short call pays for $3.29/4.82 = 68.26\%$ of the long call.
>>
>>At the first expiry with the stock at $\$35$, the short call expires worthless. The remaining call is worth $\$0.02$, so the spread is worth $\$0.02$. The profit is $0.02 - 1.53 = -\$1.51$. This is close to the maximum loss of $\$1.53$, which the trader would reach as the stock falls toward zero.

>[!example] (Medium) Profit at the First Expiry
>Take the spread from the previous example, bought for $\$1.53$. Three months later the short call expires. Consider three cases. In case (a) the stock is at $\$52$ and the remaining three-month call is quoted at $\$4.52$. In case (b) the stock is at $\$45$ and the call is quoted at $\$1.15$. In case (c) the stock is at $\$50$, but the market now expects smaller price swings, and the call is quoted at $\$2.31$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the profit of the spread in each case.
>>In case (a) the short call pays its holder $52 - 50 = \$2$. The spread is worth $4.52 - 2 = \$2.52$, and the profit is $2.52 - 1.53 = \$0.99$.
>>
>>In case (b) the short call expires worthless. The spread is worth $\$1.15$, and the profit is $1.15 - 1.53 = -\$0.38$. A fall of $10\%$ leaves the stock too far from the strike.
>>
>>In case (c) the short call expires worthless. The spread is worth $\$2.31$, and the profit is $\$0.78$. With the volatility unchanged, the same stock price would give $\$1.76$. The lower volatility priced into the call removes more than half of the profit.

>The next example uses a diagonal spread, which appears in the homework but not in the lecture notes.

>[!example] (Hard) A Diagonal Spread With a Higher Long Strike
>A stock trades at $\$50$ and pays no dividends. The risk-free rate is $5\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A trader sells a three-month European call with strike $\$50$ for $\$3.29$. She buys a six-month European call with strike $\$55$ for $\$2.79$. In three months the remaining $\$55$ call will be quoted as follows. It costs $\$0.05$ if the stock is at $\$40$, $\$1.42$ at $\$50$, and $\$15.86$ at $\$70$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the net premium, the profit at the first expiry for each stock price, and the largest possible loss at that date under any model.
>>The trader receives $3.29 - 2.79 = \$0.50$ today. The long call has the higher strike, so it is cheaper than the short call.
>>
>>At $S_{T_1} = 40$ the short call expires. The spread is worth $\$0.05$, and the profit is $0.05 + 0.50 = \$0.55$. At $S_{T_1} = 50$ the spread is worth $\$1.42$, and the profit is $\$1.92$. At $S_{T_1} = 70$ the short call costs $\$20$. The spread is worth $15.86 - 20 = -\$4.14$, and the profit is $-\$3.64$.
>>
>>For a bound that holds under any model, use $c_\tau(S; 55) \ge S - 55e^{-0.05 \times 0.25} = S - 54.32$. For $S_{T_1} > 50$ the spread is then worth at least $(S_{T_1} - 54.32) - (S_{T_1} - 50) = -\$4.32$. The profit is at least $-4.32 + 0.50 = -\$3.82$. For $S_{T_1} \le 50$ the spread is worth at least zero, and the profit is at least $\$0.50$. The trader can lose at most $\$3.82$ at the first expiry, whatever the volatility.
