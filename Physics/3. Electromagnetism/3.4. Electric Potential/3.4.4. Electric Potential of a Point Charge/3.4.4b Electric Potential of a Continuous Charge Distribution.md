>[!note] Electric Potential of a Continuous Charge Distribution
>The potential of charge spread over a line, surface, or volume follows from the [[3.4.4. Electric Potential of a Point Charge|point-charge potential]] by **slice and dice**. Cut the source charge into pieces $dq$ small enough to act as point charges. Each piece adds $dq/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$, where $r$ is its distance to the field point P. The total is
>$$V = \int \frac{dq}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r}.$$
>The zero is at infinity, carried over from the point-charge formula. The steps:
>- Draw the object, one typical piece $dq$, and the distance $r$ from it to P.
>- Write $dq$ in terms of one variable, such as $dq = \lambda\,dy$ or $dq = \sigma\,dA$.
>- Set the limits so that every piece of charge is counted once.
>- Integrate. No components, projection cosines, or symmetry cancellations are needed, because $V$ is a scalar.
>
>This method is the one to use when Gauss's law cannot give $\vec E$. Finding $\vec E$ by slice and dice and then integrating again would take two integrals.
>
>Standard results:
>- A **ring** has radius $a$ and charge $Q$. On its axis, at a distance $z$ from its center,
>$$V = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\sqrt{z^2 + a^2}}.$$
>- A **straight rod** has length $2L$ and charge per length $\lambda$. On its perpendicular bisector, at a distance $x$ from its midpoint,
>$$V = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{L + \sqrt{L^2 + x^2}}{x}.$$
>- For a **piece of a spherical shell** of radius $R$, take P at the sphere's center. Every bit of the charge is then a distance $R$ away. So $V = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 R)$ for a piece of any shape with charge $Q$. Let a uniform $\sigma$ cover the polar angles from $\theta_0$ to $\pi$, measured from the $+z$ axis. That part is a cap around the $-z$ axis, and
>$$V = \frac{\sigma R}{2\varepsilon_0}\,(1 + \cos\theta_0).$$
>The same cap has half-angle $\alpha = \pi - \theta_0$, measured from its own axis. In that angle the result reads $V = \frac{\sigma R}{2\varepsilon_0}(1 - \cos\alpha)$.
>
>The ring's potential is known only on its axis. Off the axis the integral leads to elliptic integrals. These have no expression in powers, roots, logs, or trigonometric functions. [[3.4.5. Electric Field from Electric Potential]] shows which field components this leaves within reach.

# Intuition

Slice and dice works the same way for the potential as for the field, with one simplification. Each piece contributes a number. There is no direction to track, so nothing cancels by symmetry and no cosine factor appears. The integrals are often harder, though. For the rod, the field integral reduces to $\int\cos u\,du$, while the potential integral reduces to $\int\sec u\,du$.

A potential found this way is known only where it was computed. The ring formula holds on the axis and nowhere else.

The ring from the Easy example is cut into 8 pieces, and each piece adds the same $dV$ at P. Then P moves along the axis, and the graph traces $V(z)$.

# Derivation

For the ring, every piece sits the same distance $\sqrt{z^2 + a^2}$ from P. The right triangle with legs $a$ and $z$ gives this distance. It comes outside the integral:
$$V = \int \frac{dq}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\sqrt{z^2 + a^2}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\sqrt{z^2 + a^2}}\int dq = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\sqrt{z^2 + a^2}}.$$

For the rod, put it on the $y$-axis from $-L$ to $L$, and put P at $(x, 0)$. A piece at height $y$ has charge $dq = \lambda\,dy$ and sits $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ from P:
$$V = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{-L}^{L}\frac{dy}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}}.$$
Substitute $y = x\tan u$, as for the [[3.2.4a Infinite Line of Charge|infinite line]]. Then $dy = x\sec^2 u\,du$ and $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = x\sec u$. The factors of $x$ cancel:
$$\int\frac{dy}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}} = \int \sec u\,du = \ln\left|\sec u + \tan u\right|.$$
Return to $y$ with the right triangle of legs $x$ and $y$, which gives $\tan u = y/x$ and $\sec u = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}/x$. Evaluating from $y = -L$ to $y = L$,
$$V = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{\sqrt{x^2 + L^2} + L}{\sqrt{x^2 + L^2} - L}.$$
Multiply the top and bottom of the fraction by $\sqrt{x^2 + L^2} + L$. The bottom becomes $x^2$, and the fraction becomes a square. The log of a square is twice the log, which gives the form in the callout.

For the cap, slice the shell into rings centered on the $z$-axis. A ring at polar angle $\theta$ has radius $R\sin\theta$. Its center sits $R\cos\theta$ from the sphere's center, along the axis. Every point of the ring is then a distance $\sqrt{R^2\sin^2\theta + R^2\cos^2\theta} = R$ from the center. The ring's width is $R\,d\theta$, so its charge is
$$dq = \sigma\,(2\pi R\sin\theta)(R\,d\theta).$$
Adding the rings from $\theta_0$ to $\pi$,
$$V = \int_{\theta_0}^{\pi}\frac{2\pi\sigma R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R} = \frac{\sigma R}{2\varepsilon_0}\Big[-\cos\theta\Big]_{\theta_0}^{\pi} = \frac{\sigma R}{2\varepsilon_0}\,(1 + \cos\theta_0).$$
At $\theta_0 = 0$ the cap is the whole sphere. Then $V = \sigma R/\varepsilon_0$, which equals $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 R)$ with $Q = 4\pi R^2\sigma$.

# Properties

#### *(Zero at Infinity From the Pieces)* The integral sets $V(\infty) = 0$ with no separate choice, because every piece already uses that zero.

The building block $dq/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$ has its zero at infinity. A sum of such terms has the same zero. Another reference point would mean adding a constant to every piece. The same fact makes the method fail for an infinite object. An infinite line carries infinite charge, and its integral diverges. For such objects the potential comes from the field, as in [[3.4.3. Electric Potential from Electric Field]].

#### *(Near and Far Limits)* Near a long rod its potential matches the infinite line's, and far away it matches a point charge's.

For $x \ll L$, $\sqrt{L^2 + x^2} \approx L$, so $V \approx \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{2L}{x}$. That is the infinite-line potential $-\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{x}{r_{\text{ref}}}$ with $r_{\text{ref}} = 2L$. As $L$ grows, $r_{\text{ref}}$ moves out toward infinity, and $V$ at a fixed $x$ grows without limit. This is why the infinite line has no zero at infinity. For $x \gg L$, the log becomes $\ln(1 + L/x) \approx L/x$. Then $V \approx 2\lambda L/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 x)$, the potential of a point charge $Q = 2\lambda L$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Potential of a Ring
>A ring of radius $5.0\ \text{cm}$ carries $8.0\ \text{nC}$ spread evenly.
>
>>[!example]- Find the potential at its center and on its axis $12\ \text{cm}$ from the center.
>>At the center, $z = 0$, and every piece is $0.050\ \text{m}$ away:
>>$$V = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(8.0 \times 10^{-9})}{0.050} = 1.44 \times 10^{3}\ \text{V}.$$
>>At $z = 0.12\ \text{m}$, each piece is $\sqrt{0.12^2 + 0.050^2} = 0.13\ \text{m}$ away, so
>>$$V = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(8.0 \times 10^{-9})}{0.13} = 553\ \text{V}.$$
>>On the axis, the potential is largest at the center. The field there is zero.

>[!example] (Easy) A Charged Hemisphere
>A thin plastic bowl is half of a sphere of radius $R = 12\ \text{cm}$. It carries $\sigma = 3.0 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{C/m}^2$ spread evenly. Point P is the center of the sphere, at the middle of the bowl's rim.
>
>>[!example]- Find the potential at P from the cap formula. Then check it with the bowl's total charge.
>>The bowl covers the polar angles from $\theta_0 = \pi/2$ to $\pi$, so $\cos\theta_0 = 0$. The cap formula gives
>>$$V = \frac{\sigma R}{2\varepsilon_0} = \frac{(3.0 \times 10^{-8})(0.12)}{2(8.85 \times 10^{-12})} = 203\ \text{V}.$$
>>For the check, the bowl's area is $2\pi R^2(1 + \cos\theta_0) = 2\pi R^2 = 0.0905\ \text{m}^2$. Its charge is $Q = \sigma \times 0.0905 = 2.71 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{C}$. Every bit of it is $R$ from P, so
>>$$V = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R} = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(2.71 \times 10^{-9})}{0.12} = 203\ \text{V}.$$

>[!example] (Medium) A Rod Seen From Its Bisector
>A rod $30\ \text{cm}$ long carries $\lambda = 4.0\ \text{nC/m}$. Point P lies on its perpendicular bisector, $20\ \text{cm}$ from its midpoint.
>
>>[!example]- Find the potential at P. Compare it with a point charge holding the rod's whole charge at the midpoint.
>>Here $L = 0.15\ \text{m}$ and $x = 0.20\ \text{m}$, so $\sqrt{L^2 + x^2} = 0.25\ \text{m}$. The rod formula gives
>>$$V = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{0.15 + 0.25}{0.20} = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{-9}}{2\pi(8.85 \times 10^{-12})}\ln 2 = (71.9)(0.693) = 49.9\ \text{V}.$$
>>The rod's charge is $Q = 2\lambda L = 1.2\ \text{nC}$. At the midpoint, it would give $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 x) = 53.9\ \text{V}$. The rod's value is $7.6\%$ lower, because most of its charge is farther than $20\ \text{cm}$ from P.

>[!example] (Medium) Beyond the End of a Rod
>This is the rod from the Medium example of [[3.2.4. Electric Field of a Continuous Charge Distribution]]. It lies on the $x$-axis from $x = 0$ to $x = L = 0.30\ \text{m}$ and carries $12\ \text{nC}$ spread evenly. Point P sits on the axis at $x = L + d$, with $d = 0.10\ \text{m}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the potential at P.
>>The rod has $\lambda = 12\ \text{nC}/0.30\ \text{m} = 40\ \text{nC/m}$. A piece at position $x$ carries $dq = \lambda\,dx$ and sits $L + d - x$ from P:
>>$$V = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_0^L \frac{dx}{L + d - x} = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Big[-\ln(L + d - x)\Big]_0^L = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{L + d}{d}.$$
>>With the numbers,
>>$$V = (8.99 \times 10^{9})(40 \times 10^{-9})\ln\frac{0.40}{0.10} = (359.6)(1.3863) = 498.5\ \text{V}.$$

>[!example] (Hard) A Charged Disk on Its Axis
>A thin disk of radius $a = 0.10\ \text{m}$ carries $\sigma = 2.0 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{C/m}^2$ spread evenly. Point P lies on its axis, $z = 0.050\ \text{m}$ from the center.
>
>>[!example]- Find the potential at P by slicing the disk into rings.
>>A ring of radius $s$ and width $ds$ has area $2\pi s\,ds$ and charge $dq = 2\pi\sigma s\,ds$. Every point of it is $\sqrt{z^2 + s^2}$ from P. The ring result gives its potential, and the rings run from $s = 0$ to $s = a$:
>>$$V = \int_0^a \frac{2\pi\sigma s\,ds}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\sqrt{z^2 + s^2}} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\Big[\sqrt{z^2 + s^2}\Big]_0^a = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(\sqrt{z^2 + a^2} - z\right).$$
>>With the numbers, $\sigma/(2\varepsilon_0) = 1130\ \text{V/m}$ and $\sqrt{0.0025 + 0.010} = 0.1118\ \text{m}$, so
>>$$V = 1130\,(0.1118 - 0.050) = 69.8\ \text{V}.$$
>>Far away, $z \gg a$, the bracket becomes $a^2/(2z)$. The potential then becomes $\sigma\pi a^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 z)$, the potential of a point charge $\sigma\pi a^2$.
