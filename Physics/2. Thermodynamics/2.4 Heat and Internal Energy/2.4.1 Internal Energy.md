>[!note] Internal Energy
>The **internal energy** $E_{\text{int}}$ of a system is the total energy of all its molecules. It includes the translational [[1.3b Kinetic Energy|kinetic energy]] of the molecules and the kinetic energy of their rotation and vibration. It also includes the [[1.3d.1 Potential Energy|potential energy]] of the forces between them. Chemical and nuclear energy stored inside the molecules are left out unless a process changes them. Some texts call this quantity **thermal energy** or write it as $U$ or $E$. These notes write $E_{\text{int}}$.
>
>Internal energy is a **state function**. Its value depends only on the present state of the system, given by quantities such as $T$, $V$, and $N$. It does not depend on how the system reached that state. The two ways to change it are to transfer energy in as [[2.4.2 Heat|heat]], the subject of the next note, or to do work on the system. Neither heat nor work is a state function; both describe transfers. The [[2.5.2 First Law of Thermodynamics|first law]] of section 2.5 is the bookkeeping rule that relates the three.
>
>For an ideal gas the molecules exert no forces on one another, so the potential energy term is zero. A monatomic ideal gas has only translational kinetic energy, and [[2.3.1 Molecular Interpretation of Temperature|kinetic theory]] gives
>$$E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{3}{2} NkT = \tfrac{3}{2} nRT \qquad (\text{monatomic ideal gas}).$$
>Diatomic molecules also rotate, and near room temperature a diatomic ideal gas has $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{5}{2} NkT$. The general form, from the [[2.3.4 Equipartition of Energy|equipartition theorem]], counts the number $d$ of accessible degrees of freedom per molecule,
>$$E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2}\, NkT = \tfrac{d}{2}\, nRT,$$
>with $d = 3$ for a monatomic gas and $d = 5$ for a diatomic gas between roughly $-100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $800\ ^\circ\text{C}$. In every case the internal energy of an ideal gas depends on $T$ alone, and not on $P$ or $V$. Changing the temperature by $\Delta T$ changes the internal energy by $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2} Nk\, \Delta T$, whatever the process.
>
>Real gases, liquids, and solids have intermolecular potential energy as well, so their internal energy depends on volume and phase in addition to temperature. Liquid water at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ has more internal energy than ice at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ by the [[2.4.4 Latent Heat|latent heat of fusion]], even though the temperature is the same.

# Intuition

Three words get confused, and the picture below separates them. Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of one molecule. Internal energy is the total over all molecules. Heat is energy in transit between two bodies at different temperatures. A cup of water at $90\ ^\circ\text{C}$ has hotter molecules than a bathtub at $40\ ^\circ\text{C}$, so its temperature is higher. The bathtub holds several hundred times more molecules, so its internal energy is far larger. If the cup is poured into the tub, heat flows from the cup's water to the tub's water, from high temperature to low. The tub had more energy to begin with, and that does not matter. Temperature decides the direction. Total energy does not.

The state-function idea is the reason internal energy is useful. Heat and work depend on the details of a process. Internal energy depends only on where the system starts and where it ends. A gas taken from state 1 to state 2 by any route gains the same $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$. The sum $Q - W$ is therefore the same for every route, even when $Q$ and $W$ separately are not. For an ideal gas the bookkeeping is simplest of all. Look up the temperatures at the two ends, and $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$ follows, whatever happened in between.

# Properties

#### *(Temperature Alone for Ideal Gases)* Compressing or expanding an ideal gas at fixed temperature leaves its internal energy unchanged.

Ideal gas molecules do not interact, so pushing them closer together stores no potential energy. The internal energy is the kinetic energy of the molecules, and kinetic theory ties that to $T$. A change at fixed temperature therefore has $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$, and section 2.5 uses this on the [[2.5.3c Isothermal Process|isothermal process]]: any heat the gas absorbs during it leaves again as work. A real gas departs from this rule slightly, because its molecules attract one another and separating them costs energy.

#### *(Counting Modes)* The factor in front of $nRT$ counts the independent ways a molecule can store energy.

A monatomic gas has three, one for each direction of motion, and each contributes $\tfrac{1}{2} NkT$. A diatomic molecule adds two rotations about axes perpendicular to its bond, for a total of five and $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{5}{2} NkT$. At high temperature the bond also vibrates, adding two more. Only the *accessible* degrees of freedom count. Vibration needs more energy per molecule than rotation, so it freezes out first as the gas cools. Rotation freezes out at still lower temperature. Translation never freezes out. The [[2.3.4 Equipartition of Energy|equipartition theorem]] states the rule, and [[2.5.4 Molar Specific Heats]] shows how measured heat capacities confirm the count.

#### *(Change from the $PV$ Product)* For an ideal gas, $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$ can be read off a $P$-$V$ diagram without knowing the temperatures.

Substituting $NkT = PV$ into $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2} NkT$ gives $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2} PV$, so between any two states
$$\Delta E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2}\left( P_2 V_2 - P_1 V_1 \right).$$
The formula uses only the coordinates of the two endpoints, which is what a $P$-$V$ diagram shows. It holds for every path between them, whatever route the gas takes on the diagram, and section 2.5 uses it on each of the four standard [[2.5.3 Thermodynamic Processes|processes]]. On a closed loop the endpoints coincide and $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$.

#### *(Only Changes Matter)* The zero of internal energy is a convention, and every calculation uses differences.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not discuss where the zero of internal energy is set. This property is standard textbook material.

The formula $\tfrac{d}{2} NkT$ sets $E_{\text{int}} = 0$ at absolute zero and counts only molecular motion. Adding the chemical energy of the bonds, or the rest energy of the atoms, would shift every value by a constant and change no $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$. The first law involves only changes, so the choice of zero never affects an answer. A problem that asks for "the internal energy" of an ideal gas means the value with this conventional zero.

#### *(Solids and Liquids)* Most of the internal energy in condensed matter is vibration of atoms about fixed positions, plus bond energy.

An atom in a solid oscillates in the potential well made by its neighbors. It has both kinetic and potential energy, with equal averages for a spring-like well. This is why most solids near room temperature have a molar heat capacity close to $3R$, twice the monatomic gas value. Melting and boiling add potential energy without changing the temperature, which is what latent heat measures.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Helium in a Balloon
>A balloon holds $1.00\ \text{mol}$ of helium at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find its internal energy, and the change when it warms to $40\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>>Helium is monatomic, so
>>$$E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{3}{2} nRT = \tfrac{3}{2}(1.00\ \text{mol})(8.314\ \text{J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}))(293.15\ \text{K}) = 3.66 \times 10^{3}\ \text{J}.$$
>>The change depends only on the temperature difference, $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{3}{2}(1.00)(8.314)(20.0) = 249\ \text{J}$. The balloon expands as it warms and does work on the air, but the work does not enter this calculation. Internal energy tracks $T$ alone.

>[!example] (Medium) Cup and Bathtub
>A cup holds $0.25\ \text{kg}$ of water at $90\ ^\circ\text{C}$. A bathtub holds $150\ \text{kg}$ of water at $40\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Water is not an ideal gas, so use the [[2.4.3 Specific Heat|specific heat]] of water, $4186\ \text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K})$, to measure internal energy above $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- Which holds more internal energy, and by what factor?
>>Above the reference point, the cup holds
>>$$E_{\text{cup}} = (0.25\ \text{kg})(4186\ \text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}))(90\ \text{K}) = 9.4 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J},$$
>>and the tub holds $(150)(4186)(40) = 2.5 \times 10^{7}\ \text{J}$, about $270$ times more. The cup has the higher temperature, so heat flows from cup to tub when they mix. The tub has the larger internal energy. The two statements do not conflict, because temperature is an average per molecule and internal energy is a total.

>[!example] (Medium) Nitrogen versus Helium
>Two flasks each hold $1.00\ \text{mol}$ of gas at $300\ \text{K}$. One holds helium, the other nitrogen. Treat nitrogen as a diatomic ideal gas with rotation but no vibration.
>
>>[!example]- Compare their internal energies and their average translational kinetic energies per molecule.
>>Helium has $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{3}{2} RT = 3.74 \times 10^{3}\ \text{J}$. Nitrogen has $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{5}{2} RT = 6.24 \times 10^{3}\ \text{J}$, larger by the factor $5/3$ because each molecule also rotates. The translational kinetic energy per molecule is $\tfrac{3}{2} kT$ for both, since both are at $300\ \text{K}$. Nitrogen stores the extra $RT$ in rotation. Its molecules do not move faster on average than the temperature demands.
