>[!note] Barrier Options
>A **barrier option** is a European call or put with strike $K$, expiry $T$ and a barrier $B$. The contract fixes the dates on which the price is observed. Let $M_T$ and $m_T$ be the highest and lowest observed prices, and $S_T$ the final price. Touching $B$ changes the contract in one of two ways.
>- A **knock-out** is cancelled when the price touches $B$.
>- A **knock-in** comes alive only when the price touches $B$.
>
>A barrier above today's price is an up barrier, and a barrier below it is a down barrier. The four single-barrier calls with strike $K$ pay
>$$C_{UO} = \max(S_T - K, 0)\,\mathbf{1}\{M_T < B\}, \qquad C_{DO} = \max(S_T - K, 0)\,\mathbf{1}\{m_T > B\},$$
>$$C_{UI} = \max(S_T - K, 0)\,\mathbf{1}\{M_T \ge B\}, \qquad C_{DI} = \max(S_T - K, 0)\,\mathbf{1}\{m_T \le B\}.$$
>The letters U and D give the side of the barrier, and O and I mean out and in. The indicator $\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}$ equals $1$ when the event occurs and $0$ otherwise. Put versions replace $\max(S_T - K, 0)$ with $\max(K - S_T, 0)$. A knock-out (KO) and the knock-in (KI) with the same terms add up to the vanilla European call. This is **in-out parity**:
>$$C_{KO} + C_{KI} = C.$$
>Here $C$ is the price of the vanilla call, and the pair is UO with UI or DO with DI. Barrier options carry one of two labels, set by the vanilla payoff at the barrier.
>- A **regular** barrier option has a zero vanilla payoff at the barrier, as for a down-and-out call with $B \le K$.
>- A **reverse** barrier option has a positive vanilla payoff at the barrier, as for an up-and-out call with $B > K$. The up-and-out call's payoff drops from $B - K$ to $0$ when the price touches $B$.
>
>Like [[2.3.6.2b Asian Options|Asian]] and [[2.3.6.2c Lookback Options|lookback options]], a barrier option is path-dependent, since its payoff uses prices observed before $T$. It builds on the vanilla options of the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]]. One-touch binaries in [[2.3.6.1b Binary Options]] are the simplest barrier contracts. [[2.3.6.3b Autocallables]] and [[2.3.6.3e Reverse Convertible Notes]] embed a down-and-in put.

# Intuition

Many exposures depend on a path. A barrier removes the payoff on some paths, so a knock-out costs less than the vanilla. The matching knock-in pays only on those paths, so it costs less as well.

# Properties

#### *(Regular and Reverse)* A reverse knock-out loses a positive payoff at the barrier, so its value and Greeks change fast near $B$.

For a regular down-and-out call, the vanilla value at $B \le K$ shrinks to zero as expiry nears. The knock-out cuts little value there, and its price falls smoothly to zero at $B$. A reverse up-and-out call with $B > K$ behaves differently. Close to expiry and slightly below $B$, it is worth almost $B - K$. At $B$ it is worth zero. The value drops over a narrow band of prices. So the delta is large and negative, and the gamma is large. The animation shows both calls with $K = 100$, $\sigma = 30\%$, $r = 4\%$ and dividend yield $q = 1\%$.

The down-and-out call ($B = 85$) stays smooth at every time to expiry $\tau$. With $\tau = 0.004$ years, the up-and-out call ($B = 125$) is worth $19.2$ near $S = 120$ and $0$ at $S = 125$. Its delta is $-4.95$ at $S = 123$. A dealer who sold it is short about five shares per option as a hedge. If the price touches $B$, the option disappears, and the dealer must buy back all of those shares at once. In-out parity passes the problem to the knock-in with the same barrier. Since $C_{UI} = C - C_{UO}$, the up-and-in call has the same steep slope with the opposite sign. Dealers protect themselves by **barrier bending**. They quote and hedge the option as if the barrier sat at a shifted level. The size of the shift depends on how competitive the market is and on the time to expiry.

>[!warning] Knock-In Labels and Notation in the Lecture
>The lecture labels the up-and-in call "regular" and the down-and-in call "reverse". It does not say where $B$ sits relative to $K$ for these two. Under the payoff rule in the callout, the label depends on that position. An up-and-in call with $B \le K$ and a down-and-in call with $B > K$ match the lecture's labels. An up-and-in call with $B > K$ is the in-out partner of the reverse up-and-out call above. So it has the same jump in value at $B$ and is equally hard to hedge. The lecture also writes survival events as indicators such as $I_{(0,B,t,T)}$, and the path extremes as $S^M$ and $S^m$.

#### *(Contract Terms)* Monitoring, rebates and the barrier type are contract terms, and each one changes the price.

Monitoring can be continuous, or discrete at fixed times such as the daily close. A knock-out checked only at the close survives an intraday touch, so it is worth more than its continuous version. A knock-out may also pay a **rebate**, a fixed cash amount paid when it is knocked out. A double barrier option has an up barrier and a down barrier. The pair (UO, DO) stays alive only while the price stays between the two barriers. The pair (UI, DI) comes alive when the price touches either one. The mixed pairs (UO, DI) and (UI, DO) combine one condition of each kind. The barrier itself comes in several types.
- A **one-touch** barrier triggers on the first touch, as in the payoffs above. The same trigger defines the one-touch binaries of [[2.3.6.1b Binary Options]].
- A **Parisian** barrier triggers only after the price stays beyond it for a set length of time.
- An **Asian** barrier compares an average over a fixed or moving window with $B$.
- An **m out of n days** barrier needs at least $m$ closes beyond $B$ within $n$ days. Convertible bond markets use it often.
- An **outside** barrier watches a second price, such as an exchange rate, while the payoff uses the first. It is common in FX markets.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The barrier shift for discrete monitoring is from Hull, Chapter 26. The lecture mentions daily monitoring but gives no correction.

Hull adjusts the continuous formulas for discrete monitoring by moving the barrier away from the price. With $\Delta t$ the time between observations, the shifted barrier is
$$B_{\text{adj}} = B\,e^{+0.5826\,\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}} \ \text{(up barrier)}, \qquad B_{\text{adj}} = B\,e^{-0.5826\,\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}} \ \text{(down barrier)}.$$
A knock-out then needs a larger move, which matches the extra survival between observations. Take a one-year down-and-out call with $S = K = 100$, $B = 90$, $\sigma = 30\%$, $r = 4\%$ and $q = 1\%$. The closed form in the next property values it at $\$8.60$ with continuous monitoring. With daily monitoring, $\Delta t = 1/252$, the shifted barrier is $89.01$ and the price is $\$9.15$. A simulation that checks the barrier at each daily close gives $\$9.16 \pm 0.03$.

#### *(Closed-Form Value)* Under constant $r$, $q$ and $\sigma$ with continuous monitoring, barrier calls and puts have closed forms in $N$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture says closed forms exist under constant parameters but does not state them. The formulas below are Hull's, Chapter 26.

Take a down barrier with $B \le K$ and a price $S > B$ at time $t$, with $\tau = T - t$. Hull defines
$$\lambda = \frac{r - q + \sigma^2/2}{\sigma^2}, \qquad y = \frac{\ln\!\big(B^2/(SK)\big)}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} + \lambda\sigma\sqrt{\tau}.$$
Here $\lambda$ and $y$ are inputs to the formula below, and $N$ is the standard normal CDF. The down-and-in call is worth
$$C_{DI} = Se^{-q\tau}\Big(\frac{B}{S}\Big)^{2\lambda}N(y) - Ke^{-r\tau}\Big(\frac{B}{S}\Big)^{2\lambda - 2}N\big(y - \sigma\sqrt{\tau}\big).$$
In-out parity then gives $C_{DO} = C - C_{DI}$, with $C$ the Black-Scholes-Merton call. The factors $(B/S)^{2\lambda}$ come from the reflection principle, which pairs each path that touches $B$ with its mirror image.

The down-and-in put with $B \le K$ is the put that a reverse convertible note sells. Its formula needs two more inputs:
$$x_1 = \frac{\ln(S/B)}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} + \lambda\sigma\sqrt{\tau}, \qquad y_1 = \frac{\ln(B/S)}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} + \lambda\sigma\sqrt{\tau}.$$
Both use the log distance from $S$ to the barrier, with the same shift as $y$. Hull's value of the down-and-in put is
$$P_{DI} = -Se^{-q\tau}N(-x_1) + Ke^{-r\tau}N\big(-x_1 + \sigma\sqrt{\tau}\big) + Se^{-q\tau}\Big(\frac{B}{S}\Big)^{2\lambda}\big[N(y) - N(y_1)\big] - Ke^{-r\tau}\Big(\frac{B}{S}\Big)^{2\lambda - 2}\big[N(y - \sigma\sqrt{\tau}) - N(y_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau})\big].$$
The first two terms value the payoff $K - S_T$ on paths that end below $B$, which must have touched $B$. The last two terms add the paths that touched $B$ and ended between $B$ and $K$. In-out parity gives $P_{DO} = P - P_{DI}$, with $P$ the Black-Scholes-Merton put. Hull lists the other cases, $B > K$, up barriers and the other puts, in the same form.

#### *(Vega, Skew and Term Structure)* A knock-out has less vega than the vanilla, and the knock-in carries the rest.

In-out parity holds for every $\sigma$, so the vegas add: $\mathcal{V}_{KO} + \mathcal{V}_{KI} = \mathcal{V}$. A higher $\sigma$ raises the vanilla's value. It also raises the chance of a knock-out, which cuts the knock-out's gain. So $\mathcal{V}_{KO} < \mathcal{V}$, and a reverse knock-out can have negative vega. In the table, both knock-ins also carry more vega per dollar of price than the vanilla. The table uses the closed form with $S = K = 100$, $\sigma = 30\%$, $r = 4\%$, $q = 1\%$ and $T = 1$. Vega is per unit of $\sigma$, so a one-point rise in volatility changes the price by $\mathcal{V}/100$.

| Call | Price | Vega $\mathcal{V}$ | $\mathcal{V}$ per \$1 of price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vanilla | $13.15$ | $38.3$ | $2.9$ |
| Down-and-out, $B = 90$ | $8.60$ | $6.2$ | $0.7$ |
| Down-and-in, $B = 90$ | $4.55$ | $32.0$ | $7.0$ |
| Up-and-out, $B = 130$ | $1.47$ | $-10.9$ | $-7.4$ |
| Up-and-in, $B = 130$ | $11.68$ | $49.2$ | $4.2$ |

The up-and-in call has more vega than the vanilla itself, because its partner's vega is negative. Volatility also enters through the shape of the [[2.3.5b Volatility Surface|volatility surface]]. A vanilla option needs one implied volatility, read at its strike and expiry. A barrier option also depends on the chance of reaching $B$ at each date before $T$. That chance depends on implied volatilities near $B$ and at every expiry up to $T$. A single flat $\sigma$ therefore misprices barrier options when the surface has a skew or a term structure. Dealers price them with the whole surface.

#### *(Static Hedging)* A fixed portfolio, closed out once at the barrier, can sometimes hedge a barrier option.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture lists approximate static hedging without an example. The exact hedge below is a standard special case and is not in the modules.

Take a down-and-out call with $B = K$ on a stock with $q = r$. Hold $e^{-q\tau}$ shares, reinvest the dividends in shares, and borrow $Ke^{-r\tau}$. This portfolio is worth $e^{-r\tau}(S - K)$, which is zero when $S = B = K$. If the price touches $B$, the option dies. At that moment the dealer sells the shares and repays the loan at no net cost. If the price never touches $B$, then $S_T > K$ and the portfolio pays $S_T - K = \max(S_T - K, 0)$. So the knock-out's value is
$$C_{DO} = e^{-r\tau}(S - K) \qquad \text{when } B = K \text{ and } q = r,$$
for every $\sigma$. With $S = 110$, $r = q = 3\%$ and one year to expiry, the option is worth $\$9.70$ at any volatility. The vanilla call is worth $\$13.87$ at $\sigma = 20\%$ and $\$17.60$ at $\sigma = 30\%$. The hedge needs no trading before the barrier and has zero vega. It fails when the price jumps through $B$. It also fails when $q \ne r$, since the portfolio is then not worth zero at $B$. In those cases dealers hold vanilla options chosen to be worth about zero along the barrier. The hedge is then approximate.

#### *(Uses)* Barrier options make hedges cheaper and turn bonds into high-coupon notes.

A hedger who thinks a touch is unlikely can buy a knock-out and save premium. The saving pays for a gap in protection. An importer who buys an up-and-out call on a foreign currency loses the hedge when that currency rises the most. Barriers also build yield products. A [[2.3.6.3e Reverse Convertible Notes|reverse convertible note]] pays a coupon above the risk-free rate. Its investor holds a high-coupon bond and has sold down-and-in puts with strike $S_0$, today's price. The premium of the puts pays for the extra coupon. The hard example below prices such a put.

# Derivation

We want $C_{UO} + C_{UI} = C$ for an up barrier. The down barrier works the same way with $m_T$ in place of $M_T$. On any path, either the price touched $B$ ($M_T \ge B$) or it did not ($M_T < B$). Exactly one of the two holds. The table lists each payoff at $T$.

| Position | No touch ($M_T < B$) | Touch ($M_T \ge B$) |
|---|---|---|
| Up-and-out call | $\max(S_T - K, 0)$ | $0$ |
| Up-and-in call | $0$ | $\max(S_T - K, 0)$ |
| Sum | $\max(S_T - K, 0)$ | $\max(S_T - K, 0)$ |

The sum pays the vanilla payoff on every path. By the [[4.2.2a Arbitrage|law of one price]], the prices agree, so $C_{UO} + C_{UI} = C$. The argument needs the same strike, expiry, barrier and monitoring dates for both contracts. It uses no model, so it holds for every $\sigma$. The Greeks of the two contracts therefore add up to the vanilla's Greeks. With a rebate $R$ on the knock-out, the sum also pays $R$ on the touched paths. The pair is then worth the vanilla plus a one-touch binary paying $R$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) An Importer's Knock-Out Hedge
>A US importer must pay GBP $400{,}000$ in six months. The exchange rate today is $1.27$ USD per GBP. A bank quotes a six-month European call on GBP with strike $1.28$ at $\$0.0285$ per pound. It quotes an up-and-out call with the same strike and a barrier of $1.40$, monitored continuously, at $\$0.0134$ per pound. Ignore interest on the premiums.
>
>>[!example]- Find the price of the matching up-and-in call, the premium saved with the knock-out, and the net dollar bill if the rate ends at $1.36$, with and without an earlier touch of $1.40$.
>>In-out parity gives the up-and-in call:
>>$$C_{UI} = C - C_{UO} = 0.0285 - 0.0134 = \$0.0151 \text{ per pound}.$$
>>On $400{,}000$ pounds the vanilla costs $\$11{,}400$ and the knock-out costs $\$5{,}360$. The importer saves $\$6{,}040$, which is $53\%$ of the vanilla premium.
>>
>>At $1.36$ the bill is $400{,}000 \times 1.36 = \$544{,}000$. If the rate never touched $1.40$, the call pays $(1.36 - 1.28) \times 400{,}000 = \$32{,}000$. The net bill is $\$512{,}000$, the cost of buying at the strike. If the rate touched $1.40$ earlier, the call is gone and the importer pays the full $\$544{,}000$. With the premium, the totals are $\$517{,}360$ and $\$549{,}360$. A vanilla call would cap the total at $\$512{,}000 + \$11{,}400 = \$523{,}400$ on every path. The saving buys a gap in protection on the paths where the pound rises the most.

>[!example] (Medium) Pricing a Rebate With In-Out Parity
>A stock trades at $\$60$. The risk-free rate is $3\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A dealer quotes three nine-month contracts. A vanilla call with strike $\$60$ costs $\$6.16$. A down-and-in call with the same strike and a barrier at $\$54$, monitored continuously, costs $\$1.61$. A one-touch binary that pays $\$1$ at expiry if the price touches $\$54$ costs $\$0.67$. A client wants a down-and-out call with strike $\$60$ and a rebate of $\$1$, paid at expiry after a knock-out.
>
>>[!example]- Find the down-and-out call without the rebate, the price with the rebate, and the share of the knock-out's saving that the rebate uses up.
>>In-out parity gives the knock-out without a rebate:
>>$$C_{DO} = C - C_{DI} = 6.16 - 1.61 = \$4.55.$$
>>It saves $\$1.61$, or $26\%$, against the vanilla call.
>>
>>Now add the rebate. Let $C_{DO}^{R}$ be the down-and-out call with the rebate. On a path that never touches $\$54$, it pays the vanilla payoff and the knock-in pays nothing. On a path that touches $\$54$, it pays $\$1$ and the knock-in pays the vanilla payoff. So the pair pays the vanilla payoff plus the one-touch payoff on every path:
>>$$C_{DO}^{R} + C_{DI} = C + 0.67.$$
>>So $C_{DO}^{R} = 6.16 + 0.67 - 1.61 = \$5.22$. The rebate costs $\$0.67$, which uses up $42\%$ of the $\$1.61$ saving. The client still saves $\$0.94$, or $15\%$. The rebate is expensive because a touch is likely. The binary's price implies a risk-neutral touch probability of $0.67e^{0.03 \times 0.75} = 0.69$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture does not price barrier puts. This example uses Hull's closed form for the down-and-in put and his discrete-monitoring shift, both from Chapter 26.

>[!example] (Hard) The Down-and-In Put Inside a Note
>A stock trades at $\$50$ and pays a dividend yield of $2\%$. Its volatility is $25\%$ per year, and the risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A dealer prices a one-year down-and-in put with strike $\$50$ and barrier $\$35$, which is $70\%$ of today's price. A reverse convertible note sells puts like this one.
>
>>[!example]- Find the value of the down-and-in put with continuous monitoring, the matching down-and-out put, and the down-and-in put with daily monitoring.
>>First the inputs. Here $\tau = 1$ and $\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = 0.25$. The exponent is
>>$$\lambda = \frac{0.04 - 0.02 + 0.03125}{0.0625} = 0.82,$$
>>so $\lambda\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = 0.205$. The three arguments are
>>$$x_1 = \frac{\ln(50/35)}{0.25} + 0.205 = 1.6317, \qquad y = \frac{\ln(35^2/50^2)}{0.25} + 0.205 = -2.6484, \qquad y_1 = \frac{\ln(35/50)}{0.25} + 0.205 = -1.2217.$$
>>The two powers are $(35/50)^{1.64} = 0.55714$ and $(35/50)^{-0.36} = 1.13701$. The discounted prices are $50e^{-0.02} = 49.0099$ and $50e^{-0.04} = 48.0395$.
>>
>>Next come the four terms of the formula:
>>$$-49.0099\,N(-1.6317) = -49.0099 \times 0.05137 = -2.518,$$
>>$$48.0395\,N(-1.3817) = 48.0395 \times 0.08353 = 4.013,$$
>>$$49.0099 \times 0.55714\,\big[N(-2.6484) - N(-1.2217)\big] = 27.3052 \times (0.00404 - 0.11091) = -2.918,$$
>>$$-48.0395 \times 1.13701\,\big[N(-2.8984) - N(-1.4717)\big] = -54.6214 \times (0.00188 - 0.07055) = 3.751.$$
>>They sum to $P_{DI} = 2.328$, or $\$2.33$. The vanilla put is worth $\$4.36$, so in-out parity gives $P_{DO} = 4.36 - 2.33 = \$2.03$. The knock-in put keeps $53\%$ of the vanilla put's value, though the stock must fall $30\%$ to activate it. Deep losses carry most of the vanilla put's value. A path that ends far below $\$50$ often touches $\$35$ on the way.
>>
>>With daily monitoring, $\Delta t = 1/252$. Hull's shift moves the down barrier to $35e^{-0.5826 \times 0.25/\sqrt{252}} = 34.68$. The same formula with $B = 34.68$ gives $P_{DI} = \$2.223$. With the vanilla put at $\$4.357$, in-out parity gives $P_{DO} = 4.357 - 2.223 = 2.134$, or $\$2.13$. A path must now close below the barrier to knock in. Fewer paths knock in, so the knock-in put is worth less.
