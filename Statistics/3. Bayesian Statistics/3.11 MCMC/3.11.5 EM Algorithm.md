>[!note] EM Algorithm
>The **Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm** is a deterministic iterative procedure for computing maximum-likelihood (MLE) or maximum-a-posteriori (MAP) estimates in models with **latent variables** $z$, whose marginal likelihood $p(y \mid \theta) = \int p(y, z \mid \theta)\,dz$ is intractable but whose **complete-data likelihood** $p(y, z \mid \theta)$ has a tractable form. EM alternates two steps:
>
>1. **E-step (Expectation).** Given the current parameter estimate $\theta^{(t)}$, compute the **responsibilities** — the posterior of the latent variables under the current parameters:
>$$q^{(t)}(z) := p(z \mid y, \theta^{(t)}).$$
>Then form the **Q-function** (expected complete-data log-likelihood):
>$$Q(\theta \mid \theta^{(t)}) := \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q^{(t)}}\big[\log p(y, z \mid \theta)\big].$$
>2. **M-step (Maximization).** Update the parameters by maximizing $Q$:
>$$\theta^{(t+1)} := \arg\max_\theta\ Q(\theta \mid \theta^{(t)}).$$
>
>EM guarantees **monotonic improvement** of the observed-data log-likelihood: $\log p(y \mid \theta^{(t+1)}) \ge \log p(y \mid \theta^{(t)})$ at every step. Under mild regularity conditions the iterates converge to a stationary point (local max, saddle, or local min), so multiple restarts are standard practice. EM is **not** a Bayesian posterior sampler — it produces a point estimate, not samples — but it sits adjacent to Bayesian methods both historically and algorithmically.
>
>**Relation to [[3.11.2 Gibbs Sampling|Gibbs sampling]].** EM is the *deterministic analog* of Gibbs for the same latent-variable augmentation. Gibbs draws $z^{(t)} \sim p(z \mid y, \theta^{(t)})$ and $\theta^{(t+1)} \sim p(\theta \mid y, z^{(t)})$ and produces samples from the joint posterior; EM replaces the Gibbs draw of $z$ with its expectation (the E-step) and replaces the Gibbs draw of $\theta$ with an argmax (the M-step), producing the MAP estimate instead of posterior samples. The same latent-variable augmentation and the same full conditionals power both algorithms — they differ only in "sample" vs. "replace with conditional expectation / argmax."
>
>**Canonical application: Gaussian mixture models.** For $y_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \sum_{j=1}^k w_j \mathcal{N}(\mu_j, \sigma_j^2)$, the latent $z_i$ is the component assignment. The E-step computes responsibility $r_{ij}^{(t)} = \mathbb{P}[z_i = j \mid y_i, \theta^{(t)}]$; the M-step updates $w_j, \mu_j, \sigma_j^2$ to their responsibility-weighted MLEs. The algorithm reduces to the **soft-assignment** version of Lloyd's $k$-means.

# Intuition

For many real models, the marginal likelihood $p(y \mid \theta) = \sum_z p(y, z \mid \theta)$ is a sum over a combinatorially large latent space, and maximizing it directly by gradient ascent is numerically unstable or impossible. The EM trick is to introduce the latent $z$ explicitly and alternate between (E) inferring $z$ given current $\theta$, and (M) re-estimating $\theta$ given current inferred $z$. Each step is an easy sub-problem: the E-step is a posterior calculation on $z$ with $\theta$ held fixed, and the M-step is a weighted MLE calculation on $\theta$ with $z$ summarized by its responsibilities. Repeating drives the observed-data log-likelihood uphill.

The central tension is between the tractable *complete* data log-likelihood $\log p(y, z \mid \theta)$ (which EM uses through its expectation) and the intractable *marginal* log-likelihood $\log p(y \mid \theta) = \log \sum_z p(y, z \mid \theta)$ (what we actually want to maximize). EM exploits a variational identity (Jensen's inequality applied to the marginal) to turn the intractable log-sum into a tractable sum-of-logs that can be optimized in closed form at each M-step. The cost is that EM finds only a local optimum — the landscape of $\log p(y \mid \theta)$ typically has many modes in latent-variable models — so multiple restarts from different initializations are the practical norm.

# Properties

#### *(Monotonic Ascent)* Observed-data log-likelihood never decreases across an iteration.

The EM update satisfies $\log p(y \mid \theta^{(t+1)}) \ge \log p(y \mid \theta^{(t)})$ for every $t$, with equality only at stationary points. This is proved via the evidence lower bound (ELBO): for any distribution $q(z)$, $\log p(y \mid \theta) \ge \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(y, z \mid \theta)] - \mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)] = Q(\theta \mid q) + H(q)$. Setting $q = p(z \mid y, \theta^{(t)})$ makes the bound tight at $\theta^{(t)}$; maximizing $Q$ over $\theta$ (the M-step) can only increase the bound and hence cannot decrease $\log p(y \mid \theta)$. See [Derivation](#Derivation).

#### *(E-step as Posterior Computation)* Responsibilities are the posterior $p(z \mid y, \theta^{(t)})$.

For discrete $z$, the responsibility $r_{ij}^{(t)} := \mathbb{P}[z_i = j \mid y_i, \theta^{(t)}]$ is computed by Bayes' rule using the current parameters: $r_{ij}^{(t)} \propto w_j^{(t)} p(y_i \mid z_i = j, \theta^{(t)})$, normalized over $j$. For continuous $z$ (linear factor models, Kalman smoothers) the E-step is a conditional Gaussian computation. In every case, the E-step is a posterior inference problem on the *latent* variables with parameters frozen; it is the step where the latent structure of the model is exploited.

#### *(M-step as Weighted MLE)* $\arg\max_\theta Q(\theta \mid \theta^{(t)})$ is usually a weighted complete-data MLE.

When $\log p(y, z \mid \theta)$ is a sum over data points with separable $\theta$-terms, $Q(\theta \mid \theta^{(t)})$ decomposes into the same sum, now weighted by responsibilities. For a Gaussian mixture,
$$Q(\theta \mid \theta^{(t)}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^k r_{ij}^{(t)}\big[\log w_j + \log \mathcal{N}(y_i; \mu_j, \sigma_j^2)\big],$$
and maximizing over $(w, \mu, \sigma)$ gives the responsibility-weighted sample estimators:
$$w_j^{(t+1)} = \frac{\sum_i r_{ij}^{(t)}}{n},\quad \mu_j^{(t+1)} = \frac{\sum_i r_{ij}^{(t)} y_i}{\sum_i r_{ij}^{(t)}},\quad (\sigma_j^2)^{(t+1)} = \frac{\sum_i r_{ij}^{(t)} (y_i - \mu_j^{(t+1)})^2}{\sum_i r_{ij}^{(t)}}.$$
These are the MLE formulas with "soft counts" $r_{ij}^{(t)}$ replacing hard $\{0, 1\}$ indicators.

#### *(Relation to Gibbs Sampling)* EM replaces Gibbs draws with expectations and argmaxes.

For the same latent-variable augmentation $(y, z, \theta)$, [[3.11.2 Gibbs Sampling|Gibbs]] alternates $z^{(t)} \sim p(z \mid y, \theta^{(t)})$ and $\theta^{(t+1)} \sim p(\theta \mid y, z^{(t)})$ — producing samples from the joint posterior. EM replaces $z^{(t)}$ with the full responsibility distribution $q^{(t)}(z) = p(z \mid y, \theta^{(t)})$ (E-step) and replaces $\theta^{(t+1)}$ with its argmax $\arg\max_\theta Q(\theta \mid \theta^{(t)})$ (M-step). Gibbs gives the full posterior distribution but is slower; EM gives the MAP point estimate much faster but loses uncertainty quantification. The two algorithms sit at opposite ends of a compute-vs-uncertainty tradeoff on the same model.

Explicit comparison for Gaussian mixtures: Gibbs draws $z_i^{(t)} \sim \text{Categorical}(r_{i1}^{(t)}, \ldots, r_{ik}^{(t)})$ (hard stochastic assignment at iteration $t$), then updates $w, \mu, \sigma$ by drawing from their conjugate posteriors given $z^{(t)}$. EM uses the responsibilities $r_{ij}^{(t)}$ as soft probabilistic assignments and sets $w, \mu, \sigma$ to their MLEs given the weighted soft assignments. As the prior becomes uninformative (Dirichlet $a_j \to 0$, Gaussian $s^2 \to \infty$, Inverse-Gamma $\alpha, \beta \to 0$), the Gibbs update for $w, \mu, \sigma^2$ shrinks toward its MLE, and EM emerges as the $\sigma \to 0$ limit of the Gibbs update.

#### *(MAP Extension)* EM computes the MAP instead of the MLE by adding a prior to the M-step.

Replace the M-step objective $Q(\theta \mid \theta^{(t)})$ with the posterior-aware quantity $Q(\theta \mid \theta^{(t)}) + \log p(\theta)$. The monotonic-improvement property generalizes immediately: EM now climbs $\log p(\theta \mid y)$ rather than $\log p(y \mid \theta)$. Conjugate priors turn the M-step into a closed-form posterior-mode computation — for Gaussian mixtures with Dirichlet / Normal / Inverse-Gamma priors, the MAP M-step is
$$w_j^{(t+1)} = \frac{a_j - 1 + \sum_i r_{ij}^{(t)}}{\sum_{j'}(a_{j'} - 1) + n},\quad \mu_j^{(t+1)} = \frac{m/s^2 + \sum_i r_{ij}^{(t)} y_i / (\sigma_j^2)^{(t)}}{1/s^2 + \sum_i r_{ij}^{(t)}/(\sigma_j^2)^{(t)}},$$
which is recognizably the posterior mode (mean) of the conjugate Gibbs update rather than a pure sample.

#### *(Convergence to Stationary Points, Not Necessarily Global)* EM can get trapped in local maxima or saddles.

The monotonic-ascent guarantee is qualitative: the sequence $\log p(y \mid \theta^{(t)})$ is nondecreasing and bounded above, so it converges. Under regularity (Wu 1983), $\theta^{(t)}$ converges to a stationary point of $\log p(y \mid \theta)$, but *which* stationary point depends on the initialization. In mixture models, label-switching and empty clusters are classical local-optimum pathologies. Running EM from multiple random restarts and keeping the best result is the standard workaround; in critical applications, combining EM with simulated annealing or running a short Gibbs warmup before EM is also common.

#### *(Convergence Speed)* Linear near the optimum, with rate determined by the fraction of missing information.

Near a local optimum $\theta^*$, EM converges linearly: $\|\theta^{(t+1)} - \theta^*\| \approx \rho \|\theta^{(t)} - \theta^*\|$ with $\rho \in [0, 1)$. The rate $\rho$ is the largest eigenvalue of the **information ratio** $I_{\text{miss}}/I_{\text{comp}}$, where $I_{\text{comp}}$ and $I_{\text{miss}}$ are the complete-data and missing-data Fisher informations at $\theta^*$. When $z$ carries little information beyond $y$ (responsibilities near $0$ or $1$), EM converges fast; when $z$ is highly informative about $\theta$ and the responsibilities are near $1/k$ for all components (highly ambiguous cluster membership), EM converges slowly. Direct gradient-based or Newton-based maximization of $\log p(y \mid \theta)$ can be faster near the optimum but requires evaluating the observed-data log-likelihood, which is the reason EM exists in the first place.

#### *(Generalizations)* ECM, GEM, and stochastic EM handle intractable M-steps and scale to large data.

When the M-step does not have a closed form, the **Generalized EM (GEM)** algorithm only requires $Q(\theta^{(t+1)} \mid \theta^{(t)}) \ge Q(\theta^{(t)} \mid \theta^{(t)})$ (a partial ascent) rather than the exact argmax — monotonic ascent of $\log p(y \mid \theta)$ still holds. **Expectation Conditional Maximization (ECM)** replaces the joint M-step with coordinate-wise maximizations. **Stochastic EM** replaces the full responsibility computation at each iteration with a Monte Carlo estimate (useful for large $n$ or intractable posteriors), trading monotonic ascent for scalability. Each variant preserves some version of the ELBO inequality.

# Examples

>[!example]- Gaussian mixture EM vs Gibbs on the same model
>**Model.** $y_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \sum_{j=1}^k w_j \mathcal{N}(\mu_j, \sigma_j^2)$ with priors $w \sim \text{Dirichlet}(a)$, $\mu_j \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(m, s^2)$, $\sigma_j^2 \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{IG}(\alpha, \beta)$.
>
>**Gibbs sampler** (from [[3.11.2 Gibbs Sampling]]):
>1. Draw $z_i^{(t)} \sim \text{Categorical}(r_{i1}^{(t)}, \ldots, r_{ik}^{(t)})$ with $r_{ij}^{(t)} \propto w_j^{(t)} \phi(y_i; \mu_j^{(t)}, (\sigma_j^2)^{(t)})$.
>2. Let $n_j^{(t)} = \sum_i \mathbf{1}\{z_i^{(t)} = j\}$, the *integer* component count.
>3. Draw $w^{(t+1)} \sim \text{Dirichlet}(a_1 + n_1^{(t)}, \ldots, a_k + n_k^{(t)})$.
>4. Draw $\mu_j^{(t+1)} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\frac{m/s^2 + \sum_{i : z_i^{(t)} = j} y_i/(\sigma_j^2)^{(t)}}{1/s^2 + n_j^{(t)}/(\sigma_j^2)^{(t)}},\ \frac{1}{1/s^2 + n_j^{(t)}/(\sigma_j^2)^{(t)}}\right)$.
>5. Draw $(\sigma_j^2)^{(t+1)} \sim \text{IG}(\alpha + n_j^{(t)}/2,\ \beta + \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i : z_i^{(t)} = j}(y_i - \mu_j^{(t+1)})^2)$.
>
>**EM algorithm** (MLE variant — take flat priors):
>1. **E-step.** Compute responsibilities $r_{ij}^{(t)} = w_j^{(t)} \phi(y_i; \mu_j^{(t)}, (\sigma_j^2)^{(t)}) / \sum_{l} w_l^{(t)} \phi(y_i; \mu_l^{(t)}, (\sigma_l^2)^{(t)})$.
>2. Compute effective (*soft*) counts $n_j^{(t)} = \sum_i r_{ij}^{(t)}$ (now a real number, not an integer).
>3. **M-step — weights.** $w_j^{(t+1)} = n_j^{(t)} / n$.
>4. **M-step — means.** $\mu_j^{(t+1)} = \sum_i r_{ij}^{(t)} y_i / n_j^{(t)}$ (responsibility-weighted sample mean).
>5. **M-step — variances.** $(\sigma_j^2)^{(t+1)} = \sum_i r_{ij}^{(t)} (y_i - \mu_j^{(t+1)})^2 / n_j^{(t)}$ (responsibility-weighted sample variance).
>
>**What changes.** The E-step replaces the stochastic Categorical draw of $z_i^{(t)}$ with the deterministic expectation $r_{ij}^{(t)} = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_i = j\} \mid y_i, \theta^{(t)}]$; the effective count $n_j^{(t)} = \sum_i r_{ij}^{(t)}$ replaces the integer count; the Dirichlet / Normal / Inverse-Gamma draws of $w, \mu, \sigma$ are replaced with their MLEs (posterior modes with uninformative priors). The algorithmic skeleton is identical; only the randomness is removed.

>[!example]- Missing-data regression (hidden labels)
>Suppose we want to fit a linear regression $y_i = x_i^\top \beta + \varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ but some observations are missing their response $y_i$. Introduce latent $z_i = y_i$ for missing entries; the complete-data log-likelihood is
>$$\log p(y, z \mid \beta, \sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (\tilde y_i - x_i^\top\beta)^2,$$
>where $\tilde y_i = y_i$ if observed, $z_i$ if not.
>
>**E-step.** Given $(\beta^{(t)}, (\sigma^2)^{(t)})$, the conditional distribution of missing $z_i$ given $y_i$ observed and parameters is $\mathcal{N}(x_i^\top \beta^{(t)}, (\sigma^2)^{(t)})$. The Q-function replaces $\tilde y_i^2$ with $(x_i^\top \beta^{(t)})^2 + (\sigma^2)^{(t)}$ for missing $i$.
>
>**M-step.** Maximize $Q$ in $\beta, \sigma^2$: this is ordinary least squares on the imputed data set where missing $y_i$ is replaced by $x_i^\top \beta^{(t)}$, with a variance correction. Iterating until convergence gives the MLE of $\beta, \sigma^2$ under missing-at-random. This is the classical **data augmentation** example that gave EM its name.

>[!example]- EM for the Bernoulli mixture (coin-flipping)
>Two biased coins $A, B$ with success probabilities $p_A, p_B$. We observe $n$ trials, each consisting of $m$ flips; the coin identity $z_i \in \{A, B\}$ is hidden. Let $y_i \in \{0, 1, \ldots, m\}$ be the number of heads in trial $i$.
>
>**E-step.** $\mathbb{P}(z_i = A \mid y_i, p_A, p_B) = \frac{\binom{m}{y_i} p_A^{y_i}(1-p_A)^{m-y_i}}{\binom{m}{y_i} p_A^{y_i}(1-p_A)^{m-y_i} + \binom{m}{y_i} p_B^{y_i}(1-p_B)^{m-y_i}} = \frac{p_A^{y_i}(1-p_A)^{m-y_i}}{p_A^{y_i}(1-p_A)^{m-y_i} + p_B^{y_i}(1-p_B)^{m-y_i}}$.
>
>**M-step.** With responsibilities $r_i := \mathbb{P}(z_i = A \mid y_i, p_A^{(t)}, p_B^{(t)})$,
>$$p_A^{(t+1)} = \frac{\sum_i r_i y_i}{m \sum_i r_i},\qquad p_B^{(t+1)} = \frac{\sum_i (1 - r_i) y_i}{m \sum_i (1 - r_i)}.$$
>Iterate until the responsibilities stabilize. The MLE of $(p_A, p_B)$ is not identifiable up to swapping labels $A \leftrightarrow B$ — the two modes of the likelihood are reflections — so the particular solution EM returns depends on initialization.

# Derivation

**Goal.** Prove the EM monotonic ascent property $\log p(y \mid \theta^{(t+1)}) \ge \log p(y \mid \theta^{(t)})$, and show how the E-step and M-step together guarantee it.

**Step 1 — ELBO decomposition.** For any parameter $\theta$ and any distribution $q(z)$ over the latent variable $z$,
$$\log p(y \mid \theta) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(y, z \mid \theta)] - \mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)]}_{\text{ELBO}(\theta, q)} + \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}(q \parallel p(z \mid y, \theta))}_{\ge 0}.$$
This identity follows from writing $\log p(y \mid \theta) = \log p(y, z \mid \theta) - \log p(z \mid y, \theta)$, taking expectations under $q$, and rearranging. Since $\mathrm{KL} \ge 0$,
$$\log p(y \mid \theta) \ge \mathrm{ELBO}(\theta, q)$$
for every $q$, with equality when $q(z) = p(z \mid y, \theta)$.

**Step 2 — E-step makes the bound tight.** At iteration $t$, set $q^{(t)}(z) := p(z \mid y, \theta^{(t)})$. Then $\mathrm{KL}(q^{(t)} \parallel p(z \mid y, \theta^{(t)})) = 0$, so
$$\log p(y \mid \theta^{(t)}) = \mathrm{ELBO}(\theta^{(t)}, q^{(t)}) = Q(\theta^{(t)} \mid \theta^{(t)}) + H(q^{(t)}),$$
where $H(q) = -\mathbb{E}_q \log q$ is the entropy of $q$. The Q-function $Q(\theta \mid \theta^{(t)}) := \mathbb{E}_{q^{(t)}}[\log p(y, z \mid \theta)]$ is the part of ELBO that depends on $\theta$; the entropy $H(q^{(t)})$ does not.

**Step 3 — M-step increases ELBO at $\theta$.** With $q = q^{(t)}$ held fixed, maximize the ELBO over $\theta$:
$$\theta^{(t+1)} = \arg\max_\theta\ \mathrm{ELBO}(\theta, q^{(t)}) = \arg\max_\theta\ Q(\theta \mid \theta^{(t)}),$$
since the entropy $H(q^{(t)})$ does not depend on $\theta$. By construction,
$$\mathrm{ELBO}(\theta^{(t+1)}, q^{(t)}) \ge \mathrm{ELBO}(\theta^{(t)}, q^{(t)}) = \log p(y \mid \theta^{(t)}).$$

**Step 4 — Conclude monotonic ascent.** Apply the ELBO inequality at the new parameter $\theta^{(t+1)}$ and any distribution $q$:
$$\log p(y \mid \theta^{(t+1)}) \ge \mathrm{ELBO}(\theta^{(t+1)}, q^{(t)}) \ge \log p(y \mid \theta^{(t)}).$$
The first inequality is ELBO $\le$ log-likelihood for any $q$; the second is the M-step improvement. Combined,
$$\log p(y \mid \theta^{(t+1)}) \ge \log p(y \mid \theta^{(t)}),$$
which is the promised monotonic ascent. The sequence $\log p(y \mid \theta^{(t)})$ is nondecreasing and bounded above (by the global maximum of the log-likelihood), so it converges. Under mild regularity conditions (continuity of $Q$ in $\theta$), $\theta^{(t)}$ itself converges to a stationary point of $\log p(y \mid \theta)$ — local max, saddle, or local min — but not necessarily the global max.

**Why this works algorithmically.** The E-step replaces an intractable $\log \int p(y, z \mid \theta)\,dz$ with a tractable expectation $\mathbb{E}_q \log p(y, z \mid \theta)$, using the best possible $q$ (the posterior under $\theta^{(t)}$) so that nothing is thrown away. The M-step then optimizes the tractable surrogate. Because the ELBO touches $\log p(y \mid \theta)$ at $\theta^{(t)}$ and lies beneath it everywhere, improving the ELBO is guaranteed to improve $\log p(y \mid \theta)$.
