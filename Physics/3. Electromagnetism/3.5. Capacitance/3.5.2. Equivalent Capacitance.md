>[!note] Equivalent Capacitance
>The **equivalent capacitance** of a group of capacitors is the capacitance of one capacitor that could replace the group. On the same supply voltage $V$, the replacement must draw the same charge from the supply. Two arrangements have simple rules.
>- **Parallel.** Two capacitors are in parallel when their ends are wired together in pairs. One end of each shares a wire, and their other ends share a second wire. Both conditions must hold. The wires give both capacitors the same potential difference, and their charges add:
>$$C_{\text{eq}} = C_1 + C_2.$$
>- **Series.** Two capacitors are in series when they are joined end to end. Nothing else may connect to the point between them. Both carry the same charge $Q$. The pair holds the charge $Q$ of one member, because the middle conductor's charge never reaches the supply. Their potential differences add, which gives
>$$\frac{1}{C_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2}, \qquad C_{\text{eq}} = \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2}.$$
>
>Both rules extend to more capacitors. Capacitances add in parallel, and reciprocals add in series. A parallel combination is larger than its largest member. A series combination is smaller than its smallest member. Many networks are neither series nor parallel, and these two rules cannot reduce them.
>
>To find the charge and potential difference of every capacitor in a network:
>- **Zip.** Find one pair in series or in parallel and replace it by its equivalent. Redraw the circuit and label the new capacitor. Repeat until one capacitor $C_{\text{eq}}$ remains across the supply.
>- Find the charge the supply delivers, $Q = C_{\text{eq}} V$.
>- **Unzip.** Go back through the drawings in reverse order. A series pair gives each member the pair's charge. A parallel pair gives each member the pair's potential difference. Then $Q = CV$ on each capacitor gives the other quantity.

# Intuition

Suppliers sell capacitors in a fixed list of values. Making every value would cost too much, and most of them would never sell. A value missing from the list is therefore built from stock parts. Parallel connections give a larger capacitance, and series connections give a smaller one.

The [[3.5.1a Parallel-Plate Capacitor|parallel-plate formula]] $C = \varepsilon_0 A/d$ shows why. With equal gaps, two plate capacitors in parallel act like one with the areas added. With equal areas, two in series act like one with the gaps added. A wider gap means a smaller capacitance.

Most errors come from misjudging which pairs are in series or in parallel.

The Easy example's two capacitors are wired in series and then in parallel. The bars compare their potential differences and charges. In series the charges match and the voltages add, and in parallel the voltages match and the charges add.

# Derivation

For a parallel pair, connect $C_1$ and $C_2$ across a supply $V$. The two upper ends share a wire, so they sit at one potential $V_A$. The two lower ends share another wire at $V_B$. Each capacitor has the potential difference $V = V_A - V_B$. Their charges are $Q_1 = C_1V$ and $Q_2 = C_2V$. The supply delivers the total
$$Q = Q_1 + Q_2 = (C_1 + C_2)V.$$
One capacitor $C_{\text{eq}}$ on the same supply holds $C_{\text{eq}}V$. Setting the two charges equal gives $C_{\text{eq}} = C_1 + C_2$.

For a series pair, connect $C_1$ and $C_2$ end to end across the supply, through an open switch. All four plates start neutral. The lower plate of $C_1$, the upper plate of $C_2$, and the wire between them form one isolated conductor. By [[3.1.2. Conservation of Charge|conservation of charge]], its net charge stays zero. Close the switch, and the supply puts $+Q$ on the upper plate of $C_1$. The facing plate of $C_1$ takes $-Q$. The isolated conductor then has $+Q$ left on its other end, the upper plate of $C_2$. The lower plate of $C_2$ takes $-Q$. Both capacitors carry the same $Q$.

Only the charge $Q$ passes through the supply. The charges on the middle conductor stay inside the pair and never reach the rest of the circuit. The equivalent capacitor must therefore hold $Q$. A count of $2Q$ would include charge that never leaves the pair.

Call the point between the two capacitors $P$. Subtracting and adding $V_P$ splits the supply's potential difference into two parts:
$$V = V_A - V_B = (V_A - V_P) + (V_P - V_B) = \frac{Q}{C_1} + \frac{Q}{C_2}.$$
One capacitor holding $Q$ at $V$ has $V = Q/C_{\text{eq}}$. Dividing by $Q$ gives $1/C_{\text{eq}} = 1/C_1 + 1/C_2$.

# Properties

#### *(No Third Connection)* Series needs a middle point with no other wires, and parallel needs shared wires at both ends.

The series argument needs the middle conductor to be isolated. A third wire lets charge flow onto or off it, and the two capacitors can then carry different charges. Two capacitors whose upper ends share a wire are in parallel only if their lower ends also share a wire. Two capacitors can sit side by side in a drawing and still fail this test. The wires at both ends decide it.

#### *(Remember to Invert)* The series rule gives $1/C_{\text{eq}}$, and the last step must invert it.

For $4.0\ \mu\text{F}$ and $12\ \mu\text{F}$ in series, $1/C_{\text{eq}} = 1/4.0 + 1/12 = 1/3.0$ in units of $\mu\text{F}^{-1}$. The equivalent capacitance is $3.0\ \mu\text{F}$. Stopping at $0.33$ gives a number with the wrong units. The size test catches the error, since a series result must be smaller than both members. For two capacitors, the product-over-sum form $C_1C_2/(C_1 + C_2)$ avoids the inversion.

#### *(Small Capacitor, Large Voltage)* In series, each capacitor's potential difference is inversely proportional to its capacitance.

Both members carry the same $Q$, so $V_1 = Q/C_1$ and $V_2 = Q/C_2$. Their ratio is $V_1/V_2 = C_2/C_1$. A small capacitor in series with a large one takes almost all of the supply voltage. This matters when each capacitor can only take a limited voltage, as in [[3.5.4a Dielectric Breakdown]].

#### *(Redraw Every Step)* The unzip step needs to know whether each merge was series or parallel.

Undoing a series merge gives each member the pair's charge. Undoing a parallel merge gives each member the pair's potential difference. Using the wrong rule gives wrong charges everywhere below that step. Each redrawn circuit records which rule applies. Working back from memory often goes wrong under time pressure.

>>
>>Two checks confirm the result. Along the path through $C_5$, $C_1$, and $C_2$, the potential differences add to $8.0 + 1.33 + 2.67 = 12\ \text{V}$. The plates joined at $P$ carry $-24$, $+16$, and $+8.0\ \mu\text{C}$, which add to zero.

>[!example] (Hard) Reconnecting Two Charged Capacitors
>Capacitor 1, with $C_1 = 2.0\ \mu\text{F}$, is charged to $12\ \text{V}$. Capacitor 2, with $C_2 = 4.0\ \mu\text{F}$, is charged to $3.0\ \text{V}$. Both are removed from their supplies. Then each positive plate is wired to the negative plate of the other capacitor.
>
>>[!example]- Find the final potential difference and the charge on each capacitor.
>>At the start, $Q_1 = C_1V_1 = 24\ \mu\text{C}$ and $Q_2 = C_2V_2 = 12\ \mu\text{C}$. One wire joins the $+24\ \mu\text{C}$ plate of capacitor 1 to the $-12\ \mu\text{C}$ plate of capacitor 2. Those two plates and the wire form one isolated conductor. By [[3.1.2. Conservation of Charge|conservation of charge]], its total stays $+12\ \mu\text{C}$. The other wire joins plates with $-24\ \mu\text{C}$ and $+12\ \mu\text{C}$, a total of $-12\ \mu\text{C}$.
>>
>>Each wire joins one end of each capacitor, so the two capacitors are now in parallel. They share one potential difference $V'$ and act as $6.0\ \mu\text{F}$. The wire with $+12\ \mu\text{C}$ is the positive side, so
>>$$V' = \frac{12\ \mu\text{C}}{6.0\ \mu\text{F}} = 2.0\ \text{V}.$$
>>The charges are $Q_1' = (2.0\ \mu\text{F})(2.0\ \text{V}) = 4.0\ \mu\text{C}$ and $Q_2' = (4.0\ \mu\text{F})(2.0\ \text{V}) = 8.0\ \mu\text{C}$. Capacitor 1 keeps its original polarity. Capacitor 2 has reversed. Its formerly negative plate now holds $+8.0\ \mu\text{C}$.
