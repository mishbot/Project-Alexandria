>[!note] Corner Solutions
>The tangency method of [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]] finds bundles on the budget line where $\text{MRS} = -p_1/p_2$. The method can fail in three ways, and each has a fix.
>
>A **corner solution** is an optimum with $x_1^* = 0$ or $x_2^* = 0$. It appears when the tangency formula returns a negative quantity. The fix is to evaluate utility at the corners $(M/p_1, 0)$ and $(0, M/p_2)$ and at every valid interior candidate, and keep the best. At an optimal corner with $x_2^* = 0$, good $1$ gives at least as much utility per dollar as good $2$:
>$$\frac{u'_{x_1}}{p_1} \ge \frac{u'_{x_2}}{p_2} \qquad \text{at } \left(\frac{M}{p_1}, 0\right).$$
>For $u = x_1(x_2 + a)$ with $a > 0$ the tangency gives $x_2 = (M - a p_2)/(2p_2)$, which is negative when income is low. The demand is
>$$x_2^* = \max\left\{0,\ \frac{M - a p_2}{2p_2}\right\}, \qquad x_1^* = \frac{M - p_2 x_2^*}{p_1},$$
>so the consumer buys no good $2$ whenever $M \le a p_2$.
>
>With **non-convex preferences** the tangency point can be a minimum along the budget line. The bordered Hessian then has $|H| < 0$. If this is the only tangency point on the budget line, the optimum is at a corner. CES utility $(\alpha x_1^{\rho} + \beta x_2^{\rho})^{1/\rho}$ with $\rho > 1$ is the standard case. Its tangency point, given by the usual CES formula, is always a minimum, and the consumer buys only one good unless the two corners tie.
>
>**Multiple optima** occur when several bundles tie. With [[6.4b Perfect Substitutes|perfect substitutes]] $u = \alpha x_1 + \beta x_2$ and $p_1/p_2 = \alpha/\beta$, every bundle on the budget line is optimal. With non-convex preferences both corners can tie. Demand is then a set of bundles.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The per-dollar inequality at a corner is an extension. The lecture checked corners by computing utility at $x_1^* = 0$ and at $x_2^* = 0$ and comparing.

# Intuition

The tangency condition comes from moving money between the two goods until neither move helps. That argument assumes the consumer can move money in both directions. At a corner the consumer would like to move more money from good $2$ to good $1$, but no money is left on good $2$. The equality between the MRS and the price ratio then becomes an inequality.

The diagram shows the corner case. At $C$ the consumer spends all income on good $1$. The indifference curve through $C$ is steeper than the budget line. Even with no good $2$, the consumer values good $1$ more than the market does. Every other point on the budget line, such as $B$, lies on a lower curve. The tangency formula for this utility places the tangency point below the $x_1$ axis, where $x_2 < 0$.

# Properties

#### *(Exam Procedure)* List every candidate, compute its utility, and keep the largest.

A safe procedure has five steps. First solve the tangency condition with the budget line. Second, drop any solution with a negative quantity. Third, compute $|H|$ at each remaining tangency point and drop points with $|H| < 0$, which are minima. Fourth, compute utility at both corners $(M/p_1, 0)$ and $(0, M/p_2)$ and at the surviving tangency points. Fifth, keep the largest value, and report all bundles that tie for it. Two families skip the first step. For [[6.4c Perfect Complements|perfect complements]] the MRS is undefined at the optimum, and the problem is solved from $\alpha x_1 = \beta x_2$ on the budget line. Perfect substitutes have a constant MRS, so the consumer compares $\alpha/p_1$ with $\beta/p_2$ and spends everything on the good with the larger ratio.

#### *(What Makes a Corner)* In $x_1(x_2 + a)$ the constant $a$ works like $a$ units of good $2$ that the consumer already owns.

For $u = x_1(x_2 + a)$ the marginal utilities are $u'_{x_1} = x_2 + a$ and $u'_{x_2} = x_1$. At the corner $(M/p_1, 0)$ good $1$ gives $a/p_1$ per dollar and good $2$ gives $M/(p_1p_2)$ per dollar. The corner is optimal when $a/p_1 \ge M/(p_1p_2)$, which is $M \le a p_2$. With no purchases of good $2$, the marginal utility of good $1$ is still $a$. A poor consumer spends everything on good $1$. Good $2$ enters the basket only after income passes $a p_2$, and from then on $x_2^*$ rises with income. The [[6.3.3 Income Changes|Engel curve]] of good $2$ is flat at zero and then slopes upward. In this region the tangency formula gives $x_1 = (M + ap_2)/(2p_1)$, which exceeds the affordable maximum $M/p_1$.

#### *(Tangency at a Minimum)* When indifference curves bend away from the origin, the tangency point is the worst bundle on the budget line nearby.

The diagram uses $u = (x_1^2 + x_2^2)^{1/2}$, the CES case $\alpha = \beta = 1$, $\rho = 2$, with the prices and income of the Hard example below. Its indifference curves are quarter circles around the origin. The curve $\bar u_A$ touches the budget line at $A$ from inside the budget set. Moving along the line away from $A$ in either direction crosses higher curves, so $A$ minimizes utility on the line. The best bundle is the corner $C$, which lies on the higher curve $\bar u_C$. The other corner $D = (0, M/p_2)$ beats $A$ but not $C$. At $\rho = 1$ the curves are straight lines, the boundary between convex ($\rho < 1$) and non-convex ($\rho > 1$) preferences. The bordered Hessian gives the same answer without a picture. For $\rho > 1$ the CES second derivatives have the signs $u''_{x_1x_1} > 0$, $u''_{x_2x_2} > 0$, $u''_{x_1x_2} < 0$, so every term of $|H|$ is negative.

#### *(Jumps in Demand)* When several bundles tie, a small price change can move the consumer from one corner to the other.

With perfect substitutes the consumer buys only good $1$ when $p_1/p_2 < \alpha/\beta$ and only good $2$ when $p_1/p_2 > \alpha/\beta$. At $p_1/p_2 = \alpha/\beta$ the indifference curves are parallel to the budget line, and every split of income is optimal. The demand for good $1$ therefore drops from $M/p_1$ to $0$ as $p_1$ crosses $\alpha p_2/\beta$. For $u = x_1^2 + x_2^2$ the two corners tie at $p_1 = p_2$. As the prices cross, the consumer jumps from one corner to the other and never buys a mix. In both cases demand jumps at the switching price. The derivative-based tools of [[6.3.1 Comparative Statics]] do not apply there.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Two Bottle Sizes
>A student buys oat milk in $2$-liter bottles ($x_1$) and $1$-liter bottles ($x_2$). Only the total amount of milk matters, so $u = 2x_1 + x_2$ in liters. The budget is $M = \$14$ and a $1$-liter bottle costs $p_2 = \$2$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the optimal bundle when the $2$-liter bottle costs $p_1 = \$3.50$, and again when it costs $p_1 = \$4$.
>>Compare utility per dollar, $\alpha/p_1$ with $\beta/p_2$. At $p_1 = 3.50$ the large bottle gives $2/3.5 \approx 0.57$ liters per dollar and the small bottle gives $1/2 = 0.5$. The student spends everything on large bottles, $x_1^* = 14/3.5 = 4$ and $x_2^* = 0$, for $8$ liters. This is a corner solution. The tangency condition never holds, because the MRS is $-2$ everywhere and $-p_1/p_2 = -1.75$.
>>
>>At $p_1 = 4$ both bottles give $0.5$ liters per dollar, since $p_1/p_2 = 2 = \alpha/\beta$. Every bundle on the budget line $4x_1 + 2x_2 = 14$ gives $7$ liters. The bundles $(3.5, 0)$, $(0, 7)$, and $(2, 3)$ are all optimal.

>[!example] (Medium) A Household With a Garden
>A household grows $4$ crates of vegetables a month in its garden. It buys $x_2$ more crates at the market for $p_2 = \$30$ each and $x_1$ baskets of other groceries for $p_1 = \$20$ each. Its utility is $u = x_1(x_2 + 4)$ and its food budget is $M = \$100$ a month.
>
>>[!example]- Find the optimal bundle. Then find the income at which the household starts to buy vegetables, and its bundle at $M = \$180$.
>>The FOCs are $x_2 + 4 = 20\lambda$ and $x_1 = 30\lambda$. Dividing gives $20x_1 = 30x_2 + 120$. With $20x_1 + 30x_2 = 100$ this gives $60x_2 = -20$, so the tangency point has $x_2 = -1/3$. That point is not feasible.
>>
>>Check the corners. At $(0, 10/3)$ utility is $0$. At $(5, 0)$ utility is $5 \cdot 4 = 20$. The corner $(5, 0)$ is optimal. At that corner groceries give $u'_{x_1}/p_1 = 4/20 = 0.20$ per dollar and market vegetables give $u'_{x_2}/p_2 = 5/30 \approx 0.17$. The household would move money toward groceries if it could, which confirms the corner.
>>
>>The general tangency solution is $x_2 = (M - 4 \cdot 30)/(2 \cdot 30)$, which is positive only when $M > 120$. At $M = 180$ it gives $x_2^* = 60/60 = 1$ crate and $x_1^* = (180 - 30)/20 = 7.5$ baskets, with utility $7.5 \cdot 5 = 37.5$. The corner $(9, 0)$ would give only $36$, so the interior point is optimal. There $|H| = 2p_1p_2 > 0$, since $u''_{x_1x_1} = u''_{x_2x_2} = 0$ and $u''_{x_1x_2} = 1$.

>[!example] (Hard) Focusing on One Game
>A player splits $M = \$12$ between gems in two mobile games. Gems in game $1$ cost $p_1 = \$2$ and gems in game $2$ cost $p_2 = \$3$. Progress feels better when it is concentrated in one game, and the player's utility is $u = (x_1^2 + x_2^2)^{1/2}$.
>
>>[!example]- The CES demand formula gives a bundle. Is it optimal? If not, find the best bundle.
>>This is CES with $\alpha = \beta = 1$ and $\rho = 2$, so $\frac{1}{\rho-1} = 1$ and $\frac{\rho}{\rho-1} = 2$. The formula gives
>>$$x_1 = \frac{12}{2\left[1 + (3/2)^2\right]} = \frac{24}{13} \approx 1.85, \qquad x_2 = \frac{12}{3\left[1 + (2/3)^2\right]} = \frac{36}{13} \approx 2.77 .$$
>>The MRS there is $-x_1/x_2 = -2/3 = -p_1/p_2$, so it is a tangency point. The second derivatives are $u''_{x_1x_1} = x_2^2/u^3 \approx 0.208$, $u''_{x_2x_2} = x_1^2/u^3 \approx 0.092$, and $u''_{x_1x_2} = -x_1x_2/u^3 \approx -0.139$. So
>>$$|H| = -4(0.092) + 12(-0.139) - 9(0.208) \approx -3.91 < 0,$$
>>and the tangency point is a minimum on the budget line, with $u \approx 3.33$.
>>
>>The corners give $u(6, 0) = 6$ and $u(0, 4) = 4$. The player buys only gems in game $1$. Game $1$ wins because its gems are cheaper and both games have the same weight. With $p_1 = p_2$ the two corners would tie, an example of multiple optima.
