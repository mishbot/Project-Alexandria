>[!note] Electric Power
>Any device with two terminals, $a$ and $b$, exchanges energy with the charge that passes through it. Let a current $I$ enter at $a$ and leave at $b$, and let $V_{ab} = V_a - V_b$. The device takes in energy at the rate
>$$P = IV_{ab}.$$
>Here $P$ is the **electric power**, $I$ is the current, and $V_{ab}$ is the potential difference across the device. A positive $P$ means the charge loses potential energy and the device takes it in. The rule holds for any device, such as a [[2.1f.1 Resistor|resistor]], a motor, a laptop, or an electric car. The unit is the watt, $1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s} = 1\ \text{V}\cdot\text{A}$.
>
>For a resistor, $V_{ab} = IR$. Substituting gives two more forms:
>$$P = I^2R = \frac{V_{ab}^2}{R}.$$
>Here $R$ is the resistance. A resistor turns all of the energy it takes in into thermal energy, so it heats up.

# Intuition

Power means the rate at which energy moves, as in [[1.3e Power]]. Electric power is the rate at which charge passing through a device gives energy to it, or takes energy from it.

Inside a resistor, the field speeds up each carrier between collisions. Each collision passes some of the carrier's kinetic energy to an atom. The atoms vibrate more, so the resistor gets hotter. The carriers' average speed stays constant, so all the work the field does on them ends up as thermal energy. Friction does the same to the work done on a sliding block. The total energy depends on how long the current flows. The rate depends only on the device and the circuit, so power is the more useful number.

A charge $dq$ enters a device at $a$ while an equal charge leaves at $b$. The drop in its potential energy gives $P = IV_{ab}$. Then the bars compare the two lamps of the Medium example.

# Derivation

Treat the device as a closed box with two leads, $a$ and $b$. Its contents do not matter. In a short time $dt$, a charge $dq = I\,dt$ enters at $a$, and an equal charge leaves at $b$. The net effect is to move $dq$ from potential $V_a$ to potential $V_b$. By [[3.4.2. Electric Potential]], its potential energy changes by $dq\,(V_b - V_a) = -dq\,V_{ab}$. The device gains the energy that the charge loses:
$$dW = dq\,V_{ab}.$$
Here $dW$ is the energy the device takes in during $dt$. Dividing by $dt$ gives the rate:
$$P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \frac{dq}{dt}\,V_{ab} = IV_{ab}.$$
Here $dq/dt$ is the current $I$. Only the charge that enters and leaves, and the potentials of the two leads, appear in this result. So $P = IV_{ab}$ holds for every device. For a resistor, substituting $V_{ab} = IR$ gives $I^2R$. Substituting $I = V_{ab}/R$ instead gives $V_{ab}^2/R$.

# Properties

#### *(Any Device)* The rule $P = IV$ applies to every two-terminal device, so a device's rated power and voltage fix its current.

The label on an electrical device lists its voltage and its power or largest current. Any two of $P$, $I$, and $V$ give the third. A $1200\ \text{W}$ hair dryer on a $120\ \text{V}$ outlet draws $1200/120 = 10\ \text{A}$. A laptop charger rated $65\ \text{W}$ at $20\ \text{V}$ delivers $3.25\ \text{A}$. Neither result needs any knowledge of what is inside the device.

#### *(Which Resistor Takes More Power)* At fixed current the larger resistance takes more power, and at fixed potential difference the smaller one does.

Use $P = I^2R$ when the current is the same, and $P = V^2/R$ when the potential difference is the same. Both forms are correct, because a different quantity is held fixed in each. Two resistors that carry the same current take power in proportion to their resistances. Two resistors with the same potential difference take power in inverse proportion to their resistances. [[3.7.2. Equivalent Resistance]] shows the circuits in which each case occurs. The Medium example compares both cases for two lamps.

#### *(Resistors Only Take Energy)* A resistor always takes in energy, whichever way the current flows.

In $P = I^2R$, the square makes $P$ positive for either sign of $I$. A resistor never returns energy to a circuit. A capacitor can store energy and give it back later, as in [[3.5.3. Energy Stored in an Electric Field]]. For a capacitor, the useful number is the total energy it can store. For a resistor, the useful number is the rate $P$, since the energy it takes keeps growing while the current flows.

#### *(Fuses)* A fuse limits the current by melting when the current is too large.

A fuse is a short wire of a metal with a low melting point. The current heats it at the rate $I^2R$. When the heating is faster than the heat loss to the surroundings, its temperature keeps rising. The heating also raises the wire's resistance, which raises $I^2R$ further. At the melting point the wire melts through, and the current stops. In a classroom demonstration, a current of roughly $30\ \text{A}$ melted a fuse wire within moments and left drops of melted metal behind. A melted fuse leaves a gap that opens the circuit. The fuse must then be replaced.

#### *(The Kilowatt-Hour)* Electric companies sell energy in kilowatt-hours, with $1\ \text{kWh} = 3.6 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J}$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not use the kilowatt-hour. Giancoli section 25.5 introduces it.

A kilowatt-hour is the energy a $1\ \text{kW}$ device uses in one hour. That is $(1000\ \text{J/s})(3600\ \text{s}) = 3.6 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J}$. It is a unit of energy, not of power. A $1500\ \text{W}$ heater that runs for $4.0$ hours uses $6.0\ \text{kWh}$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) An Electric Kettle
>A kettle is rated $1500\ \text{W}$ at $120\ \text{V}$. It runs for $3.0$ minutes.
>
>>[!example]- Find the current, the resistance of the heating element while it runs, and the energy it delivers.
>>From $P = IV$, the current is $I = P/V = 1500/120 = 12.5\ \text{A}$. From $P = V^2/R$, the resistance is
>>$$R = \frac{V^2}{P} = \frac{(120)^2}{1500} = 9.6\ \Omega.$$
>>Here $V$ is the outlet's potential difference and $P$ is the rated power. The energy is the power times the time, $(1500\ \text{W})(180\ \text{s}) = 2.7 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$. This resistance holds at the element's working temperature. The cold element has a slightly smaller resistance.

>[!example] (Medium) Two Lamps
>A $60\ \text{W}$ lamp and a $100\ \text{W}$ lamp are both rated for $120\ \text{V}$. Treat each lamp's resistance as constant.
>
>>[!example]- Find each resistance. Which lamp takes more power when each has $120\ \text{V}$ across it, and which when both carry $0.30\ \text{A}$?
>>From $R = V^2/P$ at the rated values,
>>$$R_{60} = \frac{(120)^2}{60} = 240\ \Omega, \qquad R_{100} = \frac{(120)^2}{100} = 144\ \Omega.$$
>>Here each subscript names the lamp's rated power. With $120\ \text{V}$ across each, the lamps take their rated powers. The $100\ \text{W}$ lamp, with the smaller resistance, takes more.
>>
>>With $0.30\ \text{A}$ through each, use $P = I^2R$. The $60\ \text{W}$ lamp takes $(0.30)^2(240) = 21.6\ \text{W}$. The $100\ \text{W}$ lamp takes $(0.30)^2(144) = 13.0\ \text{W}$. Now the lamp with the larger resistance takes more power. A real filament runs cooler at these lower powers, so its resistance would be lower than the rated value.

>[!example] (Hard) Melting a Fuse Wire
>A fuse is a tin wire $4.0\ \text{cm}$ long with diameter $0.50\ \text{mm}$. Tin has $\rho = 1.1 \times 10^{-7}\ \Omega\cdot\text{m}$, a mass density of $7300\ \text{kg/m}^3$, and a specific heat of $230\ \text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K})$. It melts at $232^\circ\text{C}$ with latent heat $L_F = 59\ \text{kJ/kg}$. The wire starts at $20^\circ\text{C}$ and suddenly carries $30\ \text{A}$. Assume no heat escapes and the resistance stays constant.
>
>>[!example]- Find the power, the time for the wire to reach its melting point, and the time for it to melt completely. How do these times depend on the wire's length and diameter?
>>The area is $A = \pi(0.25 \times 10^{-3})^2 = 1.96 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^2$. The resistance is $R = \rho L/A = (1.1 \times 10^{-7})(0.040)/(1.96 \times 10^{-7}) = 0.0224\ \Omega$. The power is
>>$$P = I^2R = (30)^2(0.0224) = 20.2\ \text{W}.$$
>>Here $I$ is the current and $R$ the wire's resistance. The mass is the mass density times the volume $AL$, which gives $m = (7300)(1.96 \times 10^{-7})(0.040) = 5.73 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{kg}$. Heating it by $\Delta T = 212\ \text{K}$ takes $mc\,\Delta T = 2.80\ \text{J}$, as in [[2.4.3 Specific Heat]]. Melting it takes $mL_F = 3.38\ \text{J}$, as in [[2.4.4 Latent Heat]]. The wire reaches its melting point after $2.80/20.2 = 0.139\ \text{s}$. It has melted completely after $(2.80 + 3.38)/20.2 = 0.306\ \text{s}$.
>>
>>For the dependence, let $D$ be the mass density, since $\rho$ already names the resistivity. The time is the heat needed divided by the power:
>>$$t = \frac{DAL\,(c\,\Delta T + L_F)}{I^2\rho L/A} = \frac{DA^2\,(c\,\Delta T + L_F)}{\rho I^2}.$$
>>Here $DAL$ is the mass and $I^2\rho L/A$ is the power. The length cancels, so a longer fuse melts in the same time. The time grows as $A^2$, the fourth power of the diameter. A fuse of twice the diameter takes sixteen times as long. In a real fuse, heat loss lengthens the time, and the rising resistance shortens it.
