>[!note] Normal-Normal Conjugacy (Known Variance)
>Let the unknown parameter be a mean $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$ and the observations be i.i.d. Gaussian with *known* variance $\sigma^2$. The **Normal-Normal conjugate pair** is
>$$\mu \sim N(\mu_0, \tau_0^2), \qquad y_i \mid \mu \;\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\; N(\mu, \sigma^2), \quad i = 1, \dots, n,$$
>which produces the closed-form posterior
>$$\mu \mid y \sim N(\mu_n, \tau_n^2), \qquad \frac{1}{\tau_n^2} = \frac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}, \qquad \mu_n = \tau_n^2\!\left(\frac{\mu_0}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n\bar y}{\sigma^2}\right).$$
>Here $\bar y = n^{-1}\sum_i y_i$ is the sample mean. The update rule is clean in the **precision** parametrization $\kappa = 1/\tau^2$: *posterior precision equals prior precision plus data precision*, and the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average
>$$\mu_n = \frac{\kappa_0\, \mu_0 + (n/\sigma^2)\, \bar y}{\kappa_0 + n/\sigma^2}.$$
>The prior's "effective sample size" is $n_0 = \sigma^2/\tau_0^2$: the prior carries as much information as $n_0$ pseudo-observations of value $\mu_0$.
>
>**Posterior summaries:**
>- **Mean, median, mode all equal $\mu_n$** by symmetry of the Gaussian.
>- **Posterior variance** $\tau_n^2 = 1/(\kappa_0 + n/\sigma^2)$ shrinks like $O(1/n)$ as data accumulate.
>- **Posterior predictive** for a new observation $\tilde y$: $\tilde y \mid y \sim N(\mu_n,\ \tau_n^2 + \sigma^2)$. The predictive variance decomposes into posterior uncertainty about $\mu$ plus irreducible observation noise.
>
>**Noninformative limit.** Taking $\tau_0^2 \to \infty$ (precision $\to 0$) recovers the flat prior on $\mu$ and produces $\mu \mid y \sim N(\bar y,\ \sigma^2/n)$, numerically identical to the frequentist sampling distribution of $\bar y$. The posterior mean equals the MLE and the posterior variance equals the squared standard error.

>[!note] Normal-Inverse-Gamma Conjugacy (Unknown Mean and Variance)
>When both the mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ are unknown, the conjugate prior is the **Normal-Inverse-Gamma** family, written $(\mu, \sigma^2) \sim \text{NIG}(\mu_0, \kappa_0, \alpha_0, \beta_0)$:
>$$\sigma^2 \sim \text{InvGamma}(\alpha_0, \beta_0), \qquad \mu \mid \sigma^2 \sim N\!\left(\mu_0,\ \sigma^2 / \kappa_0\right).$$
>The parameter $\kappa_0$ is the prior effective sample size for $\mu$, and $(\alpha_0, \beta_0)$ parametrize the prior for $\sigma^2$ with prior mean $\beta_0/(\alpha_0 - 1)$ for $\alpha_0 > 1$. Given $n$ observations with sample mean $\bar y$ and sample sum of squares $S = \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2$, the posterior is $\text{NIG}(\mu_n, \kappa_n, \alpha_n, \beta_n)$ with
>$$\kappa_n = \kappa_0 + n, \qquad \mu_n = \frac{\kappa_0 \mu_0 + n \bar y}{\kappa_0 + n}, \qquad \alpha_n = \alpha_0 + \frac{n}{2},$$
>$$\beta_n = \beta_0 + \frac{1}{2}S + \frac{\kappa_0 n (\bar y - \mu_0)^2}{2(\kappa_0 + n)}.$$
>The three contributions to $\beta_n$ are the prior scale, the within-sample dispersion, and a "disagreement penalty" for how far the data mean sits from the prior mean.
>
>**Marginal posterior for $\mu$** (integrating out $\sigma^2$): Student-$t$ with $2\alpha_n$ degrees of freedom,
>$$\mu \mid y \;\sim\; t_{2\alpha_n}\!\left(\mu_n,\ \frac{\beta_n}{\alpha_n \kappa_n}\right),$$
>which collapses to the Gaussian of the known-$\sigma^2$ case as $\alpha_n \to \infty$. This Student-$t$ posterior is why $t$-intervals are the default for credible intervals on a Gaussian mean when the variance is not known in advance; see [[3.2 Credible Intervals]].

# Intuition

Gaussian densities are the unique family (up to scaling) whose logarithm is a quadratic in the unknown. Multiplying two Gaussians in $\mu$ therefore produces another Gaussian, because the sum of two quadratics is again a quadratic. The prior $N(\mu_0, \tau_0^2)$ contributes the quadratic $-\tfrac{1}{2\tau_0^2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2$ and the likelihood contributes $-\tfrac{n}{2\sigma^2}(\mu - \bar y)^2$; completing the square in $\mu$ yields the posterior Gaussian with precision equal to the sum of the prior and likelihood precisions.

The central mechanism is **precision addition**. Precision measures the sharpness of information: a prior that asserts $\mu$ is known to within standard deviation $\tau_0$ has precision $1/\tau_0^2$, and $n$ observations with noise standard deviation $\sigma$ jointly carry precision $n/\sigma^2$. These add, and the posterior mean is the weighted average with weights equal to precisions. This is the Bayesian analog of how a Kalman filter fuses information from two sensors, and it generalizes directly to multivariate Gaussians (where precision is the inverse covariance matrix).

When the variance is unknown, the Student-$t$ structure emerges because the posterior for $\sigma^2$ is heavy-tailed (Inverse-Gamma), and averaging a Gaussian with variance $\sigma^2$ over an Inverse-Gamma for $\sigma^2$ produces a Student-$t$ for $\mu$. The degrees-of-freedom parameter tracks how much data has been seen: with $n$ observations and a weakly informative prior, the posterior for $\mu$ is approximately $t_{n-1}$, which recovers the frequentist $t$-distribution of the sample mean.

# Properties

#### *(Precision Addition)* Posterior precision equals prior precision plus data precision; posterior means are precision-weighted averages.

Writing $\kappa = 1/\tau^2$ for precision, the Normal-Normal update is
$$\kappa_n = \kappa_0 + n\kappa_{\text{data}}, \qquad \mu_n = \frac{\kappa_0 \mu_0 + n\kappa_{\text{data}}\bar y}{\kappa_n},$$
where $\kappa_{\text{data}} = 1/\sigma^2$. This makes the prior interpretable as $n_0 = \kappa_0/\kappa_{\text{data}} = \sigma^2/\tau_0^2$ pseudo-observations of value $\mu_0$. The multivariate extension replaces scalar precisions with precision matrices $\Lambda = \Sigma^{-1}$, and the same additive law holds: $\Lambda_n = \Lambda_0 + n\Sigma^{-1}$, $\mu_n = \Lambda_n^{-1}(\Lambda_0 \mu_0 + n\Sigma^{-1}\bar y)$.

#### *(Shrinkage to the Prior Mean)* The posterior mean is between the prior mean and the sample mean, biased toward whichever carries more precision.

Rewriting $\mu_n = w\, \mu_0 + (1-w)\, \bar y$ with $w = \kappa_0 / \kappa_n \in (0, 1)$, the posterior mean is a convex combination. When $n$ is small relative to the prior's effective sample size $n_0$, the posterior sits near $\mu_0$; when $n \gg n_0$, it sits near $\bar y$. This pattern is the univariate case of the shrinkage behaviour that drives [[3.6 Hierarchical Models|hierarchical models]] and James-Stein estimation.

#### *(Posterior Predictive is Gaussian with Inflated Variance)* New observations are predicted with variance $\tau_n^2 + \sigma^2$, never just $\sigma^2$.

For the known-$\sigma^2$ model, $\tilde y \mid y \sim N(\mu_n, \tau_n^2 + \sigma^2)$. The first term $\tau_n^2$ captures remaining uncertainty about $\mu$; the second term $\sigma^2$ is irreducible observation noise. A Bayesian predictive interval is strictly wider than the plug-in interval using $\hat\mu$, and this is precisely what protects a Bayesian forecast from the overconfidence of plug-in prediction. Under the unknown-$\sigma^2$ model, the predictive for $\tilde y$ is Student-$t$ rather than Gaussian, with location $\mu_n$ and scale $\sqrt{\beta_n(\kappa_n + 1)/(\alpha_n \kappa_n)}$ on $2\alpha_n$ degrees of freedom.

#### *(Sequential Updating)* Posterior after $n_1 + n_2$ observations equals the posterior after $n_1$ observations used as the prior for the next $n_2$.

Because precision addition is associative, processing data in batches and collapsing to a single posterior yields the same result as processing all data at once. This is the Gaussian special case of the general *(Sequential Updating)* property in [[3.1 Bayesian Framework]]: treating the posterior as the next prior gives identical inference regardless of batching. This is the fundamental structure of online learning and the Kalman filter.

#### *(Bernstein-von Mises for Normal-Normal)* With any proper prior, the posterior converges to $N(\bar y, \sigma^2/n)$ as $n \to \infty$.

The posterior mean $\mu_n \to \bar y$ and posterior variance $\tau_n^2 \to \sigma^2/n$ because the prior contribution $1/\tau_0^2$ becomes negligible against the data contribution $n/\sigma^2$. The posterior therefore recovers the frequentist sampling distribution of the MLE, delivering the *(Prior Washing Out)* property of [[3.1 Bayesian Framework]] exactly and explicitly — for Normal-Normal, BvM is not asymptotic but happens cleanly at every $n$.

#### *(Marginal Posterior is Student-t When $\sigma^2$ is Unknown)* Integrating out $\sigma^2$ from the NIG posterior gives a Student-$t$ marginal for $\mu$.

$$\mu \mid y \;\sim\; t_{2\alpha_n}\!\left(\mu_n,\ \frac{\beta_n}{\alpha_n \kappa_n}\right),$$
with $2\alpha_n$ degrees of freedom. With a Jeffreys-style noninformative prior $p(\mu, \sigma^2) \propto 1/\sigma^2$ (the limit $\kappa_0, \alpha_0, \beta_0 \to 0$), this collapses to the classical Student-$t$ result $\mu \mid y \sim t_{n-1}(\bar y,\ s^2/n)$, where $s^2 = S/(n-1)$ is the unbiased sample variance — the Bayesian posterior and the frequentist sampling distribution coincide numerically. This is why the familiar $t$-interval is also a Bayesian credible interval under the Jeffreys prior.

#### *(Noninformative Prior and Exchangeability with Frequentist Inference)* The $\tau_0 \to \infty$ limit reproduces frequentist formulas exactly.

For known $\sigma^2$, the flat-prior posterior is $N(\bar y, \sigma^2/n)$, matching the sampling distribution of $\bar y$. For unknown $\sigma^2$ with the reference prior $p(\mu, \sigma^2) \propto \sigma^{-2}$, the marginal posterior for $\mu$ is $t_{n-1}(\bar y, s^2/n)$. The Bayesian $(1-\alpha)$ credible interval then coincides with the frequentist $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval, even though their interpretations differ — see [[3.2 Credible Intervals]] for the contrast.

#### *(Multivariate Extension)* For unknown mean vector $\boldsymbol\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with known covariance $\boldsymbol\Sigma$, the conjugate prior is multivariate Gaussian.

If $\boldsymbol\mu \sim N(\boldsymbol\mu_0, \boldsymbol\Sigma_0)$ and $\mathbf{y}_i \mid \boldsymbol\mu \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} N(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma)$, then $\boldsymbol\mu \mid \mathbf{y} \sim N(\boldsymbol\mu_n, \boldsymbol\Sigma_n)$ with $\boldsymbol\Sigma_n^{-1} = \boldsymbol\Sigma_0^{-1} + n\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}$ and $\boldsymbol\mu_n = \boldsymbol\Sigma_n(\boldsymbol\Sigma_0^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu_0 + n\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{y}})$. For unknown covariance, the conjugate prior is the **Normal-Inverse-Wishart**, the multivariate analog of NIG, used throughout Bayesian linear regression; see [[3.8.1 Bayesian Linear Regression]].

# Examples

>[!example]- Example 1: Measurement Calibration (Known $\sigma^2$)
>A scale has calibration mean $\mu$ (unknown) and known measurement noise standard deviation $\sigma = 0.1$ g. A prior based on the manufacturer's specification is $\mu \sim N(100, 1)$ (prior mean 100 g, prior standard deviation 1 g). Ten calibration weighings of a reference mass yield sample mean $\bar y = 99.87$ g.
>
>*Prior precision:* $\kappa_0 = 1/1^2 = 1$.
>*Data precision:* $n/\sigma^2 = 10/0.01 = 1000$.
>*Posterior precision:* $\kappa_n = 1001$, hence $\tau_n^2 = 1/1001 \approx 0.000999$, $\tau_n \approx 0.0316$ g.
>*Posterior mean:* $\mu_n = (1 \cdot 100 + 1000 \cdot 99.87)/1001 = 99.87013$ g.
>
>The prior's effective sample size is $n_0 = \sigma^2/\tau_0^2 = 0.01$, so 10 observations overwhelm the prior entirely. The posterior sits essentially at the sample mean with a tight credible interval $[\mu_n - 1.96\tau_n,\ \mu_n + 1.96\tau_n] \approx [99.81,\ 99.93]$ g.
>
>*Predictive for the next weighing:* $\tilde y \mid y \sim N(99.870,\ \tau_n^2 + \sigma^2) = N(99.870,\ 0.01100)$, i.e. a predictive standard deviation of $\approx 0.105$ g — only slightly wider than pure noise because the posterior uncertainty about $\mu$ is already very small.

>[!example]- Example 2: Polling with an Informative Prior
>Suppose pre-election support for a candidate has been running at 52% with historical standard deviation 2%: prior $\mu \sim N(0.52, 0.02^2)$. A new poll samples $n = 400$ respondents with observed sample proportion $\bar y = 0.48$ and reported standard error $\sigma = \sqrt{0.48 \cdot 0.52 / 400} \approx 0.025$. Treating the sample proportion as approximately Gaussian with known $\sigma$:
>
>*Precisions:* $\kappa_0 = 1/0.02^2 = 2500$; $\kappa_{\text{data}} = 1/\sigma^2 = 1/0.025^2 = 1600$.
>*Posterior precision:* $\kappa_n = 2500 + 1600 = 4100$, so $\tau_n = 1/\sqrt{4100} \approx 0.01562$.
>*Posterior mean:* $\mu_n = (2500 \cdot 0.52 + 1600 \cdot 0.48)/4100 \approx 0.5044$.
>
>The posterior sits closer to the informative prior than to the new poll because the prior carries *more* precision than the single poll. A frequentist reading the poll alone would see $0.48 \pm 1.96 \cdot 0.025 = [0.431, 0.529]$; the Bayesian credible interval $[0.5044 - 1.96 \cdot 0.0156,\ 0.5044 + 1.96 \cdot 0.0156] = [0.474, 0.535]$ is narrower and shifted, reflecting the prior information. As more polls accumulate, the prior's 2500 pseudo-observations are gradually outweighed by the accumulating $n$.

>[!example]- Example 3: Unknown Variance — Student-$t$ Credible Interval
>A sample of $n = 8$ i.i.d. observations from $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ (both unknown) has sample mean $\bar y = 5.2$ and sample sum of squares $S = 14.0$. Using the reference prior $p(\mu, \sigma^2) \propto 1/\sigma^2$ (equivalent to $\kappa_0, \alpha_0, \beta_0 \to 0$ in NIG):
>
>*Posterior parameters:* $\kappa_n = n = 8$, $\mu_n = \bar y = 5.2$, $\alpha_n = n/2 = 4$, $\beta_n = S/2 = 7.0$.
>*Marginal posterior for $\mu$:* $\mu \mid y \sim t_{2\alpha_n}\!\left(\mu_n,\ \beta_n/(\alpha_n \kappa_n)\right) = t_8\!\left(5.2,\ 0.21875\right)$.
>
>The posterior scale is $\sqrt{0.21875} \approx 0.468$. A 95% equal-tail credible interval uses the 0.975 quantile of $t_8$, which is 2.306:
>$$[5.2 - 2.306 \cdot 0.468,\ 5.2 + 2.306 \cdot 0.468] = [4.12, 6.28].$$
>This matches the classical 95% $t$-interval $\bar y \pm t_{n-1, 0.975}\, s/\sqrt{n}$ with $s^2 = S/(n-1) = 2.0$, so $s/\sqrt n = \sqrt{2/8} \approx 0.500$ and $t_{7, 0.975} = 2.365$, giving $[4.02, 6.38]$. The slight numerical difference comes from using the posterior's $2\alpha_n = 8$ degrees of freedom instead of $n - 1 = 7$; it reflects the choice between the Jeffreys prior ($\alpha_0 = 0$) and the marginally stronger "classical" convention that debits one degree of freedom for estimating $\sigma^2$.

# Derivation

Goal: derive the posterior $\mu \mid y \sim N(\mu_n, \tau_n^2)$ under the known-variance Normal-Normal model.

Starting from Bayes' rule, the log posterior is
$$\log p(\mu \mid y) \;=\; \log p(\mu) + \sum_{i=1}^n \log p(y_i \mid \mu) + \text{const},$$
with both terms quadratic in $\mu$:
$$\log p(\mu) \;=\; -\tfrac{1}{2\tau_0^2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2 + c_1,$$
$$\sum_i \log p(y_i \mid \mu) \;=\; -\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (y_i - \mu)^2 + c_2 \;=\; -\tfrac{n}{2\sigma^2}(\mu - \bar y)^2 + c_3.$$
Adding the two quadratics and completing the square in $\mu$:
$$\log p(\mu \mid y) \;=\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \tfrac{n}{\sigma^2}\right)\!\!\left(\mu - \mu_n\right)^2 + c,$$
where $\mu_n$ is the weighted average $\tau_n^2(\mu_0/\tau_0^2 + n\bar y/\sigma^2)$ and $\tau_n^2 = (1/\tau_0^2 + n/\sigma^2)^{-1}$. Exponentiating gives the claimed $N(\mu_n, \tau_n^2)$ posterior.

The posterior-predictive derivation is similar: marginalize $\tilde y$ over the Gaussian posterior for $\mu$,
$$\tilde y \;=\; \mu + \epsilon, \qquad \mu \sim N(\mu_n, \tau_n^2), \qquad \epsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2),$$
with $\mu$ and $\epsilon$ independent. The sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian with added variances: $\tilde y \mid y \sim N(\mu_n, \tau_n^2 + \sigma^2)$.
