>[!note] Terminal Voltage
>An ideal source connected across a resistor $R$ drives the current $I = \mathcal{E}/R$. As $R$ shrinks toward zero, this current grows without limit. No real source behaves this way. Every real source is **current limited**, which means it has a largest current it can deliver. The limit depends on how the source is built, for example on the chemicals in a battery.
>
>A simple model describes every real source. The model is an ideal source of emf $\mathcal{E}$ in series with a small resistor $r$, the **internal resistance**. The pair together counts as the source, with positive terminal $a$ and negative terminal $b$. An external resistor $R$ across the terminals is in series with $r$, so the current is
>$$I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R + r}.$$
>Here $R + r$ is the [[3.7.2. Equivalent Resistance|equivalent resistance]] of the loop. As $R \to 0$, the current approaches the finite value $\mathcal{E}/r$. This value is the source's **short-circuit current**, the largest current it can deliver.
>
>The **terminal voltage** $V_{ab}$ is the potential difference between the terminals while the source delivers a current $I$:
>$$V_{ab} = \mathcal{E} - Ir.$$
>The term $Ir$ is the potential drop across the internal resistance. The terminal voltage equals $\mathcal{E}$ only when no current flows. The EE note [[2.1c Voltage Source]] models only the ideal source.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture introduced the internal-resistance model and the current limit $\mathcal{E}/r$. It did not write the terminal voltage formula $V_{ab} = \mathcal{E} - Ir$, which comes from Giancoli section 26.1.

# Intuition

The **terminals** of a source are the two metal contacts where wires attach. The terminal voltage is the potential difference between them, the one the rest of the circuit receives. A real source uses part of its emf to drive current through its own material. That part never reaches the terminals. A larger current uses more of it, so the terminal voltage falls as the current rises.

A fresh AA cell has an internal resistance of a few tenths of an ohm. Its short-circuit current is therefore several amperes. A classroom demonstration held up an AA cell and treated it as an ideal $1.5\ \text{V}$ source. That ideal source would push $150\ \text{A}$ through a $0.010\ \Omega$ resistor, and no AA cell comes close.

The cell of the Hard example drives a load that shrinks from an open circuit to a wire. Each point lands on the line $V_{ab} = \mathcal{E} - Ir$. The bar splits $\mathcal{E}$ into $V_{ab}$ and the drop $Ir$ inside the cell.

# Derivation

Walk through the source from terminal $b$ to terminal $a$, in the direction of the current inside it. Crossing the ideal source from $-$ to $+$ raises the potential by $\mathcal{E}$. Crossing $r$ in the direction of the current lowers it by $Ir$, by [[3.6.4. Ohm's Law]]. The potential differences along the path add, as in the series derivation of [[3.7.2. Equivalent Resistance]]:
$$V_{ab} = V_a - V_b = \mathcal{E} - Ir.$$
Here $V_a - V_b$ is the potential of the positive terminal minus that of the negative terminal.

The external resistor has the terminal voltage across it, so $V_{ab} = IR$. Setting the two expressions equal gives $IR = \mathcal{E} - Ir$. Solving for the current gives $I = \mathcal{E}/(R + r)$, the series result. Substituting this current back gives
$$V_{ab} = \mathcal{E}\,\frac{R}{R + r}.$$
The fraction $R/(R + r)$ is the share of the emf that reaches the external resistor.

# Properties

#### *(Straight-Line Drop)* Plotted against the current, the terminal voltage falls on a straight line from $\mathcal{E}$ at zero current to zero at the short-circuit current.

The line $V_{ab} = \mathcal{E} - Ir$ has intercept $\mathcal{E}$ and slope $-r$. Two pairs of current and terminal voltage fix the line, so they fix both $\mathcal{E}$ and $r$. At the short-circuit current $\mathcal{E}/r$, a wire joins the terminals, and $V_{ab} = 0$. The whole emf then drives current through $r$.

#### *(When $r$ Matters)* Internal resistance matters only when the external resistance is not much larger than $r$.

The terminal voltage is the fraction $R/(R + r)$ of the emf. For $R = 100r$, the fraction is $0.990$, and the source acts as ideal to within one percent. For $R = r$, the terminal voltage is half the emf. Most circuits in these notes have $R \gg r$, and the notes treat their sources as ideal. Circuits with large currents, such as a car's starter motor, need $r$.

#### *(Short Circuits Heat the Source)* With its terminals joined by a wire, a source dissipates its whole output inside itself.

The short-circuit current $\mathcal{E}/r$ flows through $r$ alone. The power is $I^2r = \mathcal{E}^2/r$, all of it inside the source. A $9.0\ \text{V}$ battery with $r = 1.5\ \Omega$ would deliver $6.0\ \text{A}$ and dissipate $54\ \text{W}$ inside its case. A shorted battery therefore heats up fast.

#### *(Limits of the Model)* The internal resistance summarizes a real source's behavior, and its value changes with age, temperature, and current.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture stated that the model is an approximation that every source can be fitted to. It did not discuss how $r$ changes with age and temperature. This property is standard textbook material.

No one can remove $r$ from a battery, because it is not a separate part. A battery's $r$ grows as it runs down. An old battery can show nearly its full emf with nothing attached and still fail under load. Cold also raises $r$, so car batteries deliver less current on cold mornings. Over a limited range of currents, any source still fits the model. EE generalizes the same idea to any two-terminal network in [[2.2c.2 Thevenin Equivalence]].

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Starting a Car
>A car battery has $\mathcal{E} = 12.6\ \text{V}$ and $r = 0.020\ \Omega$. The starter motor draws $150\ \text{A}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the terminal voltage while the starter runs, and the power lost inside the battery.
>>The terminal voltage is
>>$$V_{ab} = \mathcal{E} - Ir = 12.6 - (150)(0.020) = 9.6\ \text{V}.$$
>>The internal resistance takes $3.0\ \text{V}$ of the emf. Everything else wired to the battery, such as the headlights, also gets only $9.6\ \text{V}$ and dims. The power lost inside is $I^2r = (150)^2(0.020) = 450\ \text{W}$, which warms the battery.

>[!example] (Medium) Finding $\mathcal{E}$ and $r$
>A source drives $2.0\ \text{A}$ through a $2.5\ \Omega$ resistor. With a $5.5\ \Omega$ resistor in place of the first, it drives $1.0\ \text{A}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the emf, the internal resistance, and the terminal voltage in each case.
>>The current obeys $\mathcal{E} = I(R + r)$ in both cases:
>>$$\mathcal{E} = (2.0)(2.5 + r) = (1.0)(5.5 + r).$$
>>The two expressions are equal, so $5.0 + 2.0r = 5.5 + r$, and $r = 0.50\ \Omega$. Then $\mathcal{E} = (1.0)(5.5 + 0.50) = 6.0\ \text{V}$. The terminal voltages are $V_{ab} = IR = (2.0)(2.5) = 5.0\ \text{V}$ and $(1.0)(5.5) = 5.5\ \text{V}$. Both points lie on the line $V_{ab} = 6.0 - 0.50I$.

>[!example] (Hard) A Cell Under Load
>A cell has $\mathcal{E} = 1.5\ \text{V}$ and $r = 0.30\ \Omega$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the external resistance for which the terminal voltage is 90% of the emf. For that resistance, find the power the ideal source produces, the power the load receives, and the power lost inside the cell. Then find the short-circuit current.
>>The terminal voltage is the fraction $R/(R + r)$ of the emf. Setting that fraction equal to $0.90$ gives
>>$$R = 0.90(R + r), \qquad R = 9r = 2.7\ \Omega.$$
>>The current is then $I = 1.5/(2.7 + 0.30) = 0.50\ \text{A}$. The ideal source produces $\mathcal{E}I = 0.75\ \text{W}$. The load receives $I^2R = (0.50)^2(2.7) = 0.675\ \text{W}$. The internal resistance takes $I^2r = 0.075\ \text{W}$. The two parts add to $0.75\ \text{W}$. The load gets 90% of the power, the same fraction as the voltage.
>>
>>With a wire across the terminals, the current is $\mathcal{E}/r = 1.5/0.30 = 5.0\ \text{A}$. All of $\mathcal{E}^2/r = 7.5\ \text{W}$ then goes into the cell itself.
