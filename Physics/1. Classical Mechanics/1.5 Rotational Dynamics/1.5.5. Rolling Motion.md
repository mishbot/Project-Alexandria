>[!note] Rolling Motion
>**Rolling motion** is the combination of translational motion of a rigid body’s center of mass and rotational motion about its center of mass. 
>
>A common example is a wheel rolling on a surface. In this motion, the center of mass translates with velocity $v_{\text{C.M.}}$ while every point on the body also rotates with angular velocity $\omega$:
>
>
>If the wheel **rolls without slipping**, experiencing static friction, then the translational and rotational speeds are related by
>$$v_{\text{C.M.}} = R\omega \iff a_{\text{C.M.}}=R\alpha,$$
>where $R$ is the radius of the wheel. The point of contact with the ground has zero instantaneous velocity relative to the surface.
>
>If **slipping occurs**, $v_{\text{C.M.}} \neq R\omega$, and the point of contact moves relative to the ground. Rolling without slipping is a key condition in problems involving rotational dynamics, energy conservation, and torque analysis.

>[!example] (Easy) Rolling Cylinder I
>A cylinder of uniform mass $M$ and radius $R$ is placed on a frictional surface. The rotational inertia of the cylinder with respect to its central axis is $I$. A constant force of magnitude $F$ is applied to the top of the cylinder in the horizontal direction as shown in the figure.
>
>![[Screenshot 2025-08-13 at 0.32.16.png|300]]
>
>>[!example]- Find the acceleration of the cylinder, assuming that it does not skid or slide on the surface.
>>Since we have static friction in the backward direction, it holds that $F-f_{s}=Ma$ in the horizontal. We also calculate torque as $I \alpha = R(F + f_{s})=R(2F-Ma)$. Since $a = R\alpha$, then
>>$$\frac{Ia}{R}=R(2F-Ma) \iff a=\frac{2FR^2}{I+R^2M}.$$
>
>>[!example]- What is the minimal value of the coefficient of static friction $\mu_{s,\min}$ needed in order for the perfect rolling condition (no skidding, no sliding) to hold?
>>Since $f_{s}=F - Ma$ as found before, we substitute for $a$ and solve:
>>$$f_{s}=\frac{I-R^2M}{I+R^2M}F \le \mu_{s, \min}Mg \iff \mu_{s} \geq \frac{I-R^2M}{I+R^2M} \frac{F}{Mg},$$
>>where the equality is the value of $\mu_{s, \min}$.

>[!example] (Medium) Rolling Cylinder II
>A cylinder of uniform mass $M$ and radius $R$ is placed on a frictional surface. The rotational inertia of the cylinder with respect to its central axis is $I$. A constant force of magnitude $F$ is applied to the bottom right of the cylinder, at a point that is $45\degree$ to the vertical line, and with a direction that is $45\degree$ above the horizontal, as shown in the figure.
>
>![[Screenshot 2025-08-13 at 1.57.59.png|300]]
>
> Assume the cylinder does not skid nor slide on the surface, and that the force is not large enough to lift the cylinder. 
>>[!example]- Find the acceleration of the cylinder, including the magnitude and direction.
>>We have that $I\alpha=R(F-f_{s})$ This is because the movement is to the left, but the force of friction that assists the rolling motion is clockwise as opposed to the force. Then, we also have $-Ma=F\cos 45\degree$

>[!example] (Hard) Hollow Sphere Rolling Motion
>A hollow sphere of mass $M$ and radius $R$, starting from rest at height $h_1$, rolls down a rough incline that terminates, at height $h_2$, in a ramp pointing up at a $45°$ angle, as shown in the figure below. Note that $R$ is small compared to the heights $h_1$ and $h_2$. The ball rolls without slipping over its entire displacement along the ramp. 
>
>After traveling in the air, the sphere gets lodged into a stationary block of mass $M$ placed on a rough horizontal surface. The coefficient of kinetic friction is $\mu_k$. Assume that the block does not tip over or rebound after the sphere gets lodged into it.
>
>![[Screenshot 2025-11-04 at 11.49.32.png|250]]
>
>>[!example]- Determine the speed $v_{0}$ of the sphere as it leaves the ramp.
>>We have both rotational and translational energy. As such, we do:
>>$$\begin{align}
Mgh_{1}&=Mgh_{2}+\frac{1}{2}Mv_{0}^2+\frac{1}{2}I\omega_{0}^2 \\
&=Mgh_{2}+\frac{1}{2}Mv_{0}^2+\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{2}{3}MR^2 \cdot \frac{v_{0}^2}{R^2} \\
&=Mgh_{2}+\frac{1}{2}Mv_{0}^2+\frac{1}{3}Mv_{0}^2,
\end{align}$$
where $I=\frac{2}{3}MR^2$ for a hollow sphere, which leads to $v_{0}=\sqrt{ \frac{6}{5}g(h_{1}-h_{2}) }.$
>
>>[!example]- Determine the angular speed $\omega_{\text{peak}}$ of the sphere at the top of its trajectory in the air.
>>Since there is no torque exerted on the sphere when it's in the air, angular momentum is conserved. We can safely assume that $$\omega_{\text{peak}}=\omega_{0}=\frac{v_{0}}{R}=\frac{1}{R}v_{0}=\frac{1}{R}\sqrt{ \frac{6}{5}g(h_{1}-h_{2}) }.$$
>
>>[!example]- Determine what type of collision is experienced by the sphere and the block and in which direction the conservation law applies.  
>>The collision is inelastic, since if we assume that the block does not move after the collision, then the hollow sphere sticks to the block. As such, linear momentum is conserved in the horizontal direction - there is clearly vertical momentum before the collision, but the vertical momentum is zero after the collision. The combined system moves in the positive-$x$ direction. Angular momentum is also not preserved, since the block does not tip over.
>
>>[!example]- Determine the speed of the block, $v_{B}$, right after it gets struck by the sphere. You may treat both objects as point masses.  
>>We calculate $v_{f}=\sqrt{ \frac{6}{5}g(h_{1}-h_{2})\cos^245\degree }=\sqrt{ \frac{3}{5}g(h_{1}-h_{2}) }$ in the $x$-direction, since the post-collision system moves solely in the $x$ direction. As such,
>>$$Mv_{f}=2Mv_{b} \implies v_{b}=\sqrt{ \frac{3}{20}g(h_{1}-h_{2}) }.$$
>
>>[!example]- Determine the distance $d$ traveled by the block-sphere system until it gets to a stop.
>>Setting kinetic friction as a backward force, we get
>>$$2Ma=-\mu_{k}2Mg \implies a = -\mu_{k}g.$$
>>Together with the velocity-displacement equation, this becomes
>>$$0=\frac{3}{20}g(h_{1}-h_{2})-2\mu_{k}gd \implies d = \frac{3}{40\mu_{k}}(h_{1}-h_{2}).$$
>>
