>[!note] Asian Options
>An **Asian option** replaces the final price $S_T$ or the strike with the average $A_T$ of observed prices. The contract observes the price on fixing dates $t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_n = T$, where $T$ is the expiry. The arithmetic average is
>$$A_T = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} S_{t_i},$$
>where $S_{t_i}$ is the price on fixing date $t_i$. Asian options come in two kinds, each with a call and a put.
>- An **average price** call and put keep a fixed strike $K$ and pay $\max(A_T - K, 0)$ and $\max(K - A_T, 0)$.
>- An **average strike** call and put use $A_T$ as the strike and pay $\max(S_T - A_T, 0)$ and $\max(A_T - S_T, 0)$.
>
>The geometric average $G_T = \big(\prod_{i=1}^{n} S_{t_i}\big)^{1/n}$ never exceeds $A_T$. Let $C_A$ and $P_A$ be an average price call and put with the same $K$, $T$ and fixings. For a stock with no dividends, valued before the first fixing, **Asian parity** states
>$$C_A - P_A = S_0\,a - Ke^{-rT}, \qquad a = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} e^{-r(T - t_i)}.$$
>Here $S_0$ is today's price and $r$ is the continuously compounded risk-free rate. The factor $a$ averages the discount factors from $T$ back to each fixing date.
>
>Like [[2.3.6.2a Barrier Options|barrier]] and [[2.3.6.2c Lookback Options|lookback options]], an Asian option is path-dependent, since its payoff uses prices observed before $T$. It builds on the vanilla options of the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]].

# Intuition

Many exposures depend on a path. A refinery that buys oil at a monthly average price has a cost set by an average. An option on that average matches the exposure. An average moves less than a single price, so an Asian option costs less than the vanilla.

# Properties

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture says geometric average options have a closed form under constant parameters but does not state it. The formula below is Hull's, Chapter 26, for continuous averaging from the start of the option.

The log of a geometric average is an average of normal variables, so $G_T$ is lognormal. For continuous averaging over $[0, T]$, a geometric average price call is a Black-Scholes-Merton call with two inputs changed:
$$\sigma_G = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{3}}, \qquad q_G = \frac{1}{2}\Big(r + q + \frac{\sigma^2}{6}\Big).$$
Here $\sigma_G$ replaces the volatility and $q_G$ replaces the dividend yield. With $S_0 = K = 100$, $r = 5\%$, $q = 0$, $\sigma = 30\%$ and $T = 1$, this gives $\sigma_G = 17.32\%$, $q_G = 3.25\%$ and a price of $\$7.50$. The vanilla call costs $\$14.23$.

The arithmetic average price call has no closed form, but the geometric one bounds it. Every path has $G_T \le A_T$, so the geometric call pays less in every state, and $c_G \le c_A$. Monte Carlo pricing uses this pair as a **control variate**. A simulation estimates both calls on the same paths. The error in the geometric estimate is known, since its exact price is known. Subtracting that error, scaled by a regression coefficient, from the arithmetic estimate removes most of the noise. With the inputs above and $200{,}000$ paths, the arithmetic call is worth $\$7.94$. Its standard error falls from $0.027$ to $0.001$ with the control.

#### *(Uses)* Asian options hedge costs and revenues that are set by an average.

Many firms buy or sell a steady flow of goods at an average price. FX and commodity markets therefore trade Asian options widely. A refinery that buys $50{,}000$ barrels of crude oil a month at the monthly average price can cap its cost. It buys $50$ average price call contracts of $1{,}000$ barrels each on the same benchmark. The strike is $\$75$ and the premium is $\$1.80$ per barrel. Ignoring interest on the premium, the net cost per barrel is
$$A_T - \max(A_T - K, 0) + 1.80 = \min(A_T, K) + 1.80.$$
An average of $\$84$ gives a net cost of $\$76.80$, and an average of $\$68$ gives $\$69.80$. A call on the last day's price could pay nothing in a month whose average was high. An **accelerated share repurchase** is a second use. A company buys a block of its own shares from a bank at once. The average market price over the following months sets the final price. The contract therefore settles on an average.

#### *(Three-Pair Identity)* Average price, average strike and vanilla options with the same strike and expiry satisfy $c_1 + c_2 - c_3 = p_1 + p_2 - p_3$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>This identity is covered only in the homework (Hull 26.4).

Let $c_1$ and $p_1$ be the average price call and put. Let $c_2$ and $p_2$ be the average strike pair, and $c_3$ and $p_3$ the vanilla pair. Each call-put pair has a linear payoff, because $\max(x, 0) - \max(-x, 0) = x$ for every $x$:
$$\max(A_T - K, 0) - \max(K - A_T, 0) = A_T - K,$$
$$\max(S_T - A_T, 0) - \max(A_T - S_T, 0) = S_T - A_T,$$
$$\max(S_T - K, 0) - \max(K - S_T, 0) = S_T - K.$$
The first two lines sum to the third. A portfolio long $c_1$, $c_2$ and $p_3$ and short $p_1$, $p_2$ and $c_3$ therefore pays $(A_T - K) + (S_T - A_T) - (S_T - K) = 0$ in every state. With no arbitrage it costs zero today, so $c_1 - p_1 + c_2 - p_2 - c_3 + p_3 = 0$, which rearranges to the identity. The proof uses payoffs only. It holds in any model and for any averaging rule. Both Asian pairs must use the same $A_T$, and all six options must be European.

# Derivation

We want $C_A - P_A = S_0 a - Ke^{-rT}$ for a stock with no dividends, valued before the first fixing.

First, the payoff. The identity $\max(x, 0) - \max(-x, 0) = x$ gives, in every state,
$$\max(A_T - K, 0) - \max(K - A_T, 0) = A_T - K.$$
So a long call and a short put pay $A_T - K$ at $T$.

Next, replicate one term of the average. Buy $w_i = e^{-r(T - t_i)}/n$ shares today. Sell them at $t_i$ for $w_i S_{t_i}$ and invest the cash at $r$ until $T$. At $T$ the cash is
$$w_i S_{t_i} e^{r(T - t_i)} = \frac{S_{t_i}}{n}.$$
The interest earned after $t_i$ cancels the discount in $w_i$.

Then sum over the fixings. The shares bought today total $\sum_i w_i = a$, which cost $S_0 a$. At $T$ the cash adds up to $A_T$. Borrow $Ke^{-rT}$ today and repay $K$ at $T$. The strategy pays $A_T - K$ and costs $S_0 a - Ke^{-rT}$. By the law of one price, the call minus the put costs the same. This is the formula in the callout. When $r = 0$, each $w_i = 1/n$. The strategy then buys one share and sells $1/n$ of it at each fixing.

Last, the Greeks. The right side is linear in $S_0$ and does not contain $\sigma$. Differentiating once and twice in $S_0$, and once in $\sigma$, gives
$$\Delta_{C_A} - \Delta_{P_A} = a, \qquad \Gamma_{C_A} = \Gamma_{P_A}, \qquad \mathcal{V}_{C_A} = \mathcal{V}_{P_A}.$$
Here $\mathcal{V}$ is vega. Each option alone can have large gamma and vega. The two cancel only in the long call, short put pair. With $r > 0$, $a < 1$, so the delta gap is smaller than the gap of $1$ for vanilla options.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The discussion section derives the parity for a stock with no dividends, before the first fixing. With a dividend yield $q$, reinvesting the dividends until $t_i$ changes the weights to $w_i = e^{-qt_i - r(T - t_i)}/n$. After some fixings are known, their share of $A_T$ is fixed cash, and only the future fixings need shares.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Asian Payoffs on One Path
>A six-month Asian option on a commodity fixes the price at the end of each month. The six fixings are $\$72$, $\$75$, $\$71$, $\$78$, $\$82$ and $\$80$. The last fixing is the price at expiry, $S_T = \$80$. The fixed strike is $K = \$75$. Only payoffs are needed, so no discounting is used.
>
>>[!example]- Find the payoffs of the four Asian options and of the vanilla call and put with strike $\$75$. Check how the call payoffs relate.
>>The average of the fixings is
>>$$A_T = \frac{72 + 75 + 71 + 78 + 82 + 80}{6} = \frac{458}{6} = \$76.33.$$
>>- The average price call pays $\max(76.33 - 75, 0) = \$1.33$, and the average price put pays nothing.
>>- The average strike call pays $\max(80 - 76.33, 0) = \$3.67$, and the average strike put pays nothing.
>>- The vanilla call pays $\max(80 - 75, 0) = \$5$, and the vanilla put pays nothing.
>>
>>The average price call pays much less than the vanilla call. Each fixing has a weight of $1/6$. So the late prices of $\$82$ and $\$80$ raise the average only a little. The two Asian call payoffs add up to the vanilla call payoff, since $1.33 + 3.67 = 5$. All three puts pay zero here. So this path follows the payoff rule $(A_T - K) + (S_T - A_T) = S_T - K$.

>[!example] (Medium) An Average Price Put From the Call
>A stock trades at $\$64$ and pays no dividends. A one-year average price call with strike $\$62$ uses four quarterly fixings, at $t = 0.25$, $0.5$, $0.75$ and $1$. It trades at $\$7.14$, and its delta is $0.643$. The risk-free rate is $5\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Find the price of the matching average price put, the shares to buy today to replicate the call minus the put, and the put's delta, gamma and vega.
>>The share weights are $w_i = e^{-0.05(1 - t_i)}/4$:
>>$$w_1 = 0.24080, \qquad w_2 = 0.24383, \qquad w_3 = 0.24689, \qquad w_4 = 0.25000.$$
>>They sum to $a = 0.98152$. Asian parity gives
>>$$C_A - P_A = 64 \times 0.98152 - 62e^{-0.05} = 62.817 - 58.976 = 3.841.$$
>>So the put costs $P_A = 7.14 - 3.841 = \$3.30$.
>>
>>For $1{,}000$ call-minus-put pairs, buy $240.80$ shares to sell at the first fixing. Buy $243.83$, $246.89$ and $250.00$ shares for the other three. That is $981.52$ shares, which cost $\$62{,}817$. Borrow $\$58{,}976$, so the net cost is $\$3{,}841$, or $\$3.841$ per pair. The first block is sold at $t_1$ for $240.80\,S_{t_1}$. Nine months of interest grow it to $240.80\,e^{0.05 \times 0.75}S_{t_1} = 250.00\,S_{t_1}$, which is $S_{t_1}/4$ per pair.
>>
>>The put's delta is $\Delta_{P_A} = \Delta_{C_A} - a = 0.643 - 0.982 = -0.339$. The put has the same gamma and vega as the call. The call price matches a volatility of $30\%$. A simulation at that volatility gives both options a gamma of $0.0275$ and a vega of $15.77$. A vanilla call with the same strike costs $\$10.11$, so averaging cuts the call's price by $29\%$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The discussion section derives Asian parity only before the first fixing. This example applies it after two fixings are known.

>[!example] (Hard) Asian Parity After Two Fixings
>A one-year average price call and put on a stock with no dividends have strike $\$45$. They use four quarterly fixings, at $t = 0.25$, $0.5$, $0.75$ and $1$. Six months have passed, and the first two fixings were $\$41.20$ and $\$46.80$. The stock trades at $\$46.80$ today. The call trades at $\$1.66$, and its delta is $0.305$. The risk-free rate is $3.5\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A dealer quotes the put at $\$0.85$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the fair put price and the put's delta. Then find an arbitrage against the dealer's quote on $1{,}000$ pairs.
>>The two known fixings add a fixed amount to the average:
>>$$A_T = \frac{41.20 + 46.80}{4} + \frac{S_{0.75} + S_1}{4} = 22.00 + \frac{S_{0.75} + S_1}{4}.$$
>>So the call minus the put pays $A_T - 45 = (S_{0.75} + S_1)/4 - 23.00$ at expiry. The cash part is worth $-23.00e^{-0.035 \times 0.5} = -22.601$ today. The share part needs shares only for the two future fixings. Their weights are $w_3 = e^{-0.035 \times 0.25}/4 = 0.24782$ and $w_4 = 0.25000$, which sum to $0.49782$. Parity today reads
>>$$C_A - P_A = 46.80 \times 0.49782 - 22.601 = 23.298 - 22.601 = 0.697.$$
>>The fair put is $1.66 - 0.697 = \$0.96$. Only two fixings remain, so the delta gap is $0.498$. The put's delta is $0.305 - 0.498 = -0.193$.
>>
>>The dealer's put is $\$0.11$ too cheap. Buy $1{,}000$ puts at $\$0.85$ and sell $1{,}000$ calls at $\$1.66$, which brings in $\$810$. Buy $247.82$ shares to sell at the third fixing and $250.00$ shares to sell at the last one. The $497.82$ shares cost $\$23{,}298$. Borrow $23{,}000e^{-0.0175} = \$22{,}601$. The net cash today is $810 - 23{,}298 + 22{,}601 = \$113$.
>>
>>At expiry the options pay $1{,}000(45 - A_T)$. The shares, sold at the fixings with the cash invested at $3.5\%$, give $250(S_{0.75} + S_1) = 1{,}000(A_T - 22.00)$. The loan repayment is $\$23{,}000$. The total is $1{,}000(45 - A_T) + 1{,}000(A_T - 22) - 23{,}000 = 0$ in every state. So the $\$113$ received today is a riskless profit.
