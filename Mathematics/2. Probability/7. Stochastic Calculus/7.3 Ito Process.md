>[!note] Itô Process
>An **Itô process** is a quantity that changes over each short time step by a predictable amount plus a random shock. Both the expected change and the size of the shock may depend on the current value and on time. In differential form,
>$$dx = a(x, t)\,dt + b(x, t)\,dW_t,$$
>where $W_t$ is a [[7.1 Generalized Wiener Process|Wiener process]]. The subscript $t$ on $x$ is usually dropped. The function $a$ is the **drift**, the expected change in $x$ per unit time at the current value. The function $b$ is the **diffusion coefficient**, which scales the random part, so $b^2$ is the variance of the change per unit time. Over a short step $\Delta t$ that starts from the value $x$ at time $t$, the change is approximately
>$$\Delta x \approx a(x, t)\,\Delta t + b(x, t)\,\varepsilon\sqrt{\Delta t}, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).$$
>So the step is approximately normal with mean $a\,\Delta t$ and variance $b^2\Delta t$, with both coefficients taken at the start of the step. The differential is shorthand for adding up many such steps. The [[7.4 Ito Integral|Itô integral]] gives the sum of the $b\,dW_t$ terms an exact meaning. An equation of this form is called a **stochastic differential equation** (SDE). With constant $a$ and $b$ it is the generalized Wiener process. The standard stock model $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t$, called [[7.6 Geometric Brownian Motion|geometric Brownian motion]], has both coefficients proportional to the price. The [[7.7 Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process|Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process]] has a drift that pulls $x$ back toward a fixed level. The squared steps of an Itô process add up to $\int b^2\,dt$ ([[7.2 Quadratic Variation]]). [[7.5 Ito's Lemma|Itô's lemma]] gives the Itô process followed by a function $G(x, t)$.

# Intuition

A generalized Wiener process gives the same dollar noise at every price level. That does not fit a stock. Suppose a stock moves about $2\%$ on a typical day. At $\$50$ that is $\$1$, and at $\$200$ it is $\$4$. A constant scale $b$ would give both prices the same dollar move. For interest rates the problem is the drift. A rate at $8\%$ tends to fall and a rate at $1\%$ tends to rise, so the expected change depends on the level. A constant drift $a$ cannot capture that.

The fix is to let the coefficients depend on where the process is now. At the start of each short step, compute the drift and the size of the shock from the current value $x$ and the time $t$. Take the step. Then compute both again at the new value. Each step is a small generalized Wiener step whose coefficients come from the value reached by the step before.

Here is one step of the stock model, with $a = \mu S$ and $b = \sigma S$. Take $\mu = 0.10$, $\sigma = 0.30$, a daily step $\Delta t = 1/250$, and a draw $\varepsilon = 1.2$. At $S = \$50$ the drift adds $0.10 \times 50/250 = \$0.02$. The shock adds $0.30 \times 50 \times 1.2 \times \sqrt{1/250} = \$1.14$. The new price is $\$51.16$. At $S = \$200$ the same draw gives $\$0.08 + \$4.55$, and the new price is $\$204.63$. The same draw moves the higher price four times as many dollars, because both coefficients are four times as large.

The name honors Kiyosi Itô, who built the calculus for these processes. The coefficient $b$ is called the diffusion coefficient because it sets how fast the possible values of $x$ spread apart over time. Each part of the formula comes from one step above. Letting the trend and the shock size depend on the state gives $a(x, t)$ and $b(x, t)$. The Wiener process supplies the shock $dW_t$, with variance $dt$. The step-by-step update, with coefficients taken at the start of each step, is what the differential $dx$ records.

# Properties

#### *(Coefficients as Conditional Moments)* The drift and the squared diffusion coefficient are the mean and the mean square of a short step, per unit time, given the current value.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Reading $a$ and $b$ as conditional moments, and estimating them from data, are not covered in the modules.

Given $x_t = x$, a short step has $\mathbb{E}[\Delta x] \approx a(x, t)\,\Delta t$. Its square is $(\Delta x)^2 = a^2(\Delta t)^2 + 2ab\,\Delta t\,\Delta W + b^2(\Delta W)^2$, with mean $a^2(\Delta t)^2 + b^2\Delta t$. As $\Delta t \to 0$ the first term is negligible, so
$$a(x, t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\Delta x \mid x_t = x]}{\Delta t}, \qquad b(x, t)^2 = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\Delta x)^2 \mid x_t = x]}{\Delta t}.$$
These limits give each coefficient a direct reading. The drift is the expected change per unit time when the process sits at $x$. The squared diffusion coefficient is the variance of the change per unit time at $x$. The units follow. If $x$ is in dollars and time in years, $a$ is in dollars per year and $b$ in dollars per square root of a year. To estimate the coefficients from data, group the observed changes by their starting value $x$. The average change in a group estimates $a(x)\,\Delta t$, and the average squared change estimates $b(x)^2\,\Delta t$. The second estimate is precise even over a short sample, while the first needs a long one, as in [[7.2 Quadratic Variation]].

#### *(Euler Simulation Rule)* A computer simulates an Itô process by repeating the one-step update, with the coefficients taken at the start of each step.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules show simulated stock paths but do not state the simulation rule below, known as the Euler scheme.

Given the value $x_k$ at time $t_k$, the rule sets the next value to
$$x_{k+1} = x_k + a(x_k, t_k)\,\Delta t + b(x_k, t_k)\,\Delta W_k, \qquad \Delta W_k = \varepsilon_k\sqrt{\Delta t}.$$
Here $\varepsilon_k$ is a new, independent standard normal draw in each step. Each step is normal given $x_k$. When $b$ depends on $x$, the size of each shock depends on the earlier shocks. So the value after several steps is usually not normal. Adding the steps from $0$ to $t$ gives a Riemann sum of the drift terms plus the sum of the terms $b(x_k, t_k)\,\Delta W_k$. That second sum takes $b$ at the start of each step, which is the left-endpoint rule of the [[7.4 Ito Integral|Itô integral]], and its limit gives the differential its exact meaning.

#### *(Markov Property)* The distribution of the future path depends only on the current value and time, not on how the process got there.

A sequence of random variables $x_0, x_1, \dots$ has the **Markov property** when
$$\mathbb{P}[x_{n+1} \mid x_n, x_{n-1}, \dots, x_0] = \mathbb{P}[x_{n+1} \mid x_n],$$
as for a [[4.3a Markov Chains|Markov chain]]. The condition says that to forecast the future, only the present value matters. Each step of an Itô process uses $x_k$, $t_k$, and a fresh draw $\varepsilon_k$ that is independent of the past. So the past enters only through $x_k$, and an Itô process has the Markov property. For this reason the price of a European option on a stock under the standard model is a function $f(S, t)$ of today's price and time only. The rest of the price history adds no information about the payoff. A process loses the property when $a$ or $b$ depends on past values, such as the highest price so far. The state must then include that extra variable.

#### *(Stock Price Model)* In $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t$, the drift and the volatility apply to the percentage change.

The stock model is the Itô process with $a(S, t) = \mu S$ and $b(S, t) = \sigma S$. Dividing by $S$ gives $dS/S = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t$. Over a short step the return $\Delta S/S$ is approximately normal with mean $\mu\,\Delta t$ and variance $\sigma^2\Delta t$. So $\mu$ is the expected return per year, and $\sigma$ is the volatility, the annualized standard deviation of returns. [[3.1f Historical Volatility]] estimates $\sigma$ from past returns. With $\sigma = 0$ the model has no randomness. Then $d(\ln S) = dS/S = \mu\,dt$, and integrating from $0$ to $t$ gives
$$\ln\frac{S_t}{S_0} = \int_0^t \mu\,ds = \mu t, \qquad S_t = S_0e^{\mu t}.$$
This is growth at the continuously compounded rate $\mu$. With $\sigma > 0$ the step $d(\ln S) = dS/S$ is no longer correct, because $(dS)^2 = \sigma^2S^2\,dt$ does not vanish. [[7.6 Geometric Brownian Motion]] uses [[7.5 Ito's Lemma|Itô's lemma]] to find the solution.

#### *(Mean Reversion)* A drift that points toward a fixed level pulls the process back to that level.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Mean-reverting processes are not in the modules. [[7.7 Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process]] covers them.

The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process has drift $a(x, t) = \kappa(\theta - x)$ and a constant diffusion coefficient $\sigma$. Here $\theta$ is the long-run level and $\kappa > 0$ is the speed of the pull. Above $\theta$ the drift is negative, and below $\theta$ it is positive. Its size grows with the distance from $\theta$. Over a step of length $\Delta t$, the expected change closes a fraction $\kappa\,\Delta t$ of the gap to $\theta$. Here only the drift depends on $x$, while in the stock model both coefficients do. [[7.7 Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process]] solves this equation and compares it with the stock model.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) One Day of a Stock
>A stock trades at $\$80$ and follows $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t$ with $\mu = 0.10$ and $\sigma = 0.30$ per year. One trading day is $\Delta t = 1/250$ year.
>
>>[!example]- Find the approximate mean and standard deviation of the price change over one day, and the probability that the price falls by more than $\$3$. Then find the same probability for a price of $\$160$.
>>Over one step, $\Delta S \approx \mu S\,\Delta t + \sigma S\,\varepsilon\sqrt{\Delta t}$. The mean is $0.10 \times 80/250 = \$0.032$. The standard deviation is $0.30 \times 80 \times \sqrt{1/250} = \$1.518$. So the probability of a fall of more than $\$3$ is
>>$$\mathbb{P}[\Delta S < -3] \approx N\left(\frac{-3 - 0.032}{1.518}\right) = N(-1.9975) = 0.0229,$$
>>where $N$ is the standard normal CDF, written $\Phi$ in [[2.2c.3 Gaussian Distribution]]. The daily mean is about $2\%$ of the daily standard deviation, so the drift has little effect over one day. A rise of more than $\$3$ has probability $1 - N(1.9553) = 0.0253$, almost the same. At $\$160$ both coefficients double. The mean becomes $\$0.064$ and the standard deviation $\$3.036$, so $\mathbb{P}[\Delta S < -3] \approx N(-1.0093) = 0.1564$. A $\$3$ fall happens about one day in $44$ for the $\$80$ stock and about one day in $6$ for the $\$160$ stock.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The next two examples use the Euler rule. The mean-reverting rate model in the Medium example is also not in the modules.

>[!example] (Medium) Three Quarters of an Interest Rate
>A short-term interest rate $r$, in percentage points, follows $dr = 0.4\,(4 - r)\,dt + 1.0\,dW_t$, with time in years. Today $r_0 = 8$. A simulation uses quarterly steps, $\Delta t = 0.25$, and the draws $\varepsilon_1 = -0.6$, $\varepsilon_2 = 1.4$, and $\varepsilon_3 = 0.2$.
>
>>[!example]- Run three Euler steps. Find the expected rate one quarter after the last step. Compare with a generalized Wiener process whose drift is frozen at its starting value.
>>The drift is $a(r) = 0.4\,(4 - r)$ and the diffusion coefficient is $b = 1.0$. Each step adds $0.4\,(4 - r_k)(0.25) + 1.0 \times \varepsilon_k\sqrt{0.25}$, which is
>>$$\Delta r_k = 0.1\,(4 - r_k) + 0.5\,\varepsilon_k.$$
>>The first term closes $10\%$ of the gap to $4$, and the second is the shock. In the first step the drift is $0.1 \times (4 - 8) = -0.4$ and the shock is $0.5 \times (-0.6) = -0.3$, so $r_1 = 7.3$. In the second step the drift is $0.1 \times (4 - 7.3) = -0.33$ and the shock is $0.7$, so $r_2 = 7.67$. In the third step the drift is $0.1 \times (4 - 7.67) = -0.367$ and the shock is $0.1$, so $r_3 = 7.403$.
>>
>>The expected change over the next quarter is $0.1 \times (4 - 7.403) = -0.3403$, so $\mathbb{E}[r_4 \mid r_3] = 7.0627$. If the rate were at $5$, the expected change would be only $-0.1$. The frozen process keeps the starting drift $a = 0.4 \times (4 - 8) = -1.6$ per year, or $-0.4$ per quarter. With the same draws it reaches $8 - 3 \times 0.4 + 0.5 \times (-0.6 + 1.4 + 0.2) = 7.3$. The state-dependent path ends higher, because its pull weakened as the rate fell. The frozen process would keep falling by $1.6$ per year on average, reach $4$ after $2.5$ years, and continue below it. The state-dependent drift shrinks to $0$ as the rate nears $4$.

>[!example] (Hard) Two Steps Are Not Normal
>A stock trades at $\$100$ and follows $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t$ with $\mu = 0.12$ and $\sigma = 0.40$. A risk desk simulates it with two monthly Euler steps, $\Delta t = 1/12$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the mean and variance of the price after one step and after two steps. Show that the two-step price is not normal. Compare with a generalized Wiener process whose coefficients are frozen at $a = \mu S_0$ and $b = \sigma S_0$.
>>Write $c = 1 + \mu\,\Delta t = 1.01$ and $s = \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t} = 0.11547$. One step gives $S_1 = S_0(c + s\varepsilon_1)$. So $S_1$ is normal with mean $\$101$ and variance $(100 \times 0.11547)^2 = 133.33$, a standard deviation of $\$11.55$. This matches the one-step rule: the mean is $a\,\Delta t = 12/12 = 1$ above $S_0$, and the variance is $b^2\Delta t = 1600/12 = 133.33$.
>>
>>The second step uses the new price, $S_2 = S_1(c + s\varepsilon_2) = S_0(c + s\varepsilon_1)(c + s\varepsilon_2)$. The two factors are independent, each with mean $c$ and second moment $c^2 + s^2$. So the mean and variance after two steps are
>>$$\mathbb{E}[S_2] = S_0c^2 = 102.01, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_2) = S_0^2\big[(c^2 + s^2)^2 - c^4\big] = 273.80.$$
>>The standard deviation is $\$16.55$. The frozen process gives $x_2 = S_0 + 2a\,\Delta t + b\sqrt{\Delta t}\,(\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)$. It is normal with mean $102$ and variance $1600 \times 2/12 = 266.67$, a standard deviation of $\$16.33$.
>>
>>The spread of the second step depends on the first draw. If $\varepsilon_1 = 2$, then $S_1 = \$124.09$ and the second step has standard deviation $0.11547 \times 124.09 = \$14.33$. If $\varepsilon_1 = -2$, then $S_1 = \$77.91$ and the standard deviation is $\$9.00$. So $S_2$ mixes normal distributions of different widths, wider on the high side. The third central moment measures this asymmetry. Write $S_2 - S_0c^2 = S_0\big[cs(\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2) + s^2\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\big]$. Cube it and take expectations. Odd powers of a standard normal draw have mean $0$, so only one term survives:
>>$$\mathbb{E}\big[(S_2 - \mathbb{E}[S_2])^3\big] = 3S_0^3c^2s^4\,\mathbb{E}\big[(\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)^2\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\big] = 6S_0^3c^2s^4 = 1088.11.$$
>>The expectation equals $2$, because $(\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)^2\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2 = \varepsilon_1^3\varepsilon_2 + 2\varepsilon_1^2\varepsilon_2^2 + \varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2^3$ has mean $0 + 2 + 0$. A normal variable has third central moment $0$. Here the skewness, the third central moment divided by the cubed standard deviation, is $1088.11/273.80^{3/2} = 0.2402$. So $S_2$ has a longer right tail and is not normal. The frozen process has skewness $0$ because its shock sizes do not depend on earlier draws.
