>[!note] Applications
>This section applies the consumer model to choices that do not look like buying two goods, and adds one model of the labor market. [[6.5.1 Opportunity Cost]] states the principle behind the first two applications. The cost of a choice is the value of the best alternative given up, whether or not money changes hands. Under this principle an hour of leisure costs the wage $w$, and an owned warehouse can become too costly to keep.
>
>[[6.5.2 Labor Supply]] treats leisure as a good priced at the wage. In the budget $pc + wl \le M + 24w$, the person's 24 hours are valued at the market wage. With Cobb-Douglas utility $c^{\alpha}l^{1-\alpha}$ she chooses $l^* = (1-\alpha)(24 + M/w)$ and works $h^* = 24 - l^*$ hours, unless her non-labor income is high enough that she does not work. [[6.5.3 Intertemporal Choice]] treats consumption when young and when old as two goods. The budget $c_0 + c_1/(1+r) \le M_0 + M_1/(1+r)$ says that the present value of consumption cannot exceed the present value of income. At the optimum, the ratio of marginal utilities $U'(c_0)/U'(c_1)$ equals $(1+r)/(1+\delta)$, the reward for waiting relative to impatience. With the implicit function theorem, the effect of $r$ on $c_0^*$ splits into a substitution term and an income term. In both models the consumer owns an endowment of the good whose price changes, so a price rise makes a net seller richer.
>
>[[6.5.4 Taste-Based Discrimination]] studies an employer who dislikes one of two equally productive workers. A pay gap appears only when workers share the firm's surplus. Under competition, a single non-discriminating firm is enough to remove the gap, and wages fall to the reservation wage.
>
>[[6.5.5 Altruism and Charitable Donations]] shows that a utility maximizer need not be selfish. Wendy maximizes $u(c_W) + \alpha\,u(c_M)$, where $\alpha > 0$ is her weight on Mark's utility, and chooses a donation $D \ge 0$. At an interior optimum $u'(M_W - D^*) = \alpha\,u'(M_M + D^*)$. With $\alpha = 1$ she gives $(M_W - M_M)/2$ when she is richer and nothing otherwise. She gives more when $\alpha$ or $M_W$ rises and less when $M_M$ rises. [[6.5.6 Time Consistency]] extends intertemporal choice to three periods. Under $\beta$-$\delta$ preferences every future period gets an extra discount factor $\beta < 1$. The self at $t = 1$ then wants $U'(c_1)/U'(c_2) = \beta(1+r)/(1+\delta)$, while the self at $t = 0$ planned $U'(c_1)/U'(c_2) = (1+r)/(1+\delta)$. So the person consumes more at $t = 1$ than she planned, and her plan is time inconsistent.

# Prerequisites

- [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]], for the Lagrangian and the tangency condition.
- [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions]], for the case where a person does not work or does not give.
- [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]], for substitution and income effects.
- [[6.4a Cobb-Douglas]], for the fixed-share demands used in both consumer models.
- [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem]], for the comparative statics of saving and of the donation.

# Contents

- [[6.5.1 Opportunity Cost]]: the value of the best alternative given up, the wage as the price of leisure, and accounting versus opportunity cost for an owned site. **Not taught:** sunk costs.
- [[6.5.2 Labor Supply]]: full income, the Cobb-Douglas labor supply, the two effects of a wage rise, and the corner where the person does not work. **Not taught:** the Slutsky equation with a time endowment.
- [[6.5.3 Intertemporal Choice]]: the two-period budget, the discount rate, perfect smoothing when $r = \delta$, and comparative statics in income and in the interest rate.
- [[6.5.4 Taste-Based Discrimination]]: an employer who dislikes one group of workers, with wages under monopsony, a union, and perfect competition.
- [[6.5.5 Altruism and Charitable Donations]]: a donor who values the recipient's utility, the first-order condition for the gift, the corner $D^* = 0$, the equal-weight case $\alpha = 1$, and comparative statics in $\alpha$ and in both incomes. **Not taught:** the corner condition for general $\alpha$, who ends up richer, partial crowd-out, the result that only total income matters, and the log-utility closed form.
- [[6.5.6 Time Consistency]]: exponential versus $\beta$-$\delta$ discounting, the plan of the self at $t = 0$ versus the choice of the self at $t = 1$, and why present bias raises consumption at $t = 1$. **Not taught:** the value of commitment and the price a person would pay for it, naive versus sophisticated agents, and the log-utility closed forms.

# Recommended Path

Read [[6.5.1 Opportunity Cost]] first, since both consumer models that follow price a good by what the person gives up. [[6.5.2 Labor Supply]] and [[6.5.3 Intertemporal Choice]] have the same structure, a consumer with an endowment who trades at a relative price. Read labor supply first because its algebra is lighter. If short on time, read the callouts of these two notes and the Hard example of 6.5.3, which works through the implicit function theorem with numbers. [[6.5.4 Taste-Based Discrimination]] uses no calculus and can be read at any point. [[6.5.5 Altruism and Charitable Donations]] needs only one first-order condition and a corner, so it can follow 6.5.2. Read [[6.5.6 Time Consistency]] right after 6.5.3, since it adds a third period and one parameter $\beta$ to the same model. For Midterm 2, read the callouts of 6.5.5 and 6.5.6 and work their Easy examples.
