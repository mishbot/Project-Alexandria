>[!note] Delta Hedging
>**Delta hedging** removes the risk of an option position by holding shares of the stock and adjusting the holding as the price moves. Consider a node of a [[2.3.3c Binomial Tree|binomial tree]] where the stock price is $S$. One step later the price is $uS$ or $dS$, and the option is worth $f_u$ or $f_d$. The **delta** at the node is the number of shares per option that makes the next step riskless,
>$$\Delta = \frac{f_u - f_d}{uS - dS}.$$
>The cash that goes with it, invested at the risk-free rate $r$ for one step of length $\Delta t$, is
>$$A = e^{-r\Delta t}\,\frac{uS\,f_d - dS\,f_u}{uS - dS}.$$
>These are the formulas of the [[2.3.3a One-Step Binomial Model|one-step model]], applied at each node. The holding $\Delta S + A$ equals the option value $f$ at the node. Both $\Delta$ and $A$ change from node to node, so the holding must be rebalanced at every step. A writer who sells the option and holds these shares and this cash practices **dynamic hedging**. A buyer of shares and cash who uses the same rule copies the option, which is **dynamic replication**. They are the same trades seen from opposite sides.
>
>Each rebalancing is **self-financing**. The cash account pays for any shares bought and receives the proceeds of any shares sold. No money is added or withdrawn after the start. Along every path through the tree, the strategy ends with exactly the option's payoff. So the present value of the cost of hedging equals the option's fair price. In continuous time the delta becomes $\Delta = \partial f/\partial S$, the hedge ratio behind the [[2.3.3e Black-Scholes-Merton Equation|Black-Scholes-Merton equation]].

# Intuition

A writer who sells a call loses money if the stock rises. Holding shares offsets that loss. The right number of shares depends on how strongly the call responds to the stock, and that response changes as the stock moves. A call that is far in the money moves almost one for one with the stock. A call that is far out of the money barely moves. A hedge set once at the start is therefore wrong after the first step. The writer must trade at every node to keep the position riskless.

The tree shows a one-year call with strike $\$58$ on a stock at $\$60$, with six-month steps, $u = 1.2$, $d = 0.85$, and $r = 6\%$. The (Medium) example follows the hedge through it.

# Properties

#### *(Value Is Preserved at Each Node)* The holding that arrives at a node is worth exactly the option value there, so switching to the new holding costs nothing.

The shares and cash chosen at the previous node replicate the option over one step. So at the up node they are worth $\Delta_0 \cdot uS + A_0 e^{r\Delta t} = f_u$, and at the down node they are worth $f_d$. The new holding at the up node is also worth $\Delta_u \cdot uS + A_u = f_u$. Two holdings with the same value can be exchanged by trading shares against cash, with nothing left over. This is the reason the strategy is self-financing. The derivation below checks it with the explicit formulas.

#### *(Hedging Cost Equals the Price)* When every trade is funded from one loan account at $r$, the hedging cost at expiry is the same on every path.

The writer borrows at $r$ for every share purchase and credits every sale to the loan. At expiry the writer holds shares, owes the option payoff, and has a loan balance. The hedging cost at $T$ is the loan balance minus the shares' value plus the payoff owed. The replicating portfolio needs money only at $t = 0$, where it costs $f$. So the hedging cost at $T$ is $fe^{rT}$ on every path, and its present value is $f$. A writer who sells the option for more than $f$ keeps the difference with no risk. This is the same arbitrage as in the one-step model, now carried out over many steps.

#### *(Replication and Hedging Are One Strategy)* A hedged writer's net position costs nothing at the start and is worth nothing at expiry.

A buyer who replicates the option holds $\Delta$ shares and cash $A$ at each node, at a starting cost of $f$. A writer who hedges is short one option and holds the same $\Delta$ shares and cash $A$. The writer's starting cost is $\Delta S_0 + A_0 - f = 0$, since the premium pays for the hedge. At expiry the replicating portfolio is worth the payoff, so the writer's position is worth exactly zero. A dealer who sells an option and hedges it is manufacturing the option for the buyer.

#### *(Delta Moves With the Stock)* A hedger buys shares after the stock rises and sells after it falls, for calls and for puts.

For a call, $\Delta$ lies between $0$ and $1$ and rises with the stock price. In the tree above it goes from $0.6721$ to $1$ after a rise and to $0.1793$ after a fall. For a put, $\Delta$ lies between $-1$ and $0$. After a rise the put's delta moves toward $0$, so the hedger buys back part of a short position. After a fall it moves toward $-1$, so the hedger sells more shares short. In both cases the writer buys after rises and sells after falls. Near expiry, delta moves toward $0$ or $\pm 1$ as the option becomes nearly sure to expire worthless or in the money.

#### *(Continuous-Time Limit)* As the step length shrinks, the delta becomes the derivative $\partial f / \partial S$.

The delta is the change in option value divided by the change in stock price over one step. As $\Delta t \to 0$, the two later prices $uS$ and $dS$ move toward $S$, and the ratio becomes the [[2.3. Partial Derivatives|partial derivative]] $\partial f/\partial S$. In continuous time the writer holds $\partial f/\partial S$ shares per option and rebalances continuously. That choice cancels the random term in the change of the hedged position, which leads to the [[2.3.3e Black-Scholes-Merton Equation]]. Real hedgers rebalance at discrete times, so their hedge is close to riskless but not exactly riskless.

# Derivation

We want to show that rebalancing at the up node needs no new money. The notation follows the two-step tree. The root has stock price $S$, the next prices are $S_u = uS$ and $S_d = dS$, and the final prices are $S_{uu}$, $S_{ud}$, and $S_{dd}$. The option values carry the same subscripts.

At the root the holding is $\Delta_0 = (f_u - f_d)/(S_u - S_d)$ shares and cash $A_0 = e^{-r\Delta t}(S_u f_d - S_d f_u)/(S_u - S_d)$. At the up node the hedge moves to $\Delta_u = (f_{uu} - f_{ud})/(S_{uu} - S_{ud})$ shares. The cash has grown to $A_0 e^{r\Delta t}$. Selling $\Delta_0 - \Delta_u$ shares at $S_u$ adds $(\Delta_0 - \Delta_u)S_u$ to it. A negative amount means shares are bought. The new cash balance is
$$(\Delta_0 - \Delta_u)S_u + A_0 e^{r\Delta t} = \frac{f_u - f_d}{S_u - S_d}\,S_u + \frac{S_u f_d - S_d f_u}{S_u - S_d} - \Delta_u S_u.$$
The factors $e^{-r\Delta t}$ and $e^{r\Delta t}$ cancel in the second term. Combine the first two terms over the common denominator:
$$\frac{S_u f_u - S_u f_d + S_u f_d - S_d f_u}{S_u - S_d} = \frac{f_u (S_u - S_d)}{S_u - S_d} = f_u.$$
So the new cash balance is $f_u - \Delta_u S_u$. Replication at the up node requires $\Delta_u S_u + A_u = f_u$, so this balance is exactly
$$A_u = e^{-r\Delta t}\,\frac{S_{uu} f_{ud} - S_{ud} f_{uu}}{S_{uu} - S_{ud}}.$$
This is the cash the tree prescribes at the up node. No money was added or taken out. The same steps at the down node give $A_d$. At expiry, $\Delta_u S_{uu} + A_u e^{r\Delta t} = f_{uu}$ and $\Delta_u S_{ud} + A_u e^{r\Delta t} = f_{ud}$ by the choice of $\Delta_u$ and $A_u$, so the final holding pays the option's payoff.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) One Rebalancing Decision
>A desk has written $1{,}000$ six-month calls with strike $\$50$ on a stock at $\$50$. It hedges on a tree with three-month steps, $u = 1.1$, $d = 0.9$, and $r = 4\%$. At the start it holds $572.015$ shares, a delta of $0.5720$ per call, and has borrowed $\$25{,}484.53$. Three months later the stock is at $\$55$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the new delta, the trade the desk must make, and how it pays for the trade.
>>From $\$55$, the stock ends at $\$60.50$ or $\$49.50$. Each call then pays $\$10.50$ or $0$. The new delta is
>>$$\Delta_u = \frac{10.50 - 0}{60.50 - 49.50} = 0.9545.$$
>>For $1{,}000$ calls the desk needs $954.545$ shares, so it buys $382.531$ shares at $\$55$ for $\$21{,}039.20$.
>>
>>It borrows the money. The old loan has grown to $25{,}484.53\,e^{0.04 \times 0.25} = \$25{,}740.65$. The new balance is $25{,}740.65 + 21{,}039.20 = \$46{,}779.85$. The desk adds no money of its own. As a check, the shares minus the loan are worth $954.545 \times 55 - 46{,}779.85 = 52{,}500.00 - 46{,}779.85 = \$5{,}720.15$. That is $1{,}000$ times the call value of $\$5.72$ at this node.

>[!example] (Medium) The Writer's Profit and Loss on Every Path
>A dealer writes $100$ one-year European calls with strike $\$58$ on a stock at $\$60$ and hedges them on the tree in the figure: six-month steps, $u = 1.2$, $d = 0.85$, and $r = 6\%$. The fair price is $\$8.62$ per call, and the dealer sells at that price for $\$861.52$ in total.
>
>>[!example]- Follow the hedge along each path, show that the dealer ends with zero profit or loss, and show that the present value of the hedging cost equals the price of the calls.
>>At $t = 0$ the delta is $0.6721$, so the dealer buys $67.205$ shares for $\$4{,}032.30$. The premium pays $\$861.52$, and the dealer borrows the other $\$3{,}170.78$. After six months the loan has grown to $3{,}170.78\,e^{0.03} = \$3{,}267.34$.
>>
>>If the stock rises to $\$72$, the shares are worth $\$4{,}838.76$. Net of the loan, the position is worth $\$1{,}571.42$, which is $100$ times the call value of $\$15.71$ at this node. The new delta is $1$, so the dealer buys $32.795$ shares for $\$2{,}361.24$ and the loan becomes $\$5{,}628.58$. At expiry the loan has grown to $\$5{,}800.00$. If the stock ends at $\$86.40$, the dealer's $100$ shares are worth $\$8{,}640.00$, the calls cost $\$2{,}840.00$, and the loan takes $\$5{,}800.00$. The net is $0$. If it ends at $\$61.20$, the net is $6{,}120.00 - 320.00 - 5{,}800.00 = 0$.
>>
>>If the stock falls to $\$51$, the shares are worth $\$3{,}427.46$. Net of the same loan, the position is worth $\$160.11$, which is $100$ times the call value of $\$1.60$. The new delta is $0.1793$, so the dealer sells $49.278$ shares for $\$2{,}513.17$ and the loan falls to $\$754.17$. At expiry the loan is $\$777.14$. At $\$61.20$ the dealer's $17.927$ shares are worth $\$1{,}097.14$, the calls cost $\$320.00$, and the loan takes $\$777.14$, for a net of $0$. At $\$43.35$ the shares are worth $\$777.14$ and the calls expire, so the net is again $0$.
>>
>>Now count only the hedge, without the premium. Along the up path, the share purchases with interest to expiry cost $4{,}032.30\,e^{0.06} + 2{,}361.24\,e^{0.03} = 4{,}281.64 + 2{,}433.15 = \$6{,}714.79$. The final holding of $100$ shares minus the call payoffs is worth $\$5{,}800.00$ in both states. The hedging cost at expiry is $\$914.79$. Along the down path the sale returns $2{,}513.17\,e^{0.03} = \$2{,}589.71$ and the final holding is worth $\$777.14$, so the cost is $4{,}281.64 - 2{,}589.71 - 777.14 = \$914.79$, the same. Its present value is $914.79\,e^{-0.06} = \$861.52$, the price of the $100$ calls.

>[!example] (Hard) Self-Financing in Every State
>A trader replicates $100$ one-year European puts with strike $\$42$ on a stock at $\$40$. The tree has six-month steps, $u = 1.25$, $d = 0.8$, and $r = 5\%$. The final stock prices are $\$62.50$, $\$40$, and $\$25.60$, and each put pays $0$, $\$2$, or $\$16.40$. Backward induction gives a put value of $\$0.974$ at the $\$50$ node, $\$8.963$ at the $\$32$ node, and $\$4.84$ at the root.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the replicating holdings, show that rebalancing at both nodes after the first step needs no new money, and show that the final portfolio equals the put payoff in all four end states.
>>At the root, $\Delta_0 = (0.974 - 8.963)/(50 - 32) = -0.4438$ and $A_0 = \$22.59$ per put. For $100$ puts the trader shorts $44.384$ shares for $\$1{,}775.35$ and holds $\$2{,}259.38$ in cash. The net cost is $2{,}259.38 - 1{,}775.35 = \$484.03$, which is $100$ times the put price. After six months the cash has grown to $2{,}259.38\,e^{0.025} = \$2{,}316.58$.
>>
>>At the $\$50$ node, closing the short would cost $\$2{,}219.19$, so the old holding is worth $2{,}316.58 - 2{,}219.19 = \$97.39$. That is $100$ times the put value there. The new delta is $(0 - 2)/(62.50 - 40) = -0.0889$. The trader buys back $35.495$ shares for $\$1{,}774.74$, leaving $2{,}316.58 - 1{,}774.74 = \$541.84$ in cash. The tree's formula gives $100A_u = \$541.84$, so no money was added.
>>
>>At the $\$32$ node, closing the short would cost $\$1{,}420.28$, so the old holding is worth $\$896.30$, again $100$ times the put value. The new delta is $(2 - 16.40)/(40 - 25.60) = -1$. The trader shorts $55.616$ more shares for $\$1{,}779.72$, raising the cash to $2{,}316.58 + 1{,}779.72 = \$4{,}096.30$. The formula gives $100A_d = \$4{,}096.30$.
>>
>>Over the last six months the cash grows to $\$555.56$ on the up path and $\$4{,}200.00$ on the down path. The four end states are:
>>
>>| Path | Final price | Short shares | Cash | Total | Payoff of 100 puts |
>>|---|---|---|---|---|---|
>>| up, up | $\$62.50$ | $-\$555.56$ | $\$555.56$ | $0$ | $0$ |
>>| up, down | $\$40.00$ | $-\$355.56$ | $\$555.56$ | $\$200.00$ | $\$200.00$ |
>>| down, up | $\$40.00$ | $-\$4{,}000.00$ | $\$4{,}200.00$ | $\$200.00$ | $\$200.00$ |
>>| down, down | $\$25.60$ | $-\$2{,}560.00$ | $\$4{,}200.00$ | $\$1{,}640.00$ | $\$1{,}640.00$ |
>>
>>The two middle paths end at the same price with different holdings, and both pay $\$200$. The portfolio matches the payoff in every state after one payment of $\$484.03$ at the start.
