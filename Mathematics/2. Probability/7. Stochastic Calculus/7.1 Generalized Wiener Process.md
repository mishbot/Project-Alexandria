>[!note] Generalized Wiener Process
>A **generalized Wiener process** is a quantity that moves at a steady expected rate while independent random shocks push it up and down. The total variance of the shocks grows in proportion to the elapsed time. Its random part is the **Wiener process** $W_t$. It starts at $W_0 = 0$, and over a short time $\Delta t$ it changes by
>$$\Delta W = \varepsilon\sqrt{\Delta t}, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1),$$
>with a new, independent draw of $\varepsilon$ in each step. So each step has mean $\mathbb{E}[\Delta W] = 0$ and variance $\mathbb{E}[(\Delta W)^2] = \Delta t$, and steps over different time intervals are independent. Adding the steps gives $W_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t)$. More generally, $W_t - W_s \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t - s)$ for $s < t$, independent of the path up to time $s$. This is the process written $B_t$ in [[4.6 Brownian Motion]], which lists its formal axioms.
>
>The generalized Wiener process $x$ adds a constant trend $a$ and a constant scale $b$ to $W$. In differential form and in solved form,
>$$dx = a\,dt + b\,dW_t, \qquad x_t = x_0 + at + bW_t.$$
>The constant $a$ is the **drift rate**, the expected change in $x$ per unit time. The constant $b^2$ is the **variance rate**, the variance of the change in $x$ per unit time. Over any horizon $T$ the change in $x$ is normal:
>$$x_T - x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(aT,\ b^2T).$$
>The mean grows like $T$, and the standard deviation $b\sqrt{T}$ grows like $\sqrt{T}$. So the noise dominates over short horizons and the trend dominates over long ones. The process is also called Brownian motion with drift, or arithmetic Brownian motion. It can take any real value, including negative ones. An [[7.3 Ito Process|Itô process]] lets $a$ and $b$ depend on the current value and on time. [[7.2 Quadratic Variation]] shows that the squared steps of $x$ add up to $b^2T$. [[7.6 Geometric Brownian Motion]] shows that the log of a stock price is a generalized Wiener process.

# Intuition

A firm's cash balance grows by about $\$1.2$ million per year from its operations. It does not grow smoothly. Customers pay late, and machines break. Each shock is small and hard to predict, and there are many of them. A model of the balance needs a steady trend plus the sum of many small independent shocks. The trend is easy to write as $1.2t$. The shocks need more care.

Build the shocks from coin flips. Split one year into $n$ steps of length $\Delta t = 1/n$. In each step, move up or down by the same amount $h$, with probability $1/2$ each. The steps are independent, so after one year the variance is $nh^2$. With four quarterly steps of size $h = 0.5$, the variance is $4 \times 0.25 = 1$. Now refine the grid to $16$ steps. If each step keeps the size $0.5$, the variance becomes $4$. If the step shrinks in proportion to its length, to $h = 1/16$, the variance becomes $16/256 = 0.0625$, and the shocks vanish as the grid gets finer. Only $h = \sqrt{1/16} = 0.25$ keeps the variance at $1$. In general $h = \sqrt{\Delta t}$ gives variance $n\,\Delta t = 1$ for every $n$. So the size of a step must be the square root of its length.

Replacing each coin flip by a standard normal draw $\varepsilon$ gives the Wiener process, with steps $\Delta W = \varepsilon\sqrt{\Delta t}$. Its value after time $t$ is a sum of independent normal steps with total variance $t$. So it is normal with mean $0$ and variance $t$. Changes over different periods use different draws, so they are independent.

The cash balance needs two more pieces. Its shocks need not have variance exactly $1$ per year, so the Wiener step is multiplied by a scale $b$. Its trend adds $a\,\Delta t$ in every step. Each step then changes the balance by $\Delta x = a\,\Delta t + b\,\varepsilon\sqrt{\Delta t}$. The word "generalized" refers to these two additions, a drift and a scale.

Each step of the construction gives one piece of the distribution. The $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ step size makes the variance of $W_t$ equal to the elapsed time $t$. The scale $b$ turns that variance into $b^2t$. The trend contributes the mean $at$. Together they give $x_t - x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(at, b^2t)$.

# Properties

#### *(The $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ Scaling)* Among all power laws for the step size, only the square root keeps the total variance finite and nonzero.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules define the step $\varepsilon\sqrt{\Delta t}$ without arguing why the power must be $1/2$.

Split $[0, t]$ into $n$ steps of length $\Delta t = t/n$, and let each independent step have standard deviation $(\Delta t)^{\alpha}$ for some power $\alpha$. The variances add to $n(\Delta t)^{2\alpha} = t\,(\Delta t)^{2\alpha - 1}$. For $\alpha > 1/2$ this goes to $0$ as $\Delta t \to 0$, so the randomness disappears in the limit. For $\alpha < 1/2$ it grows without bound. Only $\alpha = 1/2$ gives variance $t$ on every grid. The [[2.3.3c Binomial Tree|binomial tree]] uses the same scaling. With $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ and $d = 1/u$, the log price moves by $\pm\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}$ in each step. The scaling also makes the path rough. For small $\Delta t$ a step of size $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ is much larger than $\Delta t$, so the ratio $\Delta W/\Delta t$ has no limit. [[7.2 Quadratic Variation]] works out what this means for sums of squared steps.

#### *(Exact and Approximate Normality)* With normal steps, $W_t$ is exactly normal on every grid; with coin-flip steps, it is normal only in the limit.

The derivation below writes $W_t$ as a sum of $n$ independent normal steps. A sum of independent normal variables is normal, so $W_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t)$ holds for every $n$, and no limit is needed. The [[3.3 Central Limit Theorem|central limit theorem]] matters when the steps are not normal. In a [[4.4 Random Walks|random walk]] with steps of $\pm\sqrt{\Delta t}$, the sum has mean $0$ and variance $t$ for every $n$, but its shape is not normal. With four steps over one year it takes only the five values $-1$, $-0.5$, $0$, $0.5$, and $1$. As $n$ grows the possible values fill in, and the distribution approaches $\mathcal{N}(0, t)$. The same holds for any independent, identically distributed steps with mean $0$ and variance $\Delta t$. So the generalized Wiener process fits any quantity that adds up many small independent shocks, whatever the shape of each shock.

#### *(Trend Versus Noise)* The trend $aT$ exceeds one standard deviation of the noise once $T$ exceeds $b^2/a^2$.

The ratio of trend to noise is $aT/(b\sqrt{T}) = (a/b)\sqrt{T}$, which grows like $\sqrt{T}$. The trend equals one standard deviation at $T = b^2/a^2$ and two standard deviations at $T = 4b^2/a^2$. For the figure's values, $a = 1.2$ and $b = 1$, these horizons are $0.69$ and $2.78$ years. The probability of ending below the start is
$$\mathbb{P}[x_T < x_0] = N\left(-\frac{a}{b}\sqrt{T}\right),$$
where $N$ is the standard normal CDF, written $\Phi$ in [[2.2c.3 Gaussian Distribution]]. For the figure's values it is $0.2743$ after three months, $0.1151$ after one year, and $0.0082$ after four years. The same ratio limits what data can say about the drift. The estimate $(x_T - x_0)/T$ of $a$ has standard deviation $b/\sqrt{T}$. With $a = 1.2$ and $b = 1$, it takes $2.78$ years of data before the trend is two standard deviations away from zero.

#### *(Only the Current Value Matters)* A forecast of $x$ uses today's value and the length of the horizon, and nothing else from the path.

Write $x_{t+h} = x_t + ah + b\,(W_{t+h} - W_t)$. The increment $W_{t+h} - W_t$ is independent of the path up to time $t$. So given that path, the future value is normal:
$$x_{t+h} \sim \mathcal{N}(x_t + ah,\ b^2h).$$
The forecast mean is today's value plus the trend over the horizon $h$. The forecast variance grows in proportion to $h$ and does not depend on the past. An earlier high or low point changes nothing once $x_t$ is known. As time passes, the forecast of a fixed future value $x_T$ moves with $x_t$, and its variance $b^2(T - t)$ shrinks steadily to $0$. This is the Markov property for this process. [[7.3 Ito Process]] states it for processes with state-dependent coefficients.

#### *(Changing Time Units)* Measuring time in shorter periods divides the drift rate and the variance rate by the number of periods per year, and the scale $b$ by its square root.

Let one year contain $m$ shorter periods, for example $m = 250$ trading days or $m = 12$ months. Over one period the change in $x$ is normal with mean $a/m$ and variance $b^2/m$. So with time measured in periods, the drift rate is $a/m$ and the variance rate is $b^2/m$. Both scale by $1/m$, because means and variances both add over independent periods. The scale $b$ is a standard deviation, so it scales by $1/\sqrt{m}$. For the figure's values, $a = 1.2$ and $b = 1$ per year become $a = 0.1$ and $b = 0.2887$ per month. The same rule annualizes volatility in [[3.1f Historical Volatility]]. A daily standard deviation $s$ corresponds to $s\sqrt{250}$ per year.

# Derivation

We want the distribution of $W_t$ and of $x_t$. Split $[0, t]$ into $n$ steps of length $\Delta t = t/n$. Write $W_t - W_0$ as a telescoping sum of the steps, in which all intermediate values cancel:
$$W_t - W_0 = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\big(W_{(i+1)\Delta t} - W_{i\Delta t}\big) = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\varepsilon_i\sqrt{\Delta t}.$$
Here $\varepsilon_i$ is the standard normal draw of step $i$. Different steps are independent, so $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i\varepsilon_j] = 0$ for $i \neq j$. Taking expectations term by term gives $\mathbb{E}[W_t - W_0] = 0$. The variance of a sum of independent terms is the sum of the variances, so $\operatorname{Var}(W_t - W_0) = n\,\Delta t = t$. A sum of independent normal variables is normal. With $W_0 = 0$ this gives
$$W_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t), \qquad \mathbb{E}[W_t] = 0, \qquad \mathbb{E}[W_t^2] = t.$$
So the variance of $W_t$ equals the elapsed time. The same sum over the steps between times $s$ and $t$ gives $W_t - W_s \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t - s)$. That sum uses only the draws after $s$, so it is independent of the path up to $s$. For the generalized Wiener process, summing $\Delta x = a\,\Delta t + b\,\Delta W$ over the $n$ steps gives
$$x_t - x_0 = at + bW_t \sim \mathcal{N}(at,\ b^2t).$$
The trends add up to $n\,a\,\Delta t = at$, and the constant $b$ multiplies the standard deviation of $W_t$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Cash Balance With Drift
>A firm's cash balance, in millions of dollars, follows a generalized Wiener process with drift rate $a = 0.5$ and scale $b = 2$, with time in years. Today the balance is $x_0 = 3$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the distribution of the balance in one year and in four years, and the probability that it is negative at each date.
>>After one year, $x_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(3 + 0.5,\ 2^2 \times 1) = \mathcal{N}(3.5, 4)$, with standard deviation $2$. So
>>$$\mathbb{P}[x_1 < 0] = N\left(\frac{0 - 3.5}{2}\right) = N(-1.75) = 0.0401.$$
>>Here $N$ is the standard normal CDF. After four years, $x_4 \sim \mathcal{N}(3 + 2,\ 2^2 \times 4) = \mathcal{N}(5, 16)$, with standard deviation $4$. Then $\mathbb{P}[x_4 < 0] = N(-1.25) = 0.1056$. The expected balance is $\$1.5$ million higher at four years than at one year. The standard deviation doubles, and the chance of a negative balance more than doubles. Over longer horizons the trend dominates. After $16$ years, $x_{16} \sim \mathcal{N}(11, 64)$, and $\mathbb{P}[x_{16} < 0] = N(-1.375) = 0.0846$.

>[!example] (Medium) A Stock Above Its Starting Price
>The log of a stock price, $x_t = \ln S_t$, follows a generalized Wiener process with $a = 0.08$ and $b = 0.20$ per year. A year has $250$ trading days.
>
>>[!example]- Find the drift rate and the scale per trading day. Find the probability that the stock ends above today's price after one day, one year, and ten years. Then find the horizon at which the trend equals two standard deviations.
>>Per day, the drift rate is $0.08/250 = 0.00032$ and the scale is $0.20/\sqrt{250} = 0.01265$. The stock ends above today's price when $x_T > x_0$. Since $x_T - x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(aT, b^2T)$,
>>$$\mathbb{P}[S_T > S_0] = N\left(\frac{a}{b}\sqrt{T}\right).$$
>>The ratio $a/b = 0.4$ is the trend per unit of noise over one year. Over one day the probability is $N(0.00032/0.01265) = N(0.0253) = 0.5101$. Over one year it is $N(0.4) = 0.6554$. Over ten years it is $N(1.2649) = 0.8970$. The trend equals two standard deviations when $T = 4b^2/a^2 = 4 \times 0.04/0.0064 = 25$ years. On a single day the stock rises almost exactly half the time. So a daily record shows mostly noise, even for a stock with a solid expected return.

>[!example] (Hard) Updating a Forecast
>The spread between two interest rates, in percentage points, follows a generalized Wiener process with $x_0 = 0$, $a = 0.4$, and $b = 1.2$, with time in years. After two years the spread is $x_2 = 1.5$. During the first year it fell as low as $-0.8$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the distribution of $x_5$ given the path up to year two, and the probability that $x_5 < 0$. Compare with the forecast made at time $0$.
>>The changes after year two depend on $W_5 - W_2$, which is independent of the path before year two. So only $x_2$ matters, and the low point of $-0.8$ adds no information. Write
>>$$x_5 = x_2 + 3a + b\,(W_5 - W_2), \qquad W_5 - W_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 3).$$
>>The three years left contribute a trend of $3a = 1.2$ and noise with variance $3b^2$. Given the path, $x_5$ is normal with mean $1.5 + 1.2 = 2.7$ and variance $1.44 \times 3 = 4.32$, a standard deviation of $2.078$. Then
>>$$\mathbb{P}[x_5 < 0 \mid \text{path to year } 2] = N\left(-\frac{2.7}{2.078}\right) = N(-1.2990) = 0.0970.$$
>>This is the chance that the noise of the next three years pushes the spread below zero. At time $0$ the forecast was $x_5 \sim \mathcal{N}(0.4 \times 5,\ 1.44 \times 5) = \mathcal{N}(2.0, 7.2)$, with standard deviation $2.683$. It gave $\mathbb{P}[x_5 < 0] = N(-0.7454) = 0.2280$. The observed $x_2 = 1.5$ is above its expected value of $0.8$, so the forecast mean rises from $2.0$ to $2.7$. Only three years of noise remain, so the variance falls from $7.2$ to $4.32$. Both changes lower the probability of a negative spread.
