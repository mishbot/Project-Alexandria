>[!note] Behavioral Corporate Finance
>**Behavioral corporate finance** studies how systematic deviations from the rational-decision-maker benchmark — either by investors or by managers — shape corporate financial policy. The field is organized around two perspectives that ask which side of the corporate-investor relationship is irrational:
>
>**Perspective 1 — Rational firms in inefficient markets.** Investors are subject to behavioral biases (sentiment, extrapolation, categorical thinking). Managers respond rationally to the resulting mispricings. The classic implication is *market timing*: rational managers issue equity when their stock is overvalued and repurchase when it is undervalued (Stein, $1996$). This perspective explains issuance patterns, dividend initiations, mergers, and certain investment-policy patterns as rational managerial responses to non-rational investor behavior.
>
>**Perspective 2 — Irrational managers in (possibly) rational markets.** Investors price securities correctly, but managers are subject to systematic biases — overconfidence, over-optimism, reference dependence — that distort corporate decisions. The classic implication is over-investment: overconfident managers undertake projects with negative true NPV because they overestimate their own ability or their firm's prospects (Malmendier-Tate, $2005$). This perspective explains investment-cash-flow sensitivity, capital-structure choices, and merger overpayment.
>
>**Two key ingredients for Perspective 1 to operate.** Rational managers can profitably exploit mispricings only if (i) the market is in fact mispriced — requiring [[8.4. Noise|limits to arbitrage]] — and (ii) managers can respond more aggressively than arbitrageurs can, which holds because managers have an informational advantage about their own firm and face fewer constraints on issuance than arbitrageurs face on shorting.
>
>**The empirical reach.** Both perspectives generate predictions about every major corporate-finance decision: capital-structure choice, dividend policy, real-investment policy, mergers and acquisitions, executive compensation. The chapter walks through the empirical evidence for each:
>- **[[8.13.1. Market Timing of Security Issuance|Market timing]]** — issuance and repurchase respond to over- and undervaluation (Loughran-Ritter $1995$; Baker-Wurgler $2000$). Junk-debt issuance and long-term debt issuance also exhibit market timing (Greenwood-Hanson $2013$; Baker-Greenwood-Wurgler $2003$).
>- **[[8.13.2. Catering Theory of Dividends|Catering]]** — firms initiate dividends when investor sentiment for dividend payers is high (Baker-Wurgler $2004$).
>- **[[8.13.3. Investment Policy and Equity Dependence|Investment policy]]** — investment of equity-dependent firms responds to stock-price fluctuations more strongly than that of unconstrained firms (Baker-Stein-Wurgler $2003$); four channels make sentiment affect real investment.
>- **[[8.13.4. Sentiment-Driven Mergers|Mergers]]** — misvaluation drives merger waves (Shleifer-Vishny $2003$); investor reference points shape bid offers (Baker-Pan-Wurgler $2011$ — $52$-week-high anchoring).
>- **[[8.13.5. Managerial Over-Optimism|Over-optimism]]** — Malmendier-Tate ($2005$) construct option-exercise-based proxies for CEO over-optimism (Holder67, Longholder, NetBuyer) and document over-investment, pecking-order financing, and elevated investment-cash-flow sensitivity.
>- **[[8.13.6. Managerial Overconfidence and Miscalibration|Overconfidence and miscalibration]]** — Ben-David-Graham-Harvey ($2013$) document that CFO confidence intervals on the S&P $500$ are far too narrow.
>- **[[8.13.7. Single Firm-Wide Discount Rate|Single firm-wide discount rate]]** — Kruger-Landier-Thesmar ($2015$) show CEOs use one discount rate for the whole firm rather than project-specific rates, generating over-investment in high-beta divisions and under-investment in low-beta ones.

# Intuition

The corporate-finance benchmark — Modigliani-Miller plus the standard NPV rule — assumes managers maximize true firm value, investors price securities correctly, and there are no informational frictions binding either side. Behavioral corporate finance relaxes one or both of those assumptions and asks which corporate-policy patterns become understandable as a result.

The two perspectives are not in direct competition. They explain different sets of facts. Market-timing patterns (more equity issuance after stock-price run-ups, more buybacks after declines, dividend initiation when investors love dividends) are most naturally explained by Perspective 1, with rational managers reading sentiment off the market. Over-investment patterns (undertaking negative-NPV projects, over-paying for acquisitions, refusing to issue equity even when it would fund value-creating investment) are most naturally explained by Perspective 2, with biased managers acting as if their firm is more valuable than the market believes. The two perspectives can also coexist within a single firm: the same CEO may be overconfident about her own firm while accurately reading aggregate-sentiment signals from the market.

The Malmendier-Tate ($2005$) program is the empirical centerpiece of Perspective 2. The challenge in testing managerial-bias models is constructing observable proxies for unobservable cognitive states. Malmendier and Tate's solution exploits a clean theoretical fact: a risk-averse, undiversified CEO holding in-the-money company stock options should exercise them well before expiration to diversify her wealth. A CEO who fails to exercise such options — the *Holder67* and *Longholder* indicators — reveals overconfidence about her firm's prospects. The paper's predictions (over-investment, pecking-order financing, investment-cash-flow sensitivity) are then tested with these empirical-theory-grounded proxies.

# Children

- **[[8.13.1. Market Timing of Security Issuance]]** — Stein ($1996$); Loughran-Ritter ($1995$); Baker-Wurgler ($2000$); Greenwood-Hanson ($2013$). Equity, junk-debt, and long-term-debt issuance time aggregate misvaluation.
- **[[8.13.2. Catering Theory of Dividends]]** — Baker-Wurgler ($2004$). Firms initiate dividends when the dividend premium (market-to-book of dividend-payers minus non-payers) is high. Cross-references [[8.14.4. Behavioral Theories of Dividends]].
- **[[8.13.3. Investment Policy and Equity Dependence]]** — Baker-Stein-Wurgler ($2003$). Four channels — equity dependence, investor pressure, agency, managerial wavering — through which investor sentiment can distort real investment.
- **[[8.13.4. Sentiment-Driven Mergers]]** — Shleifer-Vishny ($2003$); Dong-Hirshleifer-Richardson-Teoh ($2006$); Baker-Pan-Wurgler ($2011$). Misvaluation as a driver of merger waves; $52$-week-high reference points in bid premia.
- **[[8.13.5. Managerial Over-Optimism]]** — Malmendier-Tate ($2005$). Holder67, Longholder, NetBuyer proxies for CEO overconfidence; predictions of over-investment, pecking-order financing, and investment-cash-flow sensitivity.
- **[[8.13.6. Managerial Overconfidence and Miscalibration]]** — Ben-David-Graham-Harvey ($2013$). CFO confidence intervals on S&P $500$ returns are far too narrow; miscalibration correlates with leverage and investment.
- **[[8.13.7. Single Firm-Wide Discount Rate]]** — Kruger-Landier-Thesmar ($2015$). CEOs apply one firm-wide discount rate across divisions, over-investing in high-beta divisions and under-investing in low-beta ones.
