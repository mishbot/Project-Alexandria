>[!note] Uniform Circular Motion
>An object that moves in a circle at constant speed $\vec{v}$ is said to experience **uniform circular motion**. The *magnitude* of the velocity remains constant in this case, but the *direction* of the velocity continuously changes as the object moves around the circle. Thus, an object revolving in a circle is continuously accelerating, even when the speed remains constant: 
>```tikz
>\begin{document}
>
>\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=3, rotate=60]
>
>    % Draw the circle (part of it)
>    \draw[thick] (1.5,1.25) arc (42:-3:2cm);
>
>    % Points A and B on the circle
>    \node[fill=black,circle,inner sep=1.5pt,label=above right:{B}] at (2,0) {};
>    \node[fill=black,circle,inner sep=1.5pt,label=above:{A}] at ({2*cos(30)},{2*sin(30)}) {};
>
>    % Center of the circle C
>    \node[fill=black,circle,inner sep=1.5pt,label=below:C] at (0,0) {};
>
>    % Draw radii
>    \draw[thick] (0,0) -- (2,0) node[midway,right] {$r$}; % Radius to A
>    \draw[thick] (0,0) -- ({2*cos(30)},{2*sin(30)}) node[midway, left] {$r$}; % Radius to B
>
>    % Arc and angle
>    \draw[->] (1,0) arc[start angle=0,end angle=30,radius=1] node[midway, below]{$\Delta \theta$};
>
>    % Tangential displacement arc (delta l)
>    \draw[dashed,thick] (2,0) -- ({2*cos(30)},{2*sin(30)}) node[pos=0.25, below] {$\Delta \ell$};
>
>    % Velocity vectors
>    \draw[->,thick,green] (1.55,1.25) -- +(0.35,-0.5) node[right] {$\vec{v}_1$}; % Velocity at A
>    \draw[->,thick,green] (2.025, 0.25) -- +(0.025, -0.65) node[right] {$\vec{v}_2$}; % Velocity at B
>
>	% Downward acceleration
>	\draw[->,thick,orange] (1.925,0.5) -- +(-0.45,-0.125) node[below] {$\vec{a} = \lim\limits_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{\Delta \vec{v}}{\Delta t}$};
>
>\end{tikzpicture}
>
>\end{document}
>```
>Mathematically, the acceleration of an object in uniform circular motion is expressed as
>$$a_R = \frac{v^2}{r},$$
>where $r$ is the radius of curvature of the curve.
>>[!warning] Note!
>>1. In uniform circular motion, the speed is constant, but the acceleration is not zero.
>>2. Since $a_R$ is directed towards the center of the circle at all times, its **centripetal force** $\sum F_R = ma_R$ must also be directed towards the center of the circle.
