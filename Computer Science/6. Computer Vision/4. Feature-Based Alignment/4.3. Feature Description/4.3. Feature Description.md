>[!note] Feature Description
>**Feature description** turns a detected keypoint into a compact, matchable numerical vector — its *descriptor* — that summarizes the appearance of the patch around it. A good descriptor is **distinctive** (different keypoints have different descriptors), **invariant** (the same keypoint produces the same descriptor under rotation, scale, and illumination change), and **compact** (small enough for fast matching).
>
>Descriptors sit between [[4.1. Feature Detection|detection]] and [[4.4. Feature Matching|matching]]: the detector says *where* features are, the descriptor says *what they look like*, and the matcher pairs descriptors across images by similarity.

# Intuition
Once keypoints have been detected, the raw pixel patches around them cannot be compared directly — two views of the same corner will have different pixel values because of rotation, zoom, lighting, and noise. The descriptor is an invariant *fingerprint* of the patch: it throws away the nuisance variation (orientation, brightness offset) while preserving the structural content (gradient pattern, edge arrangement) that distinguishes one keypoint from another.

The descriptor design choices are a bias-variance tradeoff. Raw-pixel descriptors ([[4.3.1. MOPS|MOPS]]) are simple and fast but less robust. Gradient-histogram descriptors ([[4.3.2. SIFT|SIFT]]) trade pixel-accuracy for tolerance to small spatial deformation, winning matching accuracy at a modest compute cost. Binary descriptors (ORB, BRISK) sacrifice discriminative power for $100{\times}$ speedup. Learned descriptors (SuperPoint, D2-Net) win outright on modern benchmarks but require GPU inference.

### Notes
- [[4.3.1. MOPS]] — Multi-Scale Oriented Patches: simple rotation-normalized pixel patches
- [[4.3.2. SIFT]] — 128-D gradient-orientation histogram; the classical gold standard
