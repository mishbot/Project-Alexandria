>[!note] Modular Inverse
>In [[4.1 Modular Arithmetic|modular arithmetic]], a **modular inverse** $a \in \mathbb{Z}$ exists for every $x \in \mathbb{Z}$, such that $a \cdot x \equiv 1 \pmod{m}$, if and only if $\gcd(x, m)=1$ (i.e., $x$ and $m$ must be coprime). The easiest way to find the greatest common divisor of two integers is using **Euclid's Algorithm**, which recursively divides the integers based on the identity
>$$\gcd(x, m) = \gcd(m, x) = \gcd(m, x \bmod m),$$
>and terminates when the remainder becomes zero. 
>
>The **Extended Euclidean Algorithm** not only computes $\gcd(x, m)$, but also performs a backward pass to find (not necessarily distinct) integers $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$, which satisfy **Bezout's identity**,
>$$ax + bm = \gcd(x, m).$$
>It is executed as follows:
>1. Set the starting pair $\alpha_{1} = x$ and $\beta_{1} = m$.
>2. While $\beta_{i} \neq 0$ (forward pass / standard Euclid): 
>	1. Let $\alpha_{i+1} = \beta_{i}$ and $\beta_{i+1}=\alpha_{i} \pmod{\beta_{i}}$.
>3. Now set $a_{n} = 1$ and $b_{n}=0$.
>4. Then, for $j = n-1, \ldots, 2, 1$ (backward pass):
>	1. Let $a_{j}=b_{j+1}$ and $b_{j}=a_{j+1}-\lfloor \alpha_{j} / \beta_{j} \rfloor \cdot b_{j+1}$.
>5. When you are done, you will have the coefficients and factor that solve $ax+bm=d$.
# Proof & Intuition
### Inversion
Here is where modular arithmetic gets a little tricky. We will go ahead and prove necessity and sufficiency for inversion:
1. **Sufficiency ($\implies$):** Since $x$ and $m$ are coprime, neither can divide the other. Enter Bezout's Identity, which states that $ax+bm=1$. We take both sides modulo $m$, and obtain $$a \cdot x \equiv 1 \pmod{m}.$$As such, we find that $a$ is the inverse of $x$. You can think of $b$ as the "correction term" for the linear Diophantine, although it has no modular meaning unless we rearrange $m$ and $x$, such that $b$ is the inverse of $m \pmod{x}$.
2. **Necessity ($\impliedby$)** We have that $a \cdot x \equiv 1 \pmod{m}$. Then by definition of modular congruence, there exists an integer (quotient) $q$ such that: $ax=qm+1$. Rearranging, $ax-qm=1=\gcd(x, m)$ per Bezout's identity.

Intuitively, why is $\gcd(x, m)$ the condition for modular inversion? Think about it this way: if $x$ and $m$ shared a common factor $d > 1$, then that $d$ must be able to divide any linear combination of $x$ and $m$. 

### Euclid's Algorithm
The statement above will help us prove the fact that $\gcd(x, m)=\gcd(m \bmod{x})$. In order to see it, express $x=qm+r$. Then, if $d$ divides $x$ and $m$, then $d$ also divides $qm$, as well as $r=x-qm$ when rearranging. Now plug $x=16$ and $m=10$ and try it yourself.

# Properties
#### (*Divisibility*) Let $x,y,z \in \mathbb{N}$, such that $\gcd(x, y)=1$. If $x \mid yz$, then $x \mid z$.
#### (*Prime Modulus*) If $p$ is a prime number, all numbers $\{ p-1, \ldots, 1 \}$ have a modular inverse.
#### (*Fermat's Little Theorem*) For all primes $p$, $a^{p-1} \pmod{p} \equiv 1$ if $a  \ne 0$, and $a^p \equiv a \pmod{p}$ for all $a$.

>We will investigate the existence and uniqueness of inverses:
>>[!example]- Is 3 an inverse of 5 modulo 14?
>>Since $5\cdot 3 \equiv 1 \pmod{14}$, the answer is yes.
>
>>[!example]- Is 3 an inverse of 5 modulo 10?
>>No, since $\gcd(5, 10)=5 \neq 1$ (also because $5 \cdot 3 \equiv 5 \pmod{10}$).
>
>>[!example]- For all $n \in N$, is $3 + 14n$ an inverse of 5 modulo 14?
>>We have that $5(3 + 14n) \equiv 1+5 \cdot 0n \equiv 1 \pmod{14}$, so yes.
>
>>[!example]- Does 4 have an inverse modulo 8?
>>No, because $\gcd(4, 8)=2 \neq 1$.
