>[!note] Futures
>A **futures contract** is a [[2.2.2a Forward Contract|forward contract]] traded on an exchange, such as CME, NYSE Euronext, or Eurex. The exchange fixes every term except the price. It sets the contract size, the expiry dates, and the delivery method. For commodities it also sets the delivery place and the quality grade. Underlyings include commodities, currencies, equities, equity indices, interest rates, cryptocurrencies, and computing capacity. Each day the exchange sets a settlement price. Every open position then gains or loses the change in that price, paid in cash. This daily settlement is called **marking to market**. Let $F_t$ be the settlement price at the end of day $t$. The position opens at $F_0$, and day $T$ is the delivery date. Take a long position in $n$ contracts, each on $Q$ units. Its gain on day $t$ and its total gain to delivery are
>$$g_t = (F_t - F_{t-1})\,nQ, \qquad \sum_{t=1}^{T} g_t = (F_T - F_0)\,nQ.$$
>Here $nQ$ is the number of units in the position. The total depends only on the first and last prices. A short position gains the negative of each amount. This note needs no symbol for the value of a contract, because each settlement resets that value to zero.
>
>Gains and losses pass through a **margin account** that the trader keeps with a broker. Brokers use three kinds of margin.
>- **Initial margin** is the cash a trader deposits when opening a position.
>- **Maintenance margin** is a lower level, and a balance below it at a settlement brings a **margin call**.
>- **Variation margin** is the cash the trader adds after a call to restore the initial margin.
>
>A trader who does not meet the call has the position closed. Exchanges set minimum margins, and brokers can ask for more. Margin rules also differ between bona fide hedgers (traders who offset a business risk) and speculators. They differ between day traders and long-term holders, and for accounts whose positions offset each other (netting).
>
>At delivery the futures price equals the spot price, $F_T = S_T$. A long held to delivery therefore gains $(S_T - F_0)nQ$, the payoff of a forward with delivery price $K = F_0$. When the interest rate is constant, the futures price equals the forward price for the same date. The formulas in [[2.2.2b Forward Price]] then price futures too. Futures differ from forwards in four ways.
>- A forward is a custom over-the-counter (OTC) contract, while a futures contract trades on an exchange with standard terms.
>- A forward settles once at expiry, while a futures position settles every day.
>- A forward exposes each side to the other's default, while margin and the clearinghouse nearly remove that risk for futures.
>- A forward is hard to exit early, while a futures trader can exit at any time with the opposite trade.
>
>Exchanges also set daily price limits, the largest price change allowed in one day. They set position limits, the largest number of contracts one trader may hold. Equity index futures expire on the third Friday of March, June, September, and December. The contract multiple is the dollar value of one index point. It is \$250 for an S&P 500 futures contract, \$50 for an E-mini, and \$5 for a micro E-mini. **Perpetual futures** never expire, have no physical delivery, settle in cash, and trade around the clock. Qualified US futures receive special tax treatment. Arbitrageurs trade futures against the underlying asset when the futures price departs from its carry value ([[2.2.4 Cash-and-Carry Arbitrage]]).

# Intuition

A forward commits two parties to each other until delivery. Each side depends on the other to pay at the end, and one side's loss can grow for months. Finding a partner with matching needs takes effort, and leaving early takes a new negotiation. Futures address these problems with standard terms and daily settlement. Contracts with the same terms are interchangeable, so a trader exits by trading the opposite way with anyone. Each day the losing side pays its loss to the winning side. No one owes more than one day's loss at a time, and the margin account holds cash to cover it.

# Properties

#### *(Daily Resettlement)* A futures position behaves like a series of one-day forwards, each settled in cash and replaced by a new one.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules describe marking to market and margin calls. The clearinghouse, the zero value after settlement, and the term variation margin follow Hull. So does the rule that a call restores the initial margin.

Settlement pays the long $F_t - F_{t-1}$ per unit in cash. After that payment, the long holds a contract to buy at $F_t$, the current futures price. A new contract at that price costs nothing, so the old one is now worth zero too. The **clearinghouse**, often part of the exchange, takes the opposite side of every trade. Each day it collects losses from the brokers of losing traders and pays them to the brokers of winning traders. A trader is therefore exposed to the clearinghouse and never to the trader on the other side. Any balance above the initial margin can be withdrawn. The price that brings the next call follows from the balance. Let $B_r$ be the balance right after the settlement at price $F_r$. Let $M$ be the maintenance margin for the whole position. The next call comes at a settlement price beyond
$$F_r - \frac{B_r - M}{nQ} \quad \text{(long)}, \qquad F_r + \frac{B_r - M}{nQ} \quad \text{(short)}.$$
The fraction is the price move that uses up the cash above the maintenance level. Right after opening, or after a call, $B_r$ equals the initial margin $I$, so the buffer is $I - M$. In the diagram the buffer is $1{,}000/10{,}000 = \$0.10$, so the first call comes below \$4.300. Clearinghouses and many traders also use the term variation margin for the daily settlement payments themselves.

#### *(Delivery and Convergence)* Most positions close early, and traders who can deliver make the futures price equal the spot price at expiry.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules list the delivery terms but do not explain convergence. The delivery details and the arbitrage argument below follow Hull.

Most traders close their positions with an opposite trade before the delivery period starts. In a physically settled contract, the short side chooses the delivery day within the delivery month. It also chooses the place and the grade, among those the exchange allows. A cash-settled contract, such as an equity index future, ends with a final settlement price set from the index itself. Convergence then holds by the rules of the contract. For physical delivery, arbitrage enforces it. Suppose the futures price is above the spot price during the delivery period. A trader buys the asset, sells a futures contract, and delivers at once, for a sure gain of $F_t - S_t$. These sales push the futures price down. If the futures price is below the spot price, firms that want the asset buy futures and wait for delivery. Their buying pushes the futures price up. The difference $S_t - F_t$ is the **basis**, and it shrinks to zero at expiry. Hedgers who close early face basis risk, the risk that the basis on the exit date differs from their plan. The Treasury cash-futures basis trade relies on this convergence ([[2.2.4 Cash-and-Carry Arbitrage]]).

#### *(Futures Versus Forward Prices)* A futures price can differ from the forward price only when interest rates are random.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules use forward formulas for futures prices. The proof below and the effect of random interest rates follow Hull.

In this property $G_0$ denotes the forward price, since $F_0$ is the futures price. A long futures receives its gains early and can invest them. It also pays its losses early and must finance them. With a known rate, a trader can scale the position each day so that the interest offsets the timing exactly. The proof below builds that scaled position. When rates are random, the sign of the gap depends on how the asset price moves with rates. Suppose the price tends to rise when rates rise. Then gains arrive when they earn the most interest, and losses arrive when borrowing is cheap. A long futures is then worth more than a long forward at the same price. Buyers bid the futures price up, so $F_0 > G_0$. A negative link between the price and rates reverses the order. For contracts that last a few months, the gap is usually small.

>[!example]- Proof that $F_0 = G_0$ when the rate is constant
>Let $\delta$ be the interest rate per day, continuously compounded, and let delivery be at the end of day $m$.
>
>Strategy A holds $e^{i\delta}$ futures contracts during day $i$, for $i = 1, \dots, m$. It invests each day's gain, or borrows each day's loss, at $\delta$ until day $m$. It also puts $F_0$ in a bond at time $0$. The gain on day $i$ is $(F_i - F_{i-1})e^{i\delta}$, which grows to $(F_i - F_{i-1})e^{m\delta}$ by day $m$. The sum over all days is $(F_m - F_0)e^{m\delta} = (S_T - F_0)e^{m\delta}$, by convergence. The bond is worth $F_0e^{m\delta}$, so A ends with $S_Te^{m\delta}$.
>
>Strategy B buys $e^{m\delta}$ forward contracts with delivery price $G_0$ and puts $G_0$ in a bond. At delivery it is worth $(S_T - G_0)e^{m\delta} + G_0e^{m\delta} = S_Te^{m\delta}$.
>
>Both strategies pay $S_Te^{m\delta}$ in every state. Futures and forwards cost nothing to enter, so A costs $F_0$ and B costs $G_0$. By the [[4.2.2a Arbitrage|law of one price]], $F_0 = G_0$.

#### *(Leverage and Losses)* Futures traders post small margins for large positions, so price moves are large relative to the cash posted.

The position is worth $F_0nQ$, and the trader posts only the initial margin $I$. The ratio $L = F_0nQ/I$ is the **leverage**. A price move of $x\%$ changes the balance by $Lx\%$ of the initial margin. Suppose an E-mini trades at $6{,}000$ and the initial margin is \$15,000. One contract is worth $6{,}000 \times 50 = \$300{,}000$, so the leverage is $20$. A $1\%$ fall then costs \$3,000, a fifth of the margin. Offshore and decentralized platforms for perpetual futures let retail traders use leverage of 50 to 100 times, and sometimes more. At 100 times, a $1\%$ move against the trader uses up all the collateral. Traditional regulated futures often cap leverage much lower. Two firms failed after large futures losses. Nick Leeson's unauthorized and reckless trades in Nikkei 225 futures lost \$1.3 billion. The loss caused the collapse of Barings Bank in 1995. Amaranth Advisors lost \$6.6 billion in 2006 on a large, misplaced bet on natural gas futures. The bet concerned their term structure, the pattern of prices across delivery months. Amaranth collapsed after the loss. Futures trading can also move the cash market. Some analysts link the 2010 "Flash Crash" in US stocks to unusual activity in S&P 500 futures. The cause is not settled.

#### *(Perpetual Futures)* Perpetuals trade mostly offshore on crypto platforms, and the first regulated US perpetual began trading in 2026.

Some traders call perpetuals perpetual swaps, and some argue that they resemble [[2.2.5 Swaps|swaps]] more than futures. Worldwide, more than $90\%$ of futures on cryptocurrencies are perpetuals, usually traded offshore. Crypto futures and other crypto derivatives make up roughly $75$ to $80\%$ of all cryptocurrency trading volume. On Binance, for example, Bitcoin futures trade about ten times the volume of spot Bitcoin. Perpetuals also exist on gold, silver, copper, crude oil, and stocks ("equity perps"). These trade offshore around the clock and usually settle in cryptocurrency. In the US, Kalshi launched the first regulated Bitcoin perpetual on June 3, 2026. In August 2026 it filed with the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), the US futures regulator. The filing seeks onshore copper ("COPPERPERP") and equity ("US500") perpetuals.

#### *(Tax Treatment)* Four US tax rules apply to qualified futures, and most of them favor futures over the cash asset.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules list the four rules without explaining them. The explanations below summarize US tax law, mainly Section 1256 of the Internal Revenue Code.

The rules cover qualified futures traded on US regulated exchanges, such as futures on regulated commodities, indices, and certain cryptocurrencies.
- The 60/40 rule taxes $60\%$ of each gain or loss as long-term and $40\%$ as short-term, whatever the holding period. A day trader thus pays a lower blended rate than on the same gain in stocks.
- Mark-to-market accounting treats every open position as sold at its market price on the last business day of the year. A trader can owe tax on a gain not yet realized.
- Loss carry back lets an individual apply a net futures loss against futures gains of the previous three years.
- The wash sale rule defers a tax loss when a substantially identical security is bought within $30$ days. The window covers the $30$ days before and after the sale. Futures are exempt, so a trader can take a loss and reopen the position at once.

# Derivation

We want to show that a long futures position held to delivery earns the payoff of a forward with $K = F_0$.

By daily settlement, the long gains $g_t = (F_t - F_{t-1})nQ$ on day $t$. Ignore interest on the account. The total gain is then a sum in which each middle price appears once with each sign,
$$\sum_{t=1}^{T} g_t = (F_1 - F_0)nQ + (F_2 - F_1)nQ + \dots + (F_T - F_{T-1})nQ = (F_T - F_0)nQ.$$
Only the opening price $F_0$ and the final price $F_T$ survive. By convergence at delivery, $F_T = S_T$. Hence the total gain is $(S_T - F_0)nQ$. A long forward on $nQ$ units with delivery price $K = F_0$ pays exactly this amount at $T$. The two positions differ only in timing, since the futures gain arrives in daily pieces. With a constant interest rate, the property on forward prices shows that the timing leaves the fair price unchanged.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Micro Contracts and the Total Gain
>An investor buys 3 micro E-mini S&P 500 futures at $6{,}412.50$. Each micro contract is worth \$5 per index point. The settlement prices on the next three days are $6{,}398.25$, $6{,}431.00$, and $6{,}446.75$. She closes the position at the day-3 settlement price. Ignore interest on the margin account.
>
>>[!example]- Find each day's cash flow, check the total against the price change, and find the total for 3 E-minis.
>>The position is worth $3 \times 5 = \$15$ per index point. The daily cash flows are
>>$$g_1 = -14.25 \times 15 = -\$213.75, \qquad g_2 = 32.75 \times 15 = \$491.25, \qquad g_3 = 15.75 \times 15 = \$236.25.$$
>>Each first factor is the change in the settlement price over that day. The flows add up to \$513.75. The price rose by $6{,}446.75 - 6{,}412.50 = 34.25$ points over the three days, and $34.25 \times 15 = \$513.75$. The day-1 loss leaves the account first, yet only the first and last prices set the total. An E-mini is worth ten times a micro contract, so 3 E-minis would gain $34.25 \times 50 \times 3 = \$5{,}137.50$.

>[!example] (Medium) A Margin Call on a Short Position
>A trader sells 4 E-mini S&P 500 futures at $6{,}245.00$. Each contract is worth \$50 per index point. The initial margin is \$14,000 per contract and the maintenance margin is \$12,000 per contract. A margin call restores the initial margin, and the trader withdraws nothing. The settlement prices on days 1 to 4 are $6{,}257.00$, $6{,}292.50$, $6{,}265.00$, and $6{,}231.25$. The trader closes on day 4. Ignore interest on the margin account.
>
>>[!example]- Find the price that triggers the first margin call, track the balance, and check the total gain.
>>The position is worth $4 \times 50 = \$200$ per point. The account starts at $I = \$56{,}000$, and the maintenance margin is $M = \$48{,}000$. A short loses when the price rises, so the first call comes above
>>$$F_0 + \frac{I - M}{nQ} = 6{,}245 + \frac{8{,}000}{200} = 6{,}285.00.$$
>>Here $nQ = 200$ is the dollar value of one index point for the position. The balance moves as follows.
>>
>>| Day | Settlement | Gain | Balance | Call |
>>|---|---|---|---|---|
>>| 0 | $6{,}245.00$ | | $56{,}000$ | |
>>| 1 | $6{,}257.00$ | $-2{,}400$ | $53{,}600$ | |
>>| 2 | $6{,}292.50$ | $-7{,}100$ | $46{,}500$ | $9{,}500$ |
>>| 3 | $6{,}265.00$ | $+5{,}500$ | $61{,}500$ | |
>>| 4 | $6{,}231.25$ | $+6{,}750$ | $68{,}250$ | |
>>
>>On day 2 the balance of \$46,500 is below \$48,000. The trader deposits $56{,}000 - 46{,}500 = \$9{,}500$, so day 3 starts from \$56,000. The table shows the day-2 balance before the call. The next call would need a price above $6{,}292.50 + 40 = 6{,}332.50$. The total gain is $(6{,}245.00 - 6{,}231.25) \times 200 = \$2{,}750$. The trader paid in $56{,}000 + 9{,}500 = \$65{,}500$ and takes out \$68,250 at the close, a net of \$2,750. The margin call left the total gain unchanged. It only moved cash into the account while the position was losing.

>The next example compares futures and forwards when daily gains earn interest. The modules treat the two prices as equal and do not show this.

>[!example] (Hard) When Daily Gains Earn Interest
>A futures contract and a forward contract each cover 100 units of an asset for delivery in three months. Both are entered at a price of \$80.00. To keep the numbers short, the futures settles once a month instead of daily. The interest rate is $1\%$ per month, compounded monthly. Any gain is invested, and any loss is borrowed, at that rate until delivery. On path A the futures price is \$84, \$77, and \$81 at the ends of months 1, 2, and 3. On path B it is \$76, \$83, and \$81. The spot price at delivery is \$81 on both paths.
>
>>[!example]- Value both contracts at delivery on each path, and find a futures position that matches the forward on every path.
>>The forward pays $(81 - 80) \times 100 = \$100$ at delivery on both paths.
>>
>>On path A the futures pays $+\$400$, $-\$700$, and $+\$400$ at the ends of months 1, 2, and 3. The first flow earns interest for two months and the second for one month. At delivery the futures position is worth
>>$$400 \times 1.01^2 - 700 \times 1.01 + 400 = 408.04 - 707.00 + 400 = \$101.04.$$
>>On path B the flows are $-\$400$, $+\$700$, and $-\$200$. At delivery they are worth
>>$$-400 \times 1.01^2 + 700 \times 1.01 - 200 = -408.04 + 707.00 - 200 = \$98.96.$$
>>Both paths end at the same price. The futures beats the forward on path A, where the early flow is a gain that earns interest. It trails the forward on path B, where the early flow is a loss that is financed.
>>
>>A flow paid $k$ months before delivery grows by the factor $1.01^k$. So hold $1.01^{-k}$ contracts in that month, treating contracts as divisible. That is $1.01^{-2} = 0.9803$ contracts in month 1, $1.01^{-1} = 0.9901$ in month 2, and $1$ in month 3. Each month's flow, grown to delivery, then equals the plain price change times 100. The total is $400 - 700 + 400 = \$100$ on path A and $-400 + 700 - 200 = \$100$ on path B. The scaled futures matches the forward on every path. With different prices, the scaled futures and an opposite forward would lock in a sure profit.
