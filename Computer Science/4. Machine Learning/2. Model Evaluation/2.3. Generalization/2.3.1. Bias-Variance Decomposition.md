>[!note] Bias-Variance Decomposition
>The **bias-variance decomposition** splits the expected squared error of a learning method at an input $\mathbf{x}$ into three parts. Assume the responses follow $y = f(\mathbf{x}) + \varepsilon$. Here $f$ is the true regression function, and the noise $\varepsilon$ has mean $0$ and variance $\sigma^2$. A random training set $\mathcal{D}$ of $n$ samples produces the fitted model $\hat f_{\mathcal{D}}$. The **average predictor** $\bar f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\hat f_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x})]$ averages the fits over all possible training sets. For a new response $y$ at $\mathbf{x}$, drawn independently of $\mathcal{D}$,
>$$\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}, y}\Big[\big(y - \hat f_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x})\big)^2\Big] = \sigma^2 + \big(\bar f(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{x})\big)^2 + \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\Big[\big(\hat f_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x}) - \bar f(\mathbf{x})\big)^2\Big].$$
>The expectation on the left runs over the training set and the new noise. The three terms on the right are:
>
>- **Noise** $\sigma^2$: the spread of $y$ around $f(\mathbf{x})$. No model can remove it.
>- **Squared bias** $(\bar f(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{x}))^2$: the error of the average fit. It comes from a model class that is too simple or too constrained.
>- **Variance** $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[(\hat f_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x}) - \bar f(\mathbf{x}))^2]$: how far one fit strays from the average fit. It comes from flexibility relative to the amount of data.
>
>Averaging each term over $\mathbf{x}$ from the input distribution turns the left side into the expected test MSE. For vector outputs, each square becomes a squared norm. The decomposition is exact only for squared error.
>
>The **bias-variance tradeoff** describes what happens as capacity grows with $n$ fixed. More parameters, a smaller penalty $\lambda$, fewer neighbors in [[4.5. k-Nearest Neighbors|k-NN]] or deeper trees lower the bias and raise the variance. The expected test error is then U-shaped in capacity, with its minimum at an intermediate capacity. More training data lowers the variance. It does not remove the bias of a model class that cannot represent $f$. Neither term can be measured from one dataset, since the bias needs $f$ and the variance needs many training sets. Once a model has enough capacity to interpolate the training data, test error can fall again. This pattern is called **double descent**.

# Intuition

Picture repeating the whole experiment many times. Each repetition draws a new training set and fits a new model, so the fitted function is random. The word "bias" comes from statistics, where a biased estimator misses the true value on average. Here the average of all the fitted functions, $\bar f$, misses $f$ by the bias. The "variance" is the ordinary variance of the fitted value at $\mathbf{x}$ across repetitions. A straight line fit to a curved trend comes out nearly the same every time. Every copy is wrong in the same places. A high-degree polynomial follows the noise of each training set. Its fits are right on average but wrong in a different direction each time. The noise term is the part of $y$ that no function of $\mathbf{x}$ can predict.

When a modeler raises capacity, the bias falls and the variance rises. The best capacity balances the two, and it depends on $n$ and $\sigma^2$. With more data each fit varies less, so the best capacity grows with $n$.

# Properties

#### *(Shrinkage Trades Variance for Bias)* A small amount of ridge shrinkage always lowers the expected squared error of a least-squares coefficient.

Take one feature, no intercept, fixed inputs with $S = \sum_i x_i^2$, and responses $y_i = w x_i + \varepsilon_i$. [[3.2.1. Ridge Regression|Ridge regression]] with penalty $\lambda$ gives $\hat w_\lambda = \sum_i x_i y_i/(S + \lambda)$. Its bias and variance are
$$\mathbb{E}[\hat w_\lambda] - w = -\frac{\lambda}{S + \lambda}\,w, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(\hat w_\lambda) = \frac{\sigma^2 S}{(S + \lambda)^2}.$$
The size of the bias grows from $0$ at $\lambda = 0$, and the variance falls from the least-squares value $\sigma^2/S$. Their combination $(\lambda^2 w^2 + \sigma^2 S)/(S + \lambda)^2$ has a negative slope at $\lambda = 0$. Its minimum lies at $\lambda^\star = \sigma^2/w^2$. The best penalty grows with the noise variance and shrinks as the true weight grows. With many features, the same calculation applies along each singular direction of $\mathbf{X}$. A squared singular value then takes the place of $S$. Directions with small singular values have the largest variance and gain the most from shrinkage. The best $\lambda$ depends on the unknown $w$, so in practice [[2.3.2. Cross-Validation|cross-validation]] chooses it.

#### *(Diagnosing Bias and Variance)* Plots of training and validation error against capacity or against $n$ show which term dominates.

High bias shows as a training error that is already high, with the validation error close to it. More data then changes little, and the remedy is more capacity or better features. High variance shows as a low training error and a large gap to the validation error. More data, more regularization or a smaller model then help. A **learning curve** plots both errors against $n$. Under high bias the two curves meet early at a high level. Under high variance they approach each other slowly from far apart. Consider least squares with $d$ coefficients and fixed inputs. The variance of its fitted values, averaged over the training inputs, is exactly $\sigma^2 d/n$. This equals $\sigma^2/n$ times the trace of the hat matrix. Doubling $n$ halves it, while the bias of a misspecified linear model does not shrink to zero.

#### *(Other Losses Do Not Split Additively)* For 0-1 loss, bias and variance interact, and extra variance can even raise accuracy.

The derivation below drops a cross term that is zero only for squared error. For classification with 0-1 loss, consider an input where the average model predicts the wrong class. Random variation pushes some fits across the decision threshold to the correct class. More variance then lowers the error at that input. Where the average model is right, variance only hurts. Friedman (1997) and Domingos (2000) give decompositions for 0-1 loss that capture this interaction. For cross-entropy and other losses, noise, bias and variance still cause error, but their contributions do not add.

#### *(Double Descent)* In over-parameterized models, test error can fall a second time after a peak at the interpolation threshold.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Double descent comes from Prince, *Understanding Deep Learning*, section 8.4, and from Belkin et al. (2019) and Nakkiran et al. (2021).

The classical U-shaped curve describes models with fewer parameters than training points. As the parameter count approaches $n$, the model can barely fit every point. Only one fit passes through the data, and it has sharp bends between the points. Test error peaks there, at the **interpolation threshold**. Past the threshold, many fits interpolate the data, and the training algorithm picks one of them. [[6.4. Gradient Descent|Gradient descent]] on least squares, started from zero, converges to the minimum-norm interpolating solution. That solution becomes smoother as parameters are added, and test error falls again. Label noise raises the peak. In linear regression, an optimally tuned ridge penalty can remove the peak (Nakkiran et al., 2020). The peak also appears as training time or the number of samples varies. Near the threshold, adding training data can therefore raise test error.

# Derivation

Fix $\mathbf{x}$ and abbreviate $\hat f = \hat f_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x})$, $f = f(\mathbf{x})$ and $\bar f = \bar f(\mathbf{x})$. The first step separates the noise. Since $y = f + \varepsilon$,
$$(y - \hat f)^2 = \varepsilon^2 + 2\varepsilon\,(f - \hat f) + (f - \hat f)^2.$$
The new noise $\varepsilon$ has mean zero and is independent of $\mathcal{D}$, so $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\,(f - \hat f)] = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]\,\mathbb{E}[f - \hat f] = 0$. Taking expectations leaves $\sigma^2 + \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[(\hat f - f)^2]$. The second step adds and subtracts the average predictor inside the remaining square:
$$\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\big[(\hat f - f)^2\big] = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\big[(\hat f - \bar f)^2\big] + 2\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\big[\hat f - \bar f\big]\,(\bar f - f) + (\bar f - f)^2.$$
The factor $\bar f - f$ does not depend on $\mathcal{D}$, so it moves outside the expectation. The middle term vanishes because $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\hat f] = \bar f$ by definition. What remains is the variance plus the squared bias. Adding $\sigma^2$ from the first step gives the decomposition in the callout. The second step is the identity $\mathbb{E}[Z^2] = \mathrm{Var}(Z) + (\mathbb{E}[Z])^2$ from [[2.5b Second-Order Moments|second-order moments]], applied to $Z = \hat f - f$. For vector outputs, the same steps hold with inner products. The cross term becomes $2\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\hat{\mathbf{f}} - \bar{\mathbf{f}}]^T(\bar{\mathbf{f}} - \mathbf{f})$, which is again zero.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Decomposing the Error for One House
>A model predicts the sale price of one house, in thousands of dollars. The true expected price is $f(\mathbf{x}) = 320$, and sale prices scatter around it with standard deviation $\sigma = 15$. The model is retrained on five equally likely training sets and predicts $310$, $290$, $305$, $295$ and $300$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the noise, squared bias and variance. Which change would help most?
>>The average prediction is $\bar f = 300$, so the bias is $300 - 320 = -20$ and the squared bias is $400$. The deviations from $300$ are $10, -10, 5, -5$ and $0$, so the variance is $(100 + 100 + 25 + 25 + 0)/5 = 50$. The noise is $15^2 = 225$. The expected squared error is $225 + 400 + 50 = 675$, an RMSE of about $26$ thousand dollars. The squared bias is the largest term, so a more flexible model or better features would help most. More data would shrink only the $50$, and no change can remove the $225$.

>[!example] (Medium) Tuning Shrinkage for a Sensor
>A sensor reading $x$ maps to a temperature through $y = w x + \varepsilon$, with true $w = 1$ and noise variance $\sigma^2 = 4$. Five calibration readings give $S = \sum_i x_i^2 = 20$. An engineer fits ridge regression without an intercept.
>
>>[!example]- Find the bias, variance and expected squared error of $\hat w_\lambda$ for $\lambda = 0, 4, 10$. Which penalty is best?
>>The formulas from the shrinkage property give the following values:
>>
>>| $\lambda$ | Bias | Squared bias | Variance | Expected squared error |
>>|---|---|---|---|---|
>>| $0$ | $0$ | $0$ | $4 \cdot 20/20^2 = 0.200$ | $0.200$ |
>>| $4$ | $-4/24 = -0.167$ | $0.028$ | $80/24^2 = 0.139$ | $0.167$ |
>>| $10$ | $-10/30 = -0.333$ | $0.111$ | $80/30^2 = 0.089$ | $0.200$ |
>>
>>The best penalty is $\lambda^\star = \sigma^2/w^2 = 4$. It cuts the expected squared error by about $17\%$ relative to least squares. At $\lambda = 10$ the error is back at $0.200$, since the added bias cancels the saved variance. A simulation with $20{,}000$ noise draws reproduces these values. The engineer does not know $w$, so in practice cross-validation would estimate the best $\lambda$.

>[!example] (Hard) Variance Near the Interpolation Threshold
>A model fits $n = 50$ training points with $p$ features. Every feature is drawn independently from $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, and the labels carry noise of variance $\sigma^2 = 1$. To isolate the variance, take the true weights to be $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$, so every fitted weight comes from noise. For $p < n$ the fit is least squares. For $p > n$ it is the minimum-norm interpolating solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{X}^+\mathbf{y}$, where $\mathbf{X}^+$ is the [[13.2 Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse|pseudoinverse]]. For a test input with independent standard normal features, the excess test error equals $\mathbb{E}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^2$. Hastie et al. (2022) give $\sigma^2 p/(n - p - 1)$ for $p < n - 1$ and $\sigma^2 n/(p - n - 1)$ for $p > n + 1$.
>
>>[!example]- Evaluate the variance at $p = 10, 40, 48, 52, 60, 200, 1000$. Where does it peak, and why does it fall again?
>>The formulas give the following values, and simulations with thousands of draws per $p$ match them:
>>
>>| $p$ | 10 | 40 | 48 | 52 | 60 | 200 | 1000 |
>>|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
>>| Variance | 0.256 | 4.44 | 48 | 50 | 5.56 | 0.336 | 0.053 |
>>
>>Below the threshold, each added feature is another coefficient estimated from the same 50 points, so the variance rises. Near $p = n$ the matrix $\mathbf{X}$ is almost square and has some tiny singular values. Least squares divides the noise by these singular values, which inflates the weights. Above the threshold, the minimum-norm solution spreads the noise over many more directions, so each weight stays small. The variance then falls like $n/p$. Now let the true $\mathbf{w}$ be nonzero with a uniformly random direction. The min-norm fit then also has a squared bias of $\|\mathbf{w}\|^2(1 - n/p)$. It recovers only the projection of $\mathbf{w}$ onto an $n$-dimensional subspace. Whether test error has a second descent depends on how this bias grows against the falling variance.
