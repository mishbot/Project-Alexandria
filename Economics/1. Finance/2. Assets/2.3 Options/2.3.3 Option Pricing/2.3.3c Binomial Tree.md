>[!note] Binomial Tree
>A **binomial tree** prices an option by splitting its life into $N$ short steps and applying the [[2.3.3a One-Step Binomial Model|one-step model]] at every node. The **Cox-Ross-Rubinstein (CRR) model** builds the tree from the volatility $\sigma$ of the stock:
>$$\Delta t = \frac{T}{N}, \qquad u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}, \qquad d = \frac{1}{u}, \qquad p = \frac{e^{r\Delta t} - d}{u - d}.$$
>Here $T$ is the time to expiry, $r$ is the continuously compounded risk-free rate, and $p$ is the [[2.3.3b Risk-Neutral Valuation|risk-neutral probability]] of an up move. After $j$ steps with $i$ up moves, the stock price is $S_{j,i} = S_0 u^i d^{j-i}$ for $i = 0, \ldots, j$. The tree recombines, because an up move followed by a down move gives the same price as the reverse. Let $f_{j,i}$ be the option value at that node. At expiry it is the payoff, $\max(S_{N,i} - K, 0)$ for a call or $\max(K - S_{N,i}, 0)$ for a put. Earlier values follow by **backward induction**,
>$$f_{j,i} = e^{-r\Delta t}\big[p\,f_{j+1,i+1} + (1-p)\,f_{j+1,i}\big].$$
>Each node's value is the discounted risk-neutral expectation of the two values one step later. For an American option, each node takes the larger of this continuation value and the payoff from exercising at once. The choice $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ makes the variance of each one-step return equal $\sigma^2\Delta t$ to first order in $\Delta t$. For a stock with continuous dividend yield $q$, the probability becomes $p = (e^{(r-q)\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)$. As $N \to \infty$, European prices from the tree converge to the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]].

# Intuition

A single step lets the stock end at only two prices. A real stock can end anywhere in a wide range. Many short steps fix this. After $N$ steps there are $N + 1$ possible final prices. As $N$ grows, their spread approaches the [[2.2c.11 Lognormal Distribution|lognormal distribution]] of continuous-time models. The figure shows a three-step tree with the stock price above each node and the option value below it.

The values on the right edge are payoffs, known from the contract. Each earlier value comes from the two values to its right, so the computation moves from right to left. Every small triangle of one node and its two successors is a one-step model. Each node therefore has its own hedge ratio, which [[2.3.3d Delta Hedging]] uses.

# Properties

#### *(Recombination)* Because $udS_0 = duS_0$, step $j$ has only $j + 1$ nodes, so large trees are fast to compute.

The price at a node depends only on the number of up moves, not on their order. A tree with $N$ steps has $(N + 1)(N + 2)/2$ nodes in total. For $N = 100$ that is $5{,}151$ nodes, while the number of distinct up and down paths is $2^{100} \approx 1.27 \times 10^{30}$. Recombination needs the same $u$ and $d$ at every step. A model in which the step sizes depend on the path would not recombine.

#### *(Closed Form for European Options)* A European price is a sum over the $N + 1$ final nodes, weighted by binomial probabilities.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules price trees by backward induction only. The closed-form sum follows from the same rule but is not shown.

Under $p$, the number of up moves in $N$ steps has the [[2.2b.4 Binomial Distribution|binomial distribution]] with parameters $N$ and $p$. Since the price is $e^{-rT}\hat{\mathbb{E}}[f_T]$,
$$f = e^{-rT}\sum_{i=0}^{N}\binom{N}{i}p^i(1-p)^{N-i}\,f_{N,i}.$$
Here $f_{N,i}$ is the payoff at the final node with $i$ up moves. The sum gives the same number as backward induction. It does not work for American options, which need the comparison at every node.

#### *(American Options)* At each node the holder exercises when the exercise payoff exceeds the continuation value.

For an American put the node value is $\max(K - S_{j,i},\ e^{-r\Delta t}[p f_{j+1,i+1} + (1-p) f_{j+1,i}])$. For a call it is $\max(S_{j,i} - K,\ \text{continuation})$. Exercise wins at low-price nodes of a put, where the holder prefers $K$ now to waiting. For a call on a stock with no dividends, exercise never wins in the tree, which matches $C = c$ in [[2.3.1c Early Exercise]]. A check case: with $S_0 = 100$, $K = 95$, $r = 5.5\%$, $\sigma = 35\%$, $T = 1$, and $N = 100$, the European put is worth $8.7638$ and the American put $9.1932$. The difference, $0.4294$, is the value of the right to exercise early.

#### *(Convergence to Black-Scholes-Merton)* The European tree price approaches the Black-Scholes-Merton price as $N$ grows, but the error does not shrink smoothly.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules state the limit as $N \to \infty$. They do not discuss how the error behaves along the way.

For the put in the check case, the Black-Scholes-Merton price is $8.7465$, so the tree with $N = 100$ is off by $0.0173$. With $N = 99$ and $N = 101$ the tree gives $8.7582$ and $8.7589$. With $N = 200$ it gives $8.7348$, below the limit, and with $N = 1{,}000$ it gives $8.7487$. The error changes size and sign as $N$ changes, because the strike falls at different places between the final nodes. In this case the error is still more than one cent at $N = 200$.

#### *(Alternative Trees)* Other choices of $u$, $d$, and $p$ give the same limit and differ only for small $N$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules list these trees without derivations. Some textbooks attach the name Jarrow-Rudd to the equal-probability tree instead of the third tree below.

With a continuous dividend yield $q$, the stock grows at $r - q$ under risk-neutral probabilities, since dividends provide the rest of its return. Three common trees are as follows. The CRR tree keeps $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ and $d = 1/u$ and sets $p = (e^{(r-q)\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)$. The CRR equal-probability tree sets $p = 1/2$ and moves the center of the tree,
$$u = e^{(r - q - \sigma^2/2)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}, \qquad d = e^{(r - q - \sigma^2/2)\Delta t - \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}.$$
The term $(r - q - \sigma^2/2)\Delta t$ is the risk-neutral drift of $\ln S$ over one step. The Jarrow-Rudd tree keeps $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ and $d = 1/u$ and sets $p = \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{r - q - \sigma^2/2}{2\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}$. That $p$ is the CRR probability with the terms beyond first order in $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ dropped. In the check case with $N = 100$, the three trees give European puts of $8.7638$, $8.7723$, and $8.7641$.

# Derivation

We want to choose $u$ and $d$ so that the tree matches the stock's volatility. Under the model in [[3.1f Historical Volatility]], the return over a step of length $\Delta t$ has variance $\sigma^2\Delta t$.

The one-step returns are $u - 1$ and $d - 1$. Their variance under $p$ is
$$p(u-1)^2 + (1-p)(d-1)^2 - \big[p(u-1) + (1-p)(d-1)\big]^2 = p(1-p)(u-d)^2.$$
The left side is the mean square minus the squared mean. The right side follows by expanding and collecting terms. Substitute $p = (e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)$ and $1 - p = (u - e^{r\Delta t})/(u - d)$, and set the variance equal to $\sigma^2\Delta t$:
$$(e^{r\Delta t} - d)(u - e^{r\Delta t}) = \sigma^2\Delta t.$$
This is one equation for two unknowns, so we add the condition $d = 1/u$, which centers the tree on $S_0$. Expanding the product with $ud = 1$ gives $(u + d)e^{r\Delta t} - e^{2r\Delta t} - 1 = \sigma^2\Delta t$. Solve for $u + d$ and expand the exponentials in powers of $\Delta t$:
$$u + d = e^{r\Delta t} + e^{-r\Delta t} + \sigma^2\Delta t\,e^{-r\Delta t} = 2 + \sigma^2\Delta t + O(\Delta t^2).$$
The term $O(\Delta t^2)$ collects all terms of order $\Delta t^2$ and smaller. Write $u = e^a$, so $d = e^{-a}$ and $u + d = e^a + e^{-a} = 2 + a^2 + O(a^4)$. Matching the two expansions gives $a^2 = \sigma^2\Delta t + O(\Delta t^2)$. To first order, $a = \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}$. So $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ and $d = e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$, as in the callout. The variance here is computed under $p$, but $\sigma$ is the real-world volatility. [[2.3.3b Risk-Neutral Valuation]] explains why the two agree to this order.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Two-Step European Call
>A stock trades at $\$60$. Each six-month step multiplies the price by $u = 1.15$ or $d = 0.9$. A one-year European call has strike $\$61$. The risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Price the call on a two-step tree.
>>Each step has $\Delta t = 0.5$, so $e^{r\Delta t} = 1.0202$ and $p = (1.0202 - 0.9)/(1.15 - 0.9) = 0.4808$. The final prices are $\$79.35$, $\$62.10$, and $\$48.60$, with payoffs $\$18.35$, $\$1.10$, and $0$.
>>
>>At the node with price $\$69$, the value is $e^{-0.02}(0.4808 \times 18.35 + 0.5192 \times 1.10) = \$9.208$. At the node with price $\$54$, it is $e^{-0.02}(0.4808 \times 1.10 + 0.5192 \times 0) = \$0.518$. At the root,
>>$$c = e^{-0.02}(0.4808 \times 9.208 + 0.5192 \times 0.518) = \$4.60.$$
>>The closed-form sum gives the same result, $e^{-0.04}\big(0.4808^2 \times 18.35 + 2 \times 0.4808 \times 0.5192 \times 1.10\big) = \$4.60$.

>[!example] (Medium) An American Put
>A stock trades at $\$80$. Each one-year step multiplies the price by $u = 1.25$ or $d = 0.8$. A two-year put has strike $\$84$. The risk-free rate is $6\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Price the put as a European and as an American option, and find where early exercise happens.
>>Here $p = (e^{0.06} - 0.8)/(1.25 - 0.8) = 0.5819$ and the one-step discount factor is $e^{-0.06} = 0.9418$. The final prices are $\$125$, $\$80$, and $\$51.20$, with put payoffs $0$, $\$4$, and $\$32.80$.
>>
>>At the up node, with price $\$100$, the continuation value is $0.9418 \times 0.4181 \times 4 = \$1.58$. Exercise pays nothing there, so the value stays $\$1.58$. At the down node, with price $\$64$, the continuation value is $0.9418 \times (0.5819 \times 4 + 0.4181 \times 32.80) = \$15.11$. Exercise pays $84 - 64 = \$20$, which is larger. The holder exercises, and the node value is $\$20$.
>>
>>At the root, the continuation value is $0.9418 \times (0.5819 \times 1.58 + 0.4181 \times 20) = \$8.74$. Exercise would pay only $\$4$, so the American put is worth $\$8.74$. Without early exercise, the down node keeps $\$15.11$ and the root value is $\$6.81$. The right to exercise at $\$64$ is worth $\$1.93$ today. Waiting at that node is worth only $\$15.11$, so the holder takes $\$20$ now and can earn interest on it.

>[!example] (Hard) A Three-Step CRR Tree
>A stock trades at $\$50$ and pays no dividends. Its volatility is $30\%$ per year. A nine-month European call has strike $\$52$. The risk-free rate is $5\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Build a three-step CRR tree, price the call, and compare the result with a tree of $100$ steps.
>>The step is $\Delta t = 0.75/3 = 0.25$. The tree parameters are
>>$$u = e^{0.30\sqrt{0.25}} = e^{0.15} = 1.1618, \qquad d = 0.8607, \qquad p = \frac{e^{0.0125} - 0.8607}{1.1618 - 0.8607} = 0.5043.$$
>>The one-step discount factor is $e^{-0.0125} = 0.9876$. The final prices are $\$78.42$, $\$58.09$, $\$43.04$, and $\$31.88$. The call pays $\$26.42$, $\$6.09$, $0$, and $0$.
>>
>>At step $2$, the prices are $\$67.49$, $\$50.00$, and $\$37.04$. Backward induction gives call values $\$16.14$, $\$3.03$, and $0$. At step $1$, the prices are $\$58.09$ and $\$43.04$, with values $\$9.52$ and $\$1.51$. At the root,
>>$$c = 0.9876\,(0.5043 \times 9.52 + 0.4957 \times 1.51) = \$5.48.$$
>>
>>A CRR tree with $N = 100$ steps, built the same way by computer, gives $\$5.13$. The [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]] also gives $\$5.13$. The three-step tree overstates the price by $\$0.35$. With $N = 4$ and $N = 5$, the tree gives $\$5.13$ and $\$5.32$, so the error changes sign and size before it becomes small. Three steps show the method. A usable price needs many more steps.
