>[!note] Equivalent Resistance
>The **equivalent resistance** of a group of resistors is the resistance of one resistor that could replace the group. Connected to the same source, the replacement must draw the same current. Two arrangements have simple rules, which the EE notes [[2.1f.2i In-Series Resistors]] and [[2.1f.2ii Parallel Resistors]] use in circuit analysis.
>- Two resistors are in **series** when they are joined end to end and nothing else connects to the point between them. The same current passes through both, and their potential differences add. Their resistances add:
>$$R_{\text{eq}} = R_1 + R_2.$$
>- Two resistors are in **parallel** when one end of each shares a wire and their other ends share a second wire. Both have the same potential difference, and their currents add. Their reciprocals add:
>$$\frac{1}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}, \qquad R_{\text{eq}} = \frac{R_1R_2}{R_1 + R_2}.$$
>
>In both formulas $R_{\text{eq}}$ is the single resistance that replaces the pair. Both rules extend to more resistors. A series combination is larger than its largest member, and a parallel combination is smaller than its smallest member. The rules are the reverse of those for capacitors in [[3.5.2. Equivalent Capacitance]]. There, capacitances add in parallel and reciprocals add in series.
>
>A network made only of series and parallel pairs is solved by zipping and unzipping, as for capacitors:
>- To zip, replace one series or parallel pair by its equivalent, redraw, and repeat until one resistor $R_{\text{eq}}$ remains across the source. The source current is $I = \mathcal{E}/R_{\text{eq}}$.
>- To unzip, go back through the drawings in reverse order. A series pair gives each member the pair's current. A parallel pair gives each member the pair's potential difference. Then $V = IR$ on each resistor gives the other quantity.

# Intuition

A resistor limits the current in its part of a circuit, and it turns energy into heat. Two resistors in series make every charge pass through both, so the current is smaller and the resistance larger. Two resistors in parallel give the charge two paths, so more current flows and the resistance is smaller. The word "equivalent" means that the rest of the circuit behaves the same with the group or with the single resistor. The source delivers the same current at the same potential difference.

The resistance of a uniform wire grows with its length and shrinks with its cross-sectional area, as in [[3.6.3. Resistivity]]. Two equal wires joined end to end act like one wire of twice the length, with twice the resistance. Two equal wires side by side act like one wire of twice the area, with half the resistance.

The network of the Hard example is zipped one pair at a time into $R_{\text{eq}} = 3.875\ \text{k}\Omega$. Then it is unzipped in reverse to find every current and potential difference.

# Derivation

For a parallel pair, connect $R_1$ and $R_2$ across an ideal source. The two upper ends share a wire, and the two lower ends share another. Each resistor therefore has the source's potential difference $V$. The source current $I$ reaches the junction where the wires split. Charge cannot pile up at that point or appear there, by [[3.1.2. Conservation of Charge|conservation of charge]]. Every charge that arrives leaves through one of the two resistors, so
$$I = I_1 + I_2 = \frac{V}{R_1} + \frac{V}{R_2}.$$
Here $I_1$ and $I_2$ are the currents in $R_1$ and $R_2$, each from [[3.6.4. Ohm's Law]]. One resistor $R_{\text{eq}}$ on the same source draws $I = V/R_{\text{eq}}$. Setting the two currents equal and dividing by $V$ gives $1/R_{\text{eq}} = 1/R_1 + 1/R_2$.

For a series pair, call the outer ends $A$ and $B$ and the point between the resistors $C$. Nothing else connects to $C$, so charge has no other way out. The current $I$ in $R_1$ therefore equals the current in $R_2$. The potential difference across the pair splits at $C$:
$$V_{AB} = V_{AC} + V_{CB} = IR_1 + IR_2.$$
Each term on the right is the potential difference across one resistor. One resistor $R_{\text{eq}}$ carrying the same current has $V_{AB} = IR_{\text{eq}}$. Dividing by $I$ gives $R_{\text{eq}} = R_1 + R_2$.

The capacitor rules came from the same two facts, with the roles exchanged. For capacitors, $V = Q/C$ puts $C$ in the denominator, so adding potential differences in series adds reciprocals. For resistors, $V = IR$ puts $R$ in the numerator, so adding potential differences in series adds resistances. The reciprocal rule therefore moves from series to parallel.

# Properties

#### *(Wiring Decides)* Series and parallel describe how elements are connected, whatever the elements are.

The tests use only the wires. A source and a resistor joined end to end are in series if nothing else meets the joint. They then carry the same current. Two resistors drawn side by side are in parallel only if wires join both pairs of ends. A drawing can place elements near each other without connecting them. The middle-point test of [[3.5.2. Equivalent Capacitance]] applies unchanged. Only the formulas differ between resistors and capacitors.

#### *(Proportional Shares)* In series, each resistor's potential difference is proportional to its resistance, and in parallel, each resistor's current is inversely proportional to it.

Series members share one current, so $V_1/V_2 = R_1/R_2$. The largest resistor in a series chain takes the largest share of the source's emf. Parallel members share one potential difference, so $I_1/I_2 = R_2/R_1$. The smallest resistor in a parallel group carries the most current. The EE notes [[2.2h.1 Voltage Divider]] and [[2.2h.2 Current Divider]] turn these ratios into formulas.

#### *(Wires and Gaps)* A wire in parallel with a resistor takes all the current, and a gap in series with it stops all current.

A wire has zero resistance. In parallel with any $R$, the product-over-sum form gives $R_{\text{eq}} = (0)(R)/(0 + R) = 0$. The pair then has zero potential difference, so the resistor carries no current. This is a **short circuit**. A gap, such as an open switch, acts as an infinite resistance. In series with any $R$, it makes $R_{\text{eq}}$ infinite, and no current flows. This is an **open circuit**. Both limits appear again in [[3.7.6. RC Circuit]], where a capacitor acts first as a wire and later as a gap.

#### *(Sanity Checks)* Each intermediate result can be checked against the size rules before the next step.

A parallel result must be below its smallest member, and a series result above its largest. Unzipped values have two more checks. The potential differences along any path from one source terminal to the other must add to $\mathcal{E}$. The powers $I^2R$ of all the resistors must add to the source's power $\mathcal{E}I$. A classroom example used the size check on a network with $R_1 = R$, $R_2 = 2R$, and a series pair adding to $7R$. The parallel combination of $2R$ and $7R$ came out $14R/9$, below $2R$ as required. The whole network gave $R_{\text{eq}} = 23R/9$ and a source current of $9\mathcal{E}/(23R)$.

#### *(Limits of the Method)* Many networks contain no series or parallel pair, and zipping cannot reduce them.

A circuit with sources in two different branches usually has none. Neither does a bridge, where one resistor joins the middle points of two parallel branches. [[3.7.5. Kirchhoff's Loop Rule]] gives a method that solves every circuit of sources and resistors. EE courses add the [[2.1f.2iii Wye-Delta Transformation]], which turns some bridges into series and parallel pairs.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Series Versus Parallel
>Resistors of $6.0\ \Omega$ and $12\ \Omega$ connect to an ideal $18\ \text{V}$ source, first in series and then in parallel.
>
>>[!example]- Find the equivalent resistance, each resistor's current and potential difference, and the power each one dissipates, in both cases.
>>In series, $R_{\text{eq}} = 6.0 + 12 = 18\ \Omega$. Both resistors carry $I = 18/18 = 1.0\ \text{A}$. The $6.0\ \Omega$ resistor has $6.0\ \text{V}$ and dissipates $I^2R = 6.0\ \text{W}$. The $12\ \Omega$ resistor has $12\ \text{V}$ and dissipates $12\ \text{W}$.
>>
>>In parallel, the equivalent resistance is
>>$$R_{\text{eq}} = \frac{(6.0)(12)}{6.0 + 12} = 4.0\ \Omega.$$
>>Both resistors have $18\ \text{V}$. Their currents are $18/6.0 = 3.0\ \text{A}$ and $18/12 = 1.5\ \text{A}$, which add to $18/4.0 = 4.5\ \text{A}$. The powers $V^2/R$ are $54\ \text{W}$ and $27\ \text{W}$. The larger resistor dissipates more power in series and less in parallel.

>[!example] (Medium) Three Equal Resistors
>A kit holds three $30\ \Omega$ resistors.
>
>>[!example]- List every equivalent resistance that uses all three, with each resistor in series or in parallel with the rest.
>>There are four arrangements.
>>- All three in series give $3(30) = 90\ \Omega$.
>>- All three in parallel give $1/R_{\text{eq}} = 3/30$, so $R_{\text{eq}} = 10\ \Omega$.
>>- Two in series make $60\ \Omega$. That pair in parallel with the third gives $(60)(30)/(60 + 30) = 20\ \Omega$.
>>- Two in parallel make $15\ \Omega$. That pair in series with the third gives $15 + 30 = 45\ \Omega$.
>>
>>Each result passes the size test. The two mixed values lie between the all-parallel $10\ \Omega$ and the all-series $90\ \Omega$. In general, $N$ equal resistors $R$ give $NR$ in series and $R/N$ in parallel.

>[!example] (Hard) Zip and Unzip a Network
>An ideal source of emf $\mathcal{E}$ has its positive terminal at $A$ and its negative terminal at $B$. Resistor $R_1 = 2R$ runs from $A$ to point $P$. From $P$ to $B$ there are two branches. One is $R_2 = 3R$. The other is $R_3 = R$ from $P$ to point $D$, followed by $R_4 = 4R$ from $D$ to $B$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the equivalent resistance and the current in every resistor. Evaluate them for $\mathcal{E} = 31\ \text{V}$ and $R = 1.0\ \text{k}\Omega$.
>>Zip, redrawing the circuit after each step.
>>- Drawing 1. $R_3$ and $R_4$ meet at $D$, and nothing else touches $D$. They are in series, so $R_{34} = R + 4R = 5R$.
>>- Drawing 2. $R_2$ and $R_{34}$ both run from $P$ to $B$, so they are in parallel. $R_{234} = (3R)(5R)/(3R + 5R) = 15R/8$. This is below $3R$, as a parallel result must be.
>>- Drawing 3. $R_1$ and $R_{234}$ meet at $P$ with nothing else attached. They are in series, so $R_{\text{eq}} = 2R + 15R/8 = 31R/8$.
>>
>>The source current is
>>$$I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{8\mathcal{E}}{31R}.$$
>>This current passes through $R_1$. Unzip in reverse order.
>>- Drawing 3 back to 2 undoes a series merge. $R_1$ and $R_{234}$ both carry $8\mathcal{E}/(31R)$. So $V_{AP} = (2R)\big(8\mathcal{E}/(31R)\big) = 16\mathcal{E}/31$, and $V_{PB} = (15R/8)\big(8\mathcal{E}/(31R)\big) = 15\mathcal{E}/31$.
>>- Drawing 2 back to 1 undoes a parallel merge. $R_2$ and $R_{34}$ both have $15\mathcal{E}/31$ across them. So $I_2 = (15\mathcal{E}/31)/(3R) = 5\mathcal{E}/(31R)$, and $I_{34} = (15\mathcal{E}/31)/(5R) = 3\mathcal{E}/(31R)$.
>>- Drawing 1 back to the start undoes a series merge. $R_3$ and $R_4$ both carry $3\mathcal{E}/(31R)$.
>>
>>With $\mathcal{E} = 31\ \text{V}$ and $R = 1.0\ \text{k}\Omega$, the currents are $8.0\ \text{mA}$ in $R_1$, $5.0\ \text{mA}$ in $R_2$, and $3.0\ \text{mA}$ in $R_3$ and $R_4$. Three checks pass. The branch currents $5.0$ and $3.0\ \text{mA}$ add to $8.0\ \text{mA}$. Along the path through $R_1$, $R_3$, and $R_4$, the potential differences are $16$, $3.0$, and $12\ \text{V}$, which add to $31\ \text{V}$. The powers are $128$, $75$, $9.0$, and $36\ \text{mW}$, which add to $\mathcal{E}I = 248\ \text{mW}$.
