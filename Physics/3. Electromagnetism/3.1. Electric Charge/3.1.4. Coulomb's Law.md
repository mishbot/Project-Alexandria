>[!note] Coulomb's Law
>**Coulomb's law** gives the electric force between two **point charges**. A point charge is a charged object whose size is small compared with its distance to the other charges. Let charge $q_1$ sit at position $\vec r_1$ and charge $q_2$ at $\vec r_2$. The force exerted on $q_2$ by $q_1$ is
>$$\vec F_{21} = k\,\frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}^2}\,\hat r_{12}.$$
>Here $\vec r_{12} = \vec r_2 - \vec r_1$ is the [[3.1. Vector|vector]] from $q_1$ to $q_2$, and $r_{12} = |\vec r_{12}|$ is their distance. The [[3.2b Standard Unit Vector|unit vector]] $\hat r_{12} = \vec r_{12} / r_{12}$ points from $q_1$ toward $q_2$. The **Coulomb constant** is
>$$k = 8.99 \times 10^{9}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}^2.$$
>The size of the force is
>$$F = k\,\frac{|q_1|\,|q_2|}{r^2},$$
>with $r$ the distance between the charges. The direction follows from the signs:
>- The force acts along the line through the two charges.
>- Like charges ($q_1 q_2 > 0$) repel, so $\vec F_{21}$ points along $\hat r_{12}$, away from $q_1$.
>- Opposite charges ($q_1 q_2 < 0$) attract, so $\vec F_{21}$ points toward $q_1$.
>- The force exerted on $q_1$ by $q_2$ is equal and opposite, $\vec F_{12} = -\vec F_{21}$, as [[1.2.1. Newton's Laws|Newton's third law]] requires.
>
>The law has the same form as [[1.2.2. Law of Universal Gravitation|Newton's law of gravitation]], $F = G m_1 m_2 / r^2$ with $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2$. Gravity always attracts, while the electric force can attract or repel. The constant $k$ is about $10^{20}$ times larger than $G$ in SI units. From [[3.3.2. Gauss's Law]] onward the notes write $k = 1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$, with $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{C}^2/(\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2)$.

# Intuition

Two charges push or pull along the line between them. The force falls off as the square of the distance. Doubling the distance cuts the force to a quarter. Tripling it cuts the force to a ninth. Doubling either charge doubles the force. These are the same rules as for gravity, with charge in place of mass.

The animation moves one charge to twice and then three times the starting distance. The force arrows shrink to a quarter and then a ninth. The dot on the graph follows the $1/r^2$ curve. The last frame flips one sign, and the same-sized forces turn into an attraction.

The vector formula is compact but slow to use. Finding $\hat r_{12}$ for every pair takes more algebra than the physics needs. It also hides symmetry, which a free-body diagram shows at a glance. The vector form is for reference, not for solving problems. Problems are faster with a [[1.2.1. Newton's Laws|free-body diagram]], as in mechanics. Draw each electric force as an arrow along the line between the two charges. Point it away for like charges and toward for opposite charges. Then use the magnitude formula for its length. The only change from mechanics is the kind of force on the diagram.

The constant $k$ is enormous. Two charges of $1\ \text{C}$ each, one meter apart, would push on each other with $9 \times 10^{9}\ \text{N}$. That is about the weight of $600{,}000$ cars of $1500\ \text{kg}$ each. Real laboratory charges are microcoulombs, and their forces are fractions of a newton to a few newtons.

# Properties

#### *(Magnitudes on the Diagram)* Every force arrow on a free-body diagram is labeled with a positive magnitude, and signs enter only through the components.

The magnitude formula uses $|q_1|\,|q_2|$. A negative charge entered without the absolute value flips the force. Every component after that comes out with the wrong sign. The safe procedure keeps two steps apart. First decide each direction from like or unlike charges and draw the arrow. Then compute its length with absolute values. Minus signs appear only when the arrows are split into $x$ and $y$ components. A component pointing in a negative direction gets one. Without the absolute value, a later step can even ask for the square root of a negative number.

#### *(Only the Separation Matters)* The force depends on the vector from one charge to the other, never on where the origin is placed.

The positions $\vec r_1$ and $\vec r_2$ change when the origin moves, but their difference $\vec r_2 - \vec r_1$ does not. Moving the origin does not change the physical situation, so it cannot change the force. This is why the law contains $\vec r_{12}$ and not $\vec r_1$ or $\vec r_2$ alone. It also means the origin can go wherever the algebra is easiest.

#### *(Electric Versus Gravitational Force)* Between two objects of $1\ \text{mC}$ and $1\ \text{kg}$ each, the electric force is about $10^{14}$ times the gravitational force.

Both forces fall off as $1/r^2$, so their ratio does not depend on the distance:
$$\frac{F_E}{F_G} = \frac{k q^2}{G m^2} = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(10^{-3})^2}{(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(1)^2} = 1.3 \times 10^{14}.$$
Two consequences follow. In most problems gravity can be ignored, and a problem says so when it cannot. Also, a net charge in open space does not last long. A region with a net positive charge pulls in nearby electrons with a large force until it is neutral again. That is why everyday matter is almost exactly neutral. Gravity still dominates for planets and stars. Mass has one sign and always adds up, while positive and negative charges cancel.

#### *(When a Charge Is a Point)* Coulomb's law applies directly only when each charged object is small compared with the distances in the problem.

Two charged marbles a meter apart behave as point charges. Two charged plates a millimeter apart do not, because different parts of each plate sit at different distances. An extended object is split into small pieces, each piece treated as a point charge, and the forces added. The note [[3.1.5. Superposition Principle]] adds the forces, and [[3.2.4. Electric Field of a Continuous Charge Distribution]] does the splitting with an integral.

#### *(Notation for $k$)* The Coulomb constant shares its letter with other constants in physics.

The $k$ here is not the spring constant of mechanics, the Boltzmann constant, or the thermal conductivity of [[2.6.1 Thermal Conduction|conduction]]. The context of each problem fixes which one is meant. After [[3.3.2. Gauss's Law]], the notes avoid the clash by writing $1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$. The number $\varepsilon_0$ is called the permittivity of free space.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Two Equal Charges
>Two small spheres each carry $+2.0\ \mu\text{C}$. Their centers are $0.30\ \text{m}$ apart.
>
>>[!example]- Find the size and direction of the force on each sphere.
>>The magnitude is
>>$$F = k\,\frac{|q_1|\,|q_2|}{r^2} = (8.99 \times 10^{9})\,\frac{(2.0 \times 10^{-6})^2}{(0.30)^2} = 0.40\ \text{N}.$$
>>The charges are alike, so each sphere is pushed directly away from the other with $0.40\ \text{N}$.

>[!example] (Medium) Inside a Hydrogen Atom
>In a simple model of the hydrogen atom, the electron sits $5.29 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{m}$ from the proton.
>
>>[!example]- Compare the electric and gravitational forces between them, and find the electron's acceleration.
>>The electric force is
>>$$F_E = k\,\frac{e^2}{r^2} = (8.99 \times 10^{9})\,\frac{(1.602 \times 10^{-19})^2}{(5.29 \times 10^{-11})^2} = 8.24 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{N}.$$
>>The gravitational force is
>>$$F_G = G\,\frac{m_p m_e}{r^2} = (6.67 \times 10^{-11})\,\frac{(1.673 \times 10^{-27})(9.11 \times 10^{-31})}{(5.29 \times 10^{-11})^2} = 3.63 \times 10^{-47}\ \text{N}.$$
>>The ratio is $F_E / F_G = 2.3 \times 10^{39}$. Gravity plays no part in holding the atom together. The electron's acceleration toward the proton is
>>$$a = \frac{F_E}{m_e} = \frac{8.24 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{N}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}} = 9.05 \times 10^{22}\ \text{m/s}^2.$$

>[!example] (Hard) Charge on Two Hanging Balls
>Two identical small balls, each of mass $0.50\ \text{g}$, hang from the same point on silk threads $20\ \text{cm}$ long. They carry equal charges and hang at rest with each thread $10^\circ$ from the vertical.
>
>>[!example]- Find the charge on each ball.
>>Draw the free-body diagram of the right ball. Three forces act on it. The weight $mg$ points down, and the tension $T$ points along the thread. The electric force $F$ points horizontally away from the other ball. In equilibrium the vertical components give $T\cos\theta = mg$, and the horizontal components give $T\sin\theta = F$. Dividing,
>>$$F = mg\tan\theta = (0.50 \times 10^{-3})(9.8)\tan 10^\circ = 8.64 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{N}.$$
>>Each ball sits $L\sin\theta$ from the vertical, so the separation is
>>$$r = 2L\sin\theta = 2(0.20)\sin 10^\circ = 0.0695\ \text{m}.$$
>>Coulomb's law with equal charges gives $F = kq^2/r^2$, so
>>$$q = r\sqrt{\frac{F}{k}} = (0.0695)\sqrt{\frac{8.64 \times 10^{-4}}{8.99 \times 10^{9}}} = 2.2 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{C}.$$
>>Each ball carries about $22\ \text{nC}$. The data cannot give the sign, since two negative balls hang the same way as two positive ones.
