>[!note] Inclusion-Exclusion Principle
>For any events $A_1,...,A_N \subseteq \Omega$ in the same [[1.1 Probability Space|probability space]], the **inclusion-exclusion principle** states that the probability of their union can be calculated by alternately adding and subtracting the probabilities of their intersections. Formally,
>$$\mathbb{P}\Big[\bigcup^{N}_{i=1} A_i\Big]=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\mathbb{P}[A_i]-\sum^{N}_{i<j}\mathbb{P}[A_i \cap A_j]+...+(-1)^{N-1}\mathbb{P}[A_1 \cap ... \cap A_N].$$
# Intuition
Consider 3 events, $A$, $B$, and $C$. You would be quick to say that $\mathbb{P}[A \cup B \cup C]= \mathbb{P}[A] + \mathbb{P}[B] + \mathbb{P}[C]$, just like I have, dozens of times. However, keep in mind that this only holds for mutually exclusive/disjoint events (with no overlaps). For any general events $A$, $B$, and $C$, we can visualize the sample space as

We can quickly infer that 
$$\begin{align}
\mathbb{P}[A] + \mathbb{P}[B]&=\Big(\mathbb{P}[A \cap \bar{B}]+\mathbb{P}[A \cap B]\Big) +\Big(\mathbb{P}[\bar{A} \cap B]+\mathbb{P}[A \cap B]\Big) \\
&= \mathbb{P}[A \cap \bar{B}]+\mathbb{P}[A \cap \bar{B}]+2\mathbb{P}[A \cap B].
\end{align}$$
where the joint probability $\mathbb{P}[A \cap B]$ is counted twice since it is a partition of both $A$ and $B$. This yields an overestimation. As such, we must subtract it, giving rise to the inclusion-exclusion principle.

In the case of 3 events, things become a little complicated, but still understandable. While actual mathematical calculations can be done, they are complicated and I would rather make a sequential list of what's happening. In essence, I partition each event into 3 regions. Then:
1. I add $\mathbb{P}[A]$, $\mathbb{P}[B]$, and $\mathbb{P}[C]$ together. The pairwise intersections $\mathbb{P}[A \cap B]$, $\mathbb{P}[A \cap C]$, and $\mathbb{P}[B \cap C]$ appear exactly once. This means that $\mathbb{P}[A \cap B \cap C]$ appears 3 times. Each event also consists of its own non-overlapping part, as well as an overlapping part that excludes $\mathbb{P}[A \cap B \cap C]$ in order to form a valid partition.
2. I subtract $\mathbb{P}[A \cap B]$, $\mathbb{P}[A \cap C]$, and $\mathbb{P}[B \cap C]$. This leaves us with a gap where $\mathbb{P}[A \cap B \cap C]$ must be. 
3. I add $\mathbb{P}[A \cap B \cap C]$ in order to fill the gap and get $\mathbb{P}[A \cup B \cup C]$.

# Examples
>[!example] A Night in Las Vegas
>Casinos advertise the probability of rolling your number on a fair 6-sided die in 3 rolls as $\mathbb{P}[A \cup B \cup C]=3 \times \frac{1}{6}$. This is wrong, since dice rolls aren't mutually exclusive, and therefore the probability of rolling your number on multiple rolls is counted more than once. Therefore, the actual probability is:
>$$
>\begin{equation}
>\begin{aligned}
>\mathbb{P}[A \cup B \cup C] =& \space \mathbb{P}[A]+\mathbb{P}[B]+\mathbb{P}[C] - \\ & - \mathbb{P}[A \cap B]-\mathbb{P}[A \cap C]-\mathbb{P}[B \cap C] + \\ &  + \mathbb{P}[A \cap B \cap C] \approx 0.42
>\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
>$$
>This can also be represented using the following diagram of a probability space:
>```tikz
>\begin{document}
>\begin{tikzpicture}[domain=0:4]
>\draw[] (0, 0) ellipse (4.25 and 2.25) node[left]{};
>\draw[] (0, 0.5) circle (1) node[above]{$A$};
>\draw[] (-0.6, -0.5) circle (1) node[below]{$B$};
>\draw[] (0.4, -0.5) circle (1) node[below]{$C$};
>\draw[->] (0,0) -- (0,-1.6) node[below]{$A \cap B \cap C$};
>\end{tikzpicture}
>\end{document}
>```
>The 3 events overlap 3 times ($A \cap B$, $B \cap C$, $C \cap A$). Therefore, the pairwise intersections of all events have to be removed. After this, a hole is left in the middle, which is filled by the intersection of all 3 events ($A \cap B \cap C$).

>[!example] Joint Occurrence
>You know that at least one of the events $A_{i}, \space i = 1,\ldots,N$, is certain to occur, but certainly no more than two occur. $N$ is an integer $\geq 2$. Show that if the probability of occurrence of any single event is $p$, and the probability of joint occurrence of any two distinct events is $q$, we have $p \geq \frac{1}{N}$, and $q \leq \frac{2}{N(N-1)}$.
>
>>[!example] Solution
>>We know that:
>>1. One of the events $A_{i}$ is certain to occur with $\mathbb{P}(A_{i})=p$.
>>2. No more than two events $A_{i} \cap A_{j}$ can occur, and $\mathbb{P}(A_{i} \cap A_{j})=q$.
>>3. Since at least one event is certain to occur, $\mathbb{P}\Big[\bigcup^{N}_{i=1} A_i\Big]=1$
>>
>>We proceed using the inclusion-exclusion principle. We know that:
>>$$1=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\mathbb{P}(A_{i})-\sum^{}_{i < j}\mathbb{P}(A_{i} \cup A_{j}).$$
>>There are exactly $\binom{N}{2}$ ($N$ choose two) pairs of distinct events $A_{i} \cap A_{j}$. Therefore,
>>$$Np - \frac{N!}{2(N-2)!}=Np - \frac{N(N-1)}{2}q=1.$$
>>Since the difference equation results in a positive sum, $Np \geq 1 \iff p \geq \frac{1}{N}$. Additionally, $q=\frac{2(Np-1)}{N(N-1)}$. For the minimum value of $p \geq \frac{1}{N}$, this equates to $q=\frac{2(0)}{N(N-1)}$
