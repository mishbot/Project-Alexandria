>[!note] SO Groups
>The **special orthogonal group** of dimension $n$, denoted $SO(n)$, is the group of linear transformations of $\mathbb{R}^n$ that preserve lengths and orientations; it is the **rotation group** in $n$ dimensions, formally defined as
>$$SO(n)  =  \{ R \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} \mid R^TR=I, \det(R)=1 \}.$$
>
>Typically seen, $SO(3)$ is a 3-dimensional group and is fundamental in rigid-body mechanics, robotics, and computer vision.
>
>As a group under matrix multiplication, $SO(n)$ satisfies:
>- **Closure**: if $R_1, R_2 \in SO(n)$, then $R_1 R_2 \in SO(n)$.
>- **Associativity**: $(R_1 R_2)R_3 = R_1(R_2 R_3)$.
>- **Identity**: $I \in SO(n)$.
>- **Inverse**: $R^{-1} = R^T \in SO(n)$.
>
>$SO(n)$ is also a **Lie group**: a smooth manifold with a smooth group operation. The tangent space at the identity is the **Lie algebra** $\mathfrak{so}(n)$, consisting of $n\times n$ skew-symmetric matrices ($A^T = -A$). For $SO(3)$, the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ is 3-dimensional and is connected to the group via the [[1.4. Rodrigues' Formula|exponential map]].

# Intuition
The two defining constraints $R^TR = I$ and $\det(R) = 1$ encode geometry directly. Orthogonality $R^TR = I$ means the columns of $R$ form an orthonormal basis—$R$ preserves lengths and angles. The determinant constraint $\det(R) = 1$ excludes reflections (which have $\det = -1$), keeping only orientation-preserving transformations.

The group $SO(3)$ is a 3-dimensional manifold embedded in $\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$: although a rotation matrix has 9 entries, the 6 orthogonality constraints ($R^TR = I$ gives 6 independent equations) leave exactly 3 degrees of freedom. These can be parameterized by [[1.3. Euler Angles|Euler angles]], [[1.5. Exponential Coordinates|exponential coordinates]], or [[1.6. Quaternions|quaternions]], each with different trade-offs.

Topologically, $SO(3) \cong \mathbb{RP}^3$ (real projective 3-space), which means it cannot be smoothly covered by a single 3-parameter chart without singularities. This is the deep reason behind [[1.3. Euler Angles#Gimbal lock singularities by convention|gimbal lock]]: any 3-parameter representation must have at least one singular configuration.

# Properties

## Useful Identities

#### (*Inverse is Transpose*) For $R \in SO(n)$, $R^{-1} = R^T$. This follows directly from the defining property $R^TR = I$ and makes inversion computationally free.

#### (*Column/Row Orthonormality*) The columns $r_1, r_2, r_3$ of $R \in SO(3)$ satisfy $r_i \cdot r_j = \delta_{ij}$ and form a right-handed orthonormal frame: $r_1 \times r_2 = r_3$ (and cyclic permutations). The rows have the same property.

#### (*Rotation Preserves Cross Products*) For $R \in SO(3)$ and $u, v \in \mathbb{R}^3$: $R(u \times v) = (Ru) \times (Rv)$. This is equivalent to $\det(R) = 1$ and is the reason rotations preserve orientation.

#### (*Hat Conjugation*) For $R \in SO(3)$ and $\omega \in \mathbb{R}^3$: $\widehat{R\omega} = R\hat{\omega}R^T$. This identity is fundamental in [[5.1. Rigid Body Velocity|velocity kinematics]] and [[5.2. Robot Jacobians|Jacobian]] derivations—it describes how skew-symmetric matrices transform under rotation.

#### (*Dimension of $SO(n)$*) $\dim(SO(n)) = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$. For $n = 3$: $\dim(SO(3)) = 3$. For $n = 2$: $\dim(SO(2)) = 1$ (a single angle).

## $SO(2)$: Planar Rotations

Every $R \in SO(2)$ has the form $R(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$. The group is abelian ($R(\alpha)R(\beta) = R(\alpha+\beta) = R(\beta)R(\alpha)$) and topologically a circle $S^1$.

## $SO(3)$: Spatial Rotations

$SO(3)$ is non-abelian: the order of rotations matters. Its Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ consists of $3\times 3$ skew-symmetric matrices, which are in bijection with $\mathbb{R}^3$ via the hat map $\omega \mapsto \hat{\omega}$. The Lie bracket is the matrix commutator $[\hat{\omega}_1, \hat{\omega}_2] = \hat{\omega}_1\hat{\omega}_2 - \hat{\omega}_2\hat{\omega}_1 = \widehat{\omega_1 \times \omega_2}$, which corresponds to the cross product in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
