>[!note] Production Functions
>- A **production function** $y = f(\mathbf{z})$, with $f : \mathbb{R}^n_+ \to \mathbb{R}_+$, gives the output $y$ a firm makes from the inputs $\mathbf{z} = (z_1, \dots, z_n)$, such as labor, capital, land, and human capital.
>- Standard assumptions: *no free lunch*, $f(\mathbf{0}) = 0$; *positive marginal product*, $f'_i(\mathbf{z}) > 0$; *decreasing (diminishing) marginal product*, $f''_{ii}(\mathbf{z}) < 0$. The **marginal product** $f'_i$ is the extra output per extra unit of input $i$, for small changes, with all other inputs fixed.
>- With two inputs, labor $L$ and capital $K$, the **isoquant** for output $y$ is the set of bundles that produce exactly $y$, $Q(y) = \{(L, K) : f(L, K) = y\}$. By the [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]], its slope is the **marginal rate of technical substitution**
>$$\text{MRTS} = \left.\frac{dK}{dL}\right|_{f(L,K) = y} = -\frac{f'_L}{f'_K} < 0.$$
>- For a small increase $dL$ in labor, the firm can cut capital by $|\text{MRTS}|\,dL$ and still make $y$. Isoquants are **convex** when $|\text{MRTS}|$ falls as $L$ rises along the curve.
>- **Cobb-Douglas** technology $f(L, K) = AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta}$ with $A, \alpha, \beta > 0$:
>$$f'_L = \beta AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta-1} = \frac{\beta y}{L}, \qquad f''_{LL} = \beta(\beta-1)AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta-2}, \qquad \text{MRTS} = -\frac{\beta K}{\alpha L}.$$
>- The marginal product of labor diminishes if and only if $\beta < 1$. The isoquants $K = (y/A)^{1/\alpha}L^{-\beta/\alpha}$ are convex for every $\alpha, \beta > 0$, so convexity does not need diminishing marginal products.
>- Isoquants and the MRTS play the roles of [[6.1.4 Indifference Curves and MRS|indifference curves and the MRS]], with the same sign convention. Output is counted in units while utility is ordinal (not taught), so the labels on isoquants matter and [[6.7.2 Returns to Scale|returns to scale]] are meaningful.
>- Production analogues of the [[6.4 Utility Function Families|utility families]] (not taught): perfect substitutes $aL + bK$ with straight isoquants and constant $\text{MRTS} = -a/b$, and Leontief $\min(aL, bK)$ with L-shaped isoquants cornered on the ray $aL = bK$.

# Intuition

A firm turns inputs into output. A bakery with $L$ bakers and $K$ ovens makes $f(L, K)$ loaves a day. The production function is the firm's counterpart of a utility function, with one difference that matters throughout. Loaves can be counted, so twice the output means twice as many loaves. Twice the utility has no meaning.

Two questions organize the theory. The first changes one input. One more baker, with the ovens fixed, adds the marginal product $f'_L$, and each new baker adds less because she shares the same ovens. The second holds output fixed. One more baker lets the firm drop $|\text{MRTS}|$ ovens and still bake the same number of loaves. The surface below plots $y = \sqrt{LK}$ as a hill over the $(L, K)$ floor. Each isoquant is a contour line of the hill, drawn solid at its height and dashed as its shadow on the floor. The shadows are the curves of the usual two-dimensional diagram. Walking uphill in the $L$ direction measures the marginal product. Walking along a contour measures the MRTS.

# Properties

#### *(Diminishing Marginal Product)* With capital fixed, each extra worker adds less output than the worker before.

The condition $f''_{LL} < 0$ says that $f'_L$ falls as $L$ rises with $K$ held fixed. New workers share the same machines, so each one has less capital to work with. For Cobb-Douglas, $f'_L = \beta y/L$ is $\beta$ times the **average product** $y/L$, and it diminishes exactly when $\beta < 1$. The assumption concerns one input at a time. It says nothing about what happens when all inputs grow together, which is the subject of [[6.7.2 Returns to Scale]]. The function $K^{0.6}L^{0.6}$ has diminishing marginal products in both inputs, yet doubling both inputs multiplies output by $2^{1.2} \approx 2.3$.

#### *(Reading the MRTS)* $|\text{MRTS}| = f'_L/f'_K$ is the rate at which labor replaces capital at the margin, in units of capital per worker.

One more worker adds $f'_L$ units of output. Removing $|dK|$ units of capital loses $f'_K\,|dK|$ units. Output stays at $y$ when the two match, so one worker replaces $f'_L/f'_K$ units of capital. The rate is local, like a derivative. The figure shows the tangent line at the bakery bundle of the Easy example, where $\text{MRTS} = -2.25$. The tangent lies below the convex isoquant, so it overstates how much capital a whole extra worker can replace.

#### *(Convex Isoquants)* Along an isoquant, labor replaces capital less and less well as capital becomes scarce.

Moving down an isoquant raises $L$ and lowers $K$. For Cobb-Douglas, $|\text{MRTS}| = \beta K/(\alpha L)$ falls for both reasons. Convexity does not require diminishing marginal products. The function $KL$ has a constant marginal product of labor, $f'_L = K$, yet its isoquants $K = y/L$ are convex. The Medium example shows the reason. Along an isoquant $K$ falls as $L$ rises, and the lower $K$ pulls $f'_L$ down. Convexity makes the tangency point of [[6.7.3 Cost Minimization]] the cheapest bundle on the isoquant.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture used only the Cobb-Douglas production function. The two properties below are extensions.

#### *(Production Families)* The utility families of consumer theory reappear as production functions with the same isoquant shapes.

With **perfect substitutes** $f = aL + bK$, workers and machines do the same job at a fixed rate. The MRTS is the constant $-a/b$ and the isoquants are straight lines. With **Leontief** technology $f = \min(aL, bK)$, each unit of output needs $1/a$ units of labor and $1/b$ units of capital, like one driver per truck. The isoquants are L-shaped with corners on the ray $aL = bK$, and extra units of one input alone add nothing. Cobb-Douglas lies between the two. Each panel of the figure shows the isoquants for $y = 1, 2, 3$. The formulas are those of [[6.4b Perfect Substitutes]] and [[6.4c Perfect Complements]], with $(L, K)$ in place of $(x_1, x_2)$.

#### *(Output Is Cardinal)* An increasing transformation of a production function keeps the isoquant shapes but describes a different technology.

For utility, any increasing transformation $g(u)$ describes the same consumer, as [[6.1.3 Utility Functions]] explains. For production, $g(f)$ has the same isoquants and the same MRTS, since $g'$ cancels in the ratio $g'f'_L/(g'f'_K)$. Its output, marginal products, and returns to scale differ. With $f = \sqrt{LK}$ and $g(f) = f^2 = LK$, the bundle $(4, 4)$ makes $4$ units under $f$ and $16$ under $LK$. The marginal product of labor diminishes under $f$ and is constant under $LK$. Firms sell output, so the numbers on the isoquants carry economic content.

# Derivation

We want the MRTS formula and the Cobb-Douglas results. On an isoquant, $K$ is a function of $L$ defined by $f(L, K(L)) = y$. Differentiating both sides with respect to $L$ by the chain rule gives
$$f'_L + f'_K\,\frac{dK}{dL} = 0, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{dK}{dL} = -\frac{f'_L}{f'_K} \quad (f'_K \neq 0).$$
This is the [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] applied to $F(L, K) = f(L, K) - y$. For $f = AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta}$, the [[2.3. Partial Derivatives|partial derivatives]] are
$$f'_L = \beta AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta-1} = \frac{\beta y}{L}, \qquad f'_K = \alpha AK^{\alpha-1}L^{\beta} = \frac{\alpha y}{K}, \qquad \text{MRTS} = -\frac{\beta y/L}{\alpha y/K} = -\frac{\beta K}{\alpha L}.$$
Differentiating $f'_L$ again gives $f''_{LL} = \beta(\beta-1)AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta-2}$. Every factor except $\beta - 1$ is positive for $L, K > 0$, so $f''_{LL} < 0$ if and only if $\beta < 1$. For convexity, solve $AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta} = y$ for $K$ to get $K = (y/A)^{1/\alpha}L^{-\beta/\alpha}$. Then
$$\frac{dK}{dL} = -\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\left(\frac{y}{A}\right)^{1/\alpha}L^{-\beta/\alpha-1} < 0, \qquad \frac{d^2K}{dL^2} = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+1\right)\left(\frac{y}{A}\right)^{1/\alpha}L^{-\beta/\alpha-2} > 0.$$
The first derivative equals $-(\beta/\alpha)(K/L)$, the MRTS found above. The second is positive for every $\alpha, \beta > 0$, so the isoquant is convex.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Baker for 2.25 Ovens
>A bakery has $f(L, K) = 10L^{1/2}K^{1/2}$ loaves per day, with $L = 4$ bakers and $K = 9$ ovens.
>
>>[!example]- Find output, both marginal products, and the MRTS. The owner reads the MRTS as "a fifth baker replaces 2.25 ovens." How many ovens can she remove after hiring the fifth baker if output stays fixed?
>>Output is $y = 10 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = 60$. With $\alpha = \beta = 1/2$, the marginal products are $f'_L = \beta y/L = 30/4 = 7.5$ and $f'_K = \alpha y/K = 30/9 \approx 3.33$. So $\text{MRTS} = -7.5/3.33 = -2.25$, which equals $-K/L = -9/4$.
>>
>>On the isoquant $10\sqrt{5K} = 60$ we need $5K = 36$, so $K = 7.2$. The owner can remove $9 - 7.2 = 1.8$ ovens, fewer than $2.25$. Removing $2.25$ ovens would leave $10\sqrt{5 \times 6.75} \approx 58.1$ loaves. The MRTS is a rate for small changes. Over the whole fifth worker it falls from $2.25$ at $(4, 9)$ to $7.2/5 = 1.44$ at $(5, 7.2)$, because the isoquant is convex.

>[!example] (Medium) Rising Marginal Product, Convex Isoquant
>A firm has the Cobb-Douglas technology $f(L, K) = K^{1/2}L^{3/2}$, so $A = 1$, $\alpha = 1/2$, $\beta = 3/2$. A classmate argues that the marginal product of labor rises with $L$, so the isoquants must bend the wrong way.
>
>>[!example]- Check the marginal product of labor, then compute the MRTS at $(L, K) = (2, 8)$ and $(4, 1)$ on the isoquant $y = 8$. Is the classmate right?
>>The marginal product is $f'_L = \tfrac{3}{2}K^{1/2}L^{1/2}$ and $f''_{LL} = \tfrac{3}{4}K^{1/2}L^{-1/2} > 0$. It rises with $L$ at fixed $K$, which breaks the standard assumption of diminishing marginal product, since $\beta > 1$. Both bundles are on the isoquant: $\sqrt{8}\cdot 2^{3/2} = 8$ and $1 \cdot 4^{3/2} = 8$. The isoquant is $K = 64/L^3$.
>>
>>The MRTS is $-\beta K/(\alpha L) = -3K/L$. At $(2, 8)$ it is $-12$, with $f'_L = 6$ and $f'_K = 0.5$. At $(4, 1)$ it is $-0.75$, with $f'_L = 3$ and $f'_K = 4$. The size of the MRTS falls as $L$ rises, so the isoquant is convex and the classmate is wrong. Along the isoquant, $f'_L$ falls from $6$ to $3$ even though $L$ doubles, because $K$ drops from $8$ to $1$. Convexity depends on how both marginal products move along the curve, and $f''_{LL}$ alone does not settle it.

>[!example] (Hard) Production Without Capital
>Two firms produce with $g(L, K) = \sqrt{L} + \sqrt{K}$ and $h(L, K) = \sqrt{LK}$. At $(L, K) = (4, 4)$ both produce $4$ units.
>
>>[!example]- Show that both satisfy the three standard assumptions and have the same MRTS at $(4, 4)$. Which firm can produce $4$ units with no capital, and how much labor does each need when $K = 1$?
>>Both give $0$ at $(0, 0)$. For $L, K > 0$, the marginal products $g'_L = 1/(2\sqrt{L})$ and $h'_L = \sqrt{K}/(2\sqrt{L})$ are positive and fall with $L$, and the same holds for $K$. At $(4, 4)$, $g'_L = g'_K = 1/4$ and $h'_L = h'_K = 1/2$, so both have $\text{MRTS} = -1$.
>>
>>The isoquant $g = 4$ is $K = (4 - \sqrt{L})^2$. It reaches $K = 0$ at $L = 16$, so firm $g$ can drop capital entirely with $16$ workers. The isoquant $h = 4$ is $K = 16/L$, which stays positive for every $L$. Since $h(L, 0) = 0$, capital is essential for firm $h$. At $K = 1$, firm $g$ needs $\sqrt{L} = 3$, so $L = 9$, while firm $h$ needs $L = 16$. The two technologies agree in output and slope at one bundle and still differ far from it. They also differ in scale: $h$ doubles output when both inputs double, while $g$ multiplies it by only $\sqrt{2}$, as [[6.7.2 Returns to Scale]] classifies.
