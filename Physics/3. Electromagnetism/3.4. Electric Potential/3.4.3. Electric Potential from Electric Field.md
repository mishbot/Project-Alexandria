>[!note] Electric Potential from Electric Field
>The source charges make a field. The potential difference between two points A and B follows from that field along any path between them:
>$$V_B - V_A = -\int_A^B \vec E\cdot d\vec s.$$
>Here $d\vec s$ is a small step along the path. The sign rule:
>- The top limit B is the final point. Its potential $V_B$ comes first on the left side.
>- The bottom limit A is the initial point. Its potential $V_A$ is subtracted.
>- The minus sign stays in front of the integral.
>
>With this order and sign, the result never has a sign error. The formula gives only differences. To get the potential at a point P, choose a **reference point** where $V = 0$ and integrate from it to P:
>$$V(P) = -\int_{\text{ref}}^{P} \vec E\cdot d\vec s.$$
>The reference point can be any point where $V$ stays finite.
>
>The integral needs $\vec E$ at every point of a path. A value at one point is not enough. The method therefore works when [[3.3.2. Gauss's Law|Gauss's law]] gives $\vec E$ in a whole region. When the field would need slice and dice, the potential is found by slice and dice directly. That takes one integral, while the route through $\vec E$ takes two.
>
>Standard results:
>- A **uniform field** $E\,\hat x$ with the zero at $x = 0$ has $V = -Ex$. Two points a distance $d$ apart along the field differ by $Ed$.
>- An **infinite line** with charge per length $\lambda$ has $V(r) = -\dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\dfrac{r}{r_{\text{ref}}}$, where $r$ is the distance from the line. The zero sits at a chosen finite distance $r_{\text{ref}}$. Neither $r = 0$ nor $r = \infty$ can be the zero, since $V$ is infinite at both.
>- A neutral **conducting shell** with inner radius $a$ and outer radius $b$ surrounds a point charge $q$ at its center. With the zero at infinity, its potential in the three regions is
>$$V(r) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\dfrac{1}{r} - \dfrac{1}{a} + \dfrac{1}{b}\right), & r \le a,\\[2mm] \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,b}, & a \le r \le b,\\[2mm] \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r}, & r \ge b.\end{cases}$$
>
>The potential is continuous across the charged surfaces at $a$ and $b$, where the field jumps. It is constant throughout the metal, where $\vec E = 0$.

# Intuition

The field sets how fast the potential changes along a path. Moving along $\vec E$, the potential drops by $E$ volts for every meter. Moving across the field, it does not change. The integral adds up these small changes from A to B.

The minus sign comes from the definition $\Delta U = -W$. The field does positive work on a positive charge that moves along $\vec E$. That charge loses potential energy, so the potential falls along $\vec E$.

The shell shows how the method handles separate regions. Outside the shell, the field is the same as a bare point charge's. In the metal, the field is zero, so the potential keeps the value it had at the outer surface. In the cavity, the field again matches the point charge's. The potential grows inward at the same rate as near a bare charge. It starts from a different value at $r = a$, so it differs from the bare charge's potential by a constant. That constant comes from the charges on the shell's two surfaces.

A point P moves toward a positive charge and back out. The green area under $E$ from P to infinity equals $V$ at P. Moving against $\vec E$ raises $V$, and moving along $\vec E$ lowers it.

# Derivation

Start from the definition of potential. A test charge $q_0$ moves from A to B, and the electric force $q_0\,\vec E$ does the work
$$W = \int_A^B q_0\,\vec E\cdot d\vec s.$$
The potential energy changes by $\Delta U = -W$. Dividing by $q_0$ removes the test charge:
$$V_B - V_A = \frac{\Delta U}{q_0} = -\int_A^B \vec E\cdot d\vec s.$$
The electric force is conservative, so every path from A to B gives the same value.

For a uniform field $\vec E = E\,\hat x$, each step has $\vec E\cdot d\vec s = E\,d\tilde x$. The tilde marks the integration variable, so it differs from the limit $x$. From the reference point $x = 0$ to $x$,
$$V(x) - 0 = -\int_0^x E\,d\tilde x = -Ex.$$

For the infinite line, [[3.3.2b Cylindrical Symmetry|Gauss's law]] gives $\vec E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\,\hat r$, pointing straight away from the line. A radial path has $\hat r\cdot d\vec s = d\tilde r$. From the reference distance $r_{\text{ref}}$ to $r$,
$$V(r) - V(r_{\text{ref}}) = -\int_{r_{\text{ref}}}^{r} \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,\tilde r}\,d\tilde r = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{r}{r_{\text{ref}}}.$$
The two natural choices of zero both fail. With $r_{\text{ref}} = 0$, the ratio $r/r_{\text{ref}}$ is infinite. With $r_{\text{ref}} = \infty$, the ratio is zero, and $\ln 0$ is infinite. So $r_{\text{ref}}$ must be a finite distance, and $V(r_{\text{ref}}) = 0$ fixes it.

For the conducting shell, [[3.3.3a Conducting Shells]] gives the field in three regions. In the cavity and outside the shell, $\vec E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\,\hat r$. In the metal, $\vec E = 0$. Integrate from the reference point at infinity inward to the point at radius $r$, along a radial path.
- Outside, $r \ge b$, the whole path lies outside the shell:
$$V(r) = -\int_\infty^r \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,\tilde r^2}\,d\tilde r = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{\tilde r}\right]_\infty^r = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r}.$$
- In the metal, $a \le r \le b$, split the integral at $b$. The piece from $\infty$ to $b$ is the outside result at $r = b$. The piece from $b$ to $r$ is zero, because $\vec E = 0$ there:
$$V(r) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,b} + 0.$$
- In the cavity, $r \le a$, split at $b$ and at $a$. The first two pieces give $q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 b)$. The last piece runs from $a$ to $r$:
$$V(r) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,b} - \int_a^r \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,\tilde r^2}\,d\tilde r = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b}\right).$$

# Properties

#### *(Choose the Easiest Path)* The integral is the same for every path, so the path can be chosen to make $\vec E\cdot d\vec s$ simple.

For a radial field, a radial path gives $\vec E\cdot d\vec s = E\,dr$. For a uniform field, a path along the field gives $E\,dx$. Any leg that runs across the field adds nothing, because $\vec E\cdot d\vec s = 0$ on it. Two points at the same distance from a line charge lie on one cylinder around the line. A path on that cylinder has $\vec E\cdot d\vec s = 0$ at every step, so the two points have the same potential.

#### *(Zero Field in the Metal)* Where $\vec E = 0$, the potential is constant, and that constant is usually nonzero.

A common mistake sets $V = 0$ inside the metal of the shell, because $\vec E = 0$ there. The zero field only means that $V$ does not change across the metal. The value of $V$ there comes from the region outside, $q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 b)$. A field given piece by piece always needs the integral split at each boundary. Every piece between the reference point and P stays in the sum.

#### *(No Jumps in the Potential)* The potential is continuous at a charged surface, even where the field jumps.

The change in $V$ across a thin layer is the integral of a finite field over a short distance. Across a layer of thickness $\delta$, it is at most $E_{\max}\,\delta$. As $\delta$ shrinks to zero, so does the change. At $r = b$ the shell's field jumps from $q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 b^2)$ to zero. The potential only changes its slope there. A graph of $V(r)$ has corners at $a$ and $b$ and no gaps.

#### *(No Zero at Infinity for a Line)* An infinite line has no natural zero of potential because it carries an infinite total charge.

A point charge's field falls as $1/r^2$, and its integral out to infinity is finite. The line's field falls only as $1/r$, and $\int dr/r$ grows without limit. The leftover distance $r_{\text{ref}}$ never changes a physical result. It cancels in every potential difference, $V(r_2) - V(r_1) = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln(r_2/r_1)$. A real wire has a finite length. Far from it the potential does fall to zero, as [[3.4.4b Electric Potential of a Continuous Charge Distribution]] shows.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Diagonal Move
>A uniform field of $2.0 \times 10^{4}\ \text{V/m}$ points along $+x$. Point A is at the origin, and point B is at $(3.0\ \text{cm}, 4.0\ \text{cm})$.
>
>>[!example]- Find $V_B - V_A$.
>>Take the path that runs along $x$ first and then along $y$. The second leg is across the field and adds nothing. The first leg gives
>>$$V_B - V_A = -\int_0^{0.030} E\,dx = -(2.0 \times 10^{4})(0.030) = -600\ \text{V}.$$
>>Only the $3.0\ \text{cm}$ along the field matters. The straight path of $5.0\ \text{cm}$ gives the same answer.

>[!example] (Medium) Two Distances From a Charged Wire
>A long straight wire carries $\lambda = 5.0\ \text{nC/m}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the potential difference between points $8.0\ \text{cm}$ and $2.0\ \text{cm}$ from the wire. Which point has the higher potential?
>>The reference distance cancels in the difference:
>>$$V(8.0\ \text{cm}) - V(2.0\ \text{cm}) = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{8.0}{2.0} = -\frac{5.0 \times 10^{-9}}{2\pi(8.85 \times 10^{-12})}\,(1.386) = -125\ \text{V}.$$
>>The point closer to the wire is $125\ \text{V}$ higher. A positive charge released between the two points moves outward, toward lower potential.

>[!example] (Hard) A Charged Shell Around a Charge
>A point charge $q = +2.0\ \text{nC}$ sits at the center of a metal shell. The shell has inner radius $a = 5.0\ \text{cm}$ and outer radius $b = 8.0\ \text{cm}$. It carries its own net charge $Q_s = -6.0\ \text{nC}$. Take $V = 0$ at infinity.
>
>>[!example]- Find the potential of the shell and the potential at $r = 2.0\ \text{cm}$.
>>Let $E_r$ be the radial component of the field, positive when it points outward. Gauss's law with spherical surfaces gives $E_r$ in each region:
>>- Outside, $r > b$, the enclosed charge is $q + Q_s = -4.0\ \text{nC}$, so $E_r = (q + Q_s)/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$.
>>- In the metal, $E_r = 0$.
>>- In the cavity, $r < a$, the enclosed charge is $q$, so $E_r = q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$.
>>
>>Integrating inward from infinity to $b$ gives the potential of the shell:
>>$$V_{\text{shell}} = \frac{q + Q_s}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,b} = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(-4.0 \times 10^{-9})}{0.080} = -449.5\ \text{V}.$$
>>The metal adds nothing. The cavity piece runs from $a$ to $r$:
>>$$V(r) = V_{\text{shell}} + \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right) = -449.5 + (8.99 \times 10^{9})(2.0 \times 10^{-9})(50 - 20) = +90\ \text{V}.$$
>>The potential near the center is positive, although the total charge is negative. The cavity piece adds $+539.4\ \text{V}$. That is larger in size than the $-449.5\ \text{V}$ from the path outside the shell.

>[!example] (Hard) Inside a Charged Ball
>An insulating ball of radius $a = 5.0\ \text{cm}$ carries $Q = +4.0\ \text{nC}$ spread evenly through its volume. Take $V = 0$ at infinity.
>
>>[!example]- Find $V(r)$ outside and inside the ball. Evaluate it at the surface, at $r = 2.5\ \text{cm}$, and at the center.
>>Let $E_r$ be the radial component of the field, positive outward. [[3.3.2a Spherical Symmetry]] gives it in two regions:
>>$$E_r = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} \quad (r \ge a), \qquad E_r = \frac{Qr}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^3} \quad (r \le a).$$
>>Outside, the path from infinity sees the point-charge field, so $V = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$. Inside, split the integral at $a$. The outer piece gives $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 a)$, and the inner piece runs from $a$ to $r$:
>>$$V(r) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a} - \int_a^r \frac{Q\tilde r}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^3}\,d\tilde r = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a} + \frac{Q\,(a^2 - r^2)}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 a^3} = \frac{Q\,(3a^2 - r^2)}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 a^3}.$$
>>With $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 35.96\ \text{V}\cdot\text{m}$, the surface potential is $35.96/0.050 = 719\ \text{V}$. At $r = 2.5\ \text{cm}$,
>>$$V = \frac{35.96\,(3 \times 0.0025 - 0.000625)}{2\,(0.050)^3} = 989\ \text{V}.$$
>>At the center, $r = 0$, the formula gives $\tfrac32 \times 719 = 1.08 \times 10^{3}\ \text{V}$. The potential is largest at the center and stays finite there.
