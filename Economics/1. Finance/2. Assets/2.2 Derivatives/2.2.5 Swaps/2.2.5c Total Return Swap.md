>[!note] Total Return Swap
>A **total return swap** (TRS) is a contract that exchanges the total return on a reference asset for interest payments. Both legs are computed on a notional $L$ (the principal used only to compute payments). The total return is the price change plus any cash the asset pays, such as dividends. A TRS on a stock or a stock index is also called an **equity swap**. Payments fall on dates $T_1, \dots, T_n$, and $\tau_i = T_i - T_{i-1}$ is the accrual fraction (the length of period $i$ in years). Let $S_i$ be the asset price at $T_i$. The swap has two legs, and the second comes in two forms:
>- **The total-return leg** pays $L(S_i/S_{i-1} - 1)$ at each $T_i$. A dividend $D_i$ per unit paid during the period adds $LD_i/S_{i-1}$. When the return is negative, the receiver of this leg pays the loss.
>- **The floating funding leg** pays $L(R_M^{i-1,i} + s)\tau_i$ at each $T_i$. Here $R_M^{i-1,i}$ is a floating reference rate and $s$ is a spread. The rate is set at $T_{i-1}$ and paid at $T_i$.
>- **The fixed funding leg** pays $LR_K\tau_i$ at each $T_i$ for a fixed rate $R_K$.
>
>For an asset with no dividends, the total-return receiver's net cash flow at $T_i$ is
>$$\Big(\frac{S_i}{S_{i-1}} - 1\Big)L - \big(R_M^{i-1,i} + s\big)\tau_iL.$$
>The first term is the asset's return on $L$, and the second is the interest on $L$. An investor can get the same flows another way. It borrows $L$ at the floating rate each period and buys $L/S_{i-1}$ units of the asset. A dealer who pays the total return hedges with exactly this position. The results below assume that both parties can borrow and lend at $R_M$:
>- **The fair spread** is $s = 0$, because the dealer's hedge costs exactly $R_M$.
>- **A positive quoted spread** lets a dealer earn $sL\tau_i$ each period with no risk. The dealer pays the total return and holds the hedge. A negative spread lets a trader earn $-sL\tau_i$ per period with the mirror trade.
>- **The fair fixed rate** $R_K$ equals the swap rate $R_S^e$ of an [[2.2.5a Interest Rate Swap|interest rate swap]] on the same dates. A fixed-leg TRS is a floating-leg TRS plus an interest rate swap.
>
>With $\text{DF}_i$ the discount factor to $T_i$ and $R_F^{i-1,i}$ today's forward rate for period $i$, the swap rate is
>$$R_S^e = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_F^{i-1,i}\tau_i\text{DF}_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_i\text{DF}_i} = \frac{1 - \text{DF}_n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_i\text{DF}_i}.$$
>It is a weighted average of the forward rates. The second form holds when the floating rate and the discount factors come from one curve. A TRS is an interest rate swap with the fixed leg replaced by an asset's return. Like forwards and other swaps, it can be hedged with positions set up once at the start. Options need hedges that are adjusted over time.

# Intuition

An investor who wants the return on an asset normally has to buy and hold it. A TRS gives the investor the asset's gains and losses, including dividends, with no purchase. The investor pays interest instead, as if it had borrowed the purchase price. The dealer on the other side buys the asset with borrowed money. Its gains on the asset match its payments to the investor, and the interest it receives covers its loan interest. The dealer earns the spread, and its remaining risk is that the investor fails to pay.

# Properties

#### *(Uses)* A TRS gives one party an asset's return without ownership and lets an owner pass on the asset's risk.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules list exposure without ownership, risk transfer, and tax as uses. The points on leverage and the balance sheet go beyond them.

The receiver puts up no cash for the asset. So it gains exposure to $L$ of the asset with little capital of its own. This is leverage, the same exposure as buying the asset with borrowed money. The asset stays on the dealer's balance sheet. The receiver records the swap at its value, which is zero at the start. It records neither the asset nor a loan. A bank that holds a loan can pay away the loan's total return and keep the loan. The bank then earns the funding leg and no longer bears the asset's price risk. Swap payments may also be taxed differently from dividends and capital gains on the asset itself. The effect depends on each country's tax rules.

#### *(Not a Bargain)* Paying only $R_M$ for the full return is fair, though the asset is expected to earn more.

Stocks earn more than the risk-free rate on average, so getting their return for $R_M$ looks cheap. The receiver pays for the extra expected return by bearing every loss. In a bad period it pays the fall in price and the interest. The dealer's hedge earns the asset's return and costs $R_M$ in every outcome. So the dealer needs no view on expected returns. The party that bears the asset's risk earns its risk premium (the extra return above the risk-free rate). Here that party is the receiver. A [[2.2.2b Forward Price|forward price]] is set the same way, with no reference to the expected return.

#### *(Funding Sets the Spread)* Each side's own borrowing rate bounds the spread it will accept.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules assume that both parties borrow at $R_M$, which makes the fair spread zero. The band below allows the two parties different borrowing rates.

Suppose the dealer borrows at $R_M + b_{\text{dealer}}$ and the investor at $R_M + b_{\text{inv}}$. The dealer's hedge now costs $R_M + b_{\text{dealer}}$, so it loses money at any spread below $b_{\text{dealer}}$. The investor could instead borrow and buy the asset itself at a cost of $R_M + b_{\text{inv}}$. It gains nothing from a TRS with a spread above $b_{\text{inv}}$. A spread between the two bounds leaves both sides at least as well off as without the swap. So a TRS helps when the dealer can fund the asset more cheaply than the investor. A dealer also asks a higher spread from an investor that is more likely to default.

#### *(Counterparty Risk)* The dealer's hedge leaves one risk, that the receiver cannot pay after the asset falls.

After a fall, the receiver owes the dealer the loss plus interest. The dealer's hedge has lost the same amount, since its asset fell while its loan stayed the same. The receiver's payment covers that loss. If the receiver defaults, the dealer keeps the fallen asset and the loan and absorbs the loss itself. This is counterparty risk (the risk that the other side of a contract does not pay). The exposure grows with the notional relative to the receiver's capital. A receiver with large swaps and little capital can owe more after a sharp fall than it can pay. The collapse of Archegos Capital Management in March 2021 is an example of TRS misuse. It is also an example of poorly managed counterparty risk.

#### *(Variations)* The notional, the assets, and the currencies of a TRS can all differ from the basic contract.

Common variants include the following.
- The notional can change over time. Suppose the notional for period $i$ is $L_{i-1} = LS_{i-1}/S_0$, so it grows with the asset. Then the total-return leg pays $(L/S_0)(S_i - S_{i-1})$. That is the gain on a fixed holding of $L/S_0$ units, as in buying and holding the asset. With a fixed notional, the dealer instead resets its holding to $L$ worth of the asset each period.
- The swap can exchange the return on one asset for the return on another.
- A cross-currency TRS pays the asset's return in one currency and interest in another.
- A cancellable swap lets one side, or either side, end the swap early.

# Derivation

We want to show three results. A floating-leg TRS with $s = 0$ costs nothing to replicate. A spread $s \ne 0$ gives an arbitrage. The fair fixed rate is the swap rate.

Start with the replication. Take one period. At $T_{i-1}$, borrow $L$ at $R_M^{i-1,i}$ and buy $L/S_{i-1}$ units of the asset. The rate is known when the loan starts, so no forecast is needed. At $T_i$, sell the units and repay the loan. The table gives the cash flows.

| Position | $T_{i-1}$ | $T_i$ |
|---|---|---|
| Borrow $L$ | $+L$ | $-L(1 + R_M^{i-1,i}\tau_i)$ |
| Buy $L/S_{i-1}$ units, sell at $T_i$ | $-L$ | $+LS_i/S_{i-1}$ |
| Total | $0$ | $L(S_i/S_{i-1} - 1) - LR_M^{i-1,i}\tau_i$ |

The total at $T_i$ is the receiver's TRS flow with $s = 0$. Repeating the round each period, with a new loan of $L$, replicates every payment. Each round costs nothing at its start. So a TRS with $s = 0$ is worth zero at inception, by the [[4.2.2a Arbitrage|law of one price]].

Next comes the arbitrage. Let the funding leg pay $R_M + s$ with $s > 0$. A dealer pays the total return, receives $R_M + s$, and holds the replication.

| Position | $T_{i-1}$ | $T_i$ |
|---|---|---|
| Replication | $0$ | $L(S_i/S_{i-1} - 1) - LR_M^{i-1,i}\tau_i$ |
| TRS: pay return, receive $R_M + s$ | $0$ | $-L(S_i/S_{i-1} - 1) + L(R_M^{i-1,i} + s)\tau_i$ |
| Total | $0$ | $sL\tau_i$ |

The asset's return cancels, and the dealer keeps $sL\tau_i$ every period whatever the asset does. If $s < 0$, a trader reverses every position. It receives the total return in the swap, sells $L/S_{i-1}$ units short, and lends the proceeds at $R_M$. A short seller pays any dividends to the share lender ([[4.2.1. Short Selling]]), and the swap pays the same dividends to the trader. The profit is $-sL\tau_i$ per period, if the shares can be borrowed with no fee.

Last, let the receiver pay a fixed rate $R_K$ instead. Adding and subtracting the floating interest splits its net flow at $T_i$ into two brackets,
$$\Big[L\Big(\frac{S_i}{S_{i-1}} - 1\Big) - LR_M^{i-1,i}\tau_i\Big] + \Big[LR_M^{i-1,i}\tau_i - LR_K\tau_i\Big].$$
The first bracket is a floating-leg TRS with $s = 0$, worth zero. The second is an interest rate swap that receives floating and pays $R_K$. That swap is worth zero only when $R_K = R_S^e$, the swap rate for the same dates. So the fixed-leg TRS is fair exactly at $R_K = R_S^e$. At inception with any other fixed rate, the receiver holds the value of the interest rate swap,
$$V = L(R_S^e - R_K)A, \qquad A = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_i\text{DF}_i.$$
Here $A$ is the annuity factor, the value today of receiving $\tau_i$ at each $T_i$. The receiver of the total return gains when $R_K$ is below the swap rate.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Two Quarters on an Index
>A hedge fund enters a TRS with a dealer on a notional of \$25 million. The fund receives the total return on a stock index that pays no dividends. It pays the 3-month floating rate plus a spread of 0.25%. The rate is set at the start of each quarter and paid at its end. The rate uses quarterly compounding, so each interest payment is the annual rate times $L \times 0.25$. The index is at 5,000 at the start, 5,150 after one quarter, and 4,995.50 after two. The floating rate was 4.40% at the start and 4.00% after one quarter.
>
>>[!example]- Find the fund's net cash flow at the end of each quarter, and say who pays whom.
>>In quarter 1 the index return is $5{,}150/5{,}000 - 1 = 3\%$. The fund receives $25{,}000{,}000 \times 0.03 = \$750{,}000$ and pays interest of
>>$$25{,}000{,}000 \times (0.0440 + 0.0025) \times 0.25 = \$290{,}625.$$
>>The dealer pays the fund the net amount, $\$459{,}375$.
>>
>>In quarter 2 the return is $4{,}995.50/5{,}150 - 1 = -3\%$. The interest uses the rate set after quarter 1, so it is $25{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0425 \times 0.25 = \$265{,}625$. The fund owes the loss of $\$750{,}000$ plus the interest, so it pays the dealer $\$1{,}015{,}625$.
>>
>>Over the two quarters the return payments net to zero, even though the index ended 0.09% lower. A fixed notional resets the fund's exposure to \$25 million each quarter. The fund's overall loss of $\$556{,}250$ is the interest.

>[!example] (Medium) A Spread Too Wide
>A stock trades at \$80. It will pay a dividend of \$0.60 per share on the last day of the quarter. A client asks a dealer for a one-quarter TRS on \$16 million. The client receives the total return and pays the 3-month rate plus 0.50%. The 3-month rate is 3.60% with quarterly compounding, and the dealer can borrow at that rate. At the end of the quarter the dividend is paid and the stock trades at \$78.40.
>
>>[!example]- Find the dealer's profit with a cash-flow table, and the trader's profit from a quote of the rate minus 0.15%.
>>The dealer hedges by borrowing \$16,000,000 at 3.60% and buying $16{,}000{,}000/80 = 200{,}000$ shares. The total return on the notional is $200{,}000 \times (78.40 + 0.60) - 16{,}000{,}000 = -\$200{,}000$. So the client pays the dealer \$200,000 for the return. The client also pays interest of $16{,}000{,}000 \times 0.041 \times 0.25 = \$164{,}000$.
>>
>>| Position | Start | End of quarter |
>>|---|---|---|
>>| Borrow \$16,000,000 at 3.60% | $+16{,}000{,}000$ | $-16{,}144{,}000$ |
>>| Buy 200,000 shares, sell at the end | $-16{,}000{,}000$ | $+15{,}680{,}000$ |
>>| Dividends received | $0$ | $+120{,}000$ |
>>| TRS: return and interest from the client | $0$ | $+364{,}000$ |
>>| Total | $0$ | $+20{,}000$ |
>>
>>The dealer keeps $\$20{,}000 = 0.005 \times 16{,}000{,}000 \times 0.25$, the spread on the notional. The stock price does not affect this result. Had the stock risen, the dealer would have paid the gain to the client. Its shares would have earned the same gain.
>>
>>A quote of the rate minus 0.15% is below the fair spread of zero. A trader receives the total return from that dealer and pays $16{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0345 \times 0.25 = \$138{,}000$ of interest. It hedges by selling 200,000 shares short at \$80 and lending the \$16,000,000 at 3.60%. At the end the loan returns \$16,144,000. The trader buys back the shares for \$15,680,000 and pays the \$120,000 dividend to the share lender. The swap's return of $-\$200{,}000$ means the trader pays \$200,000 there too. The total is
>>$$16{,}144{,}000 - 15{,}680{,}000 - 120{,}000 - 200{,}000 - 138{,}000 = \$6{,}000,$$
>>which equals $0.0015 \times 16{,}000{,}000 \times 0.25$. The trade assumes the shares can be borrowed with no fee.

>[!example] (Hard) Pricing a Fixed-Leg TRS
>A hedge fund wants the return on a stock index for two years and wants to pay a fixed rate. Payments are every six months on a notional of \$50 million. A dealer offers the swap at a fixed rate of 4.70%. Zero rates $z_i$ with continuous compounding are 3.90%, 4.10%, 4.25%, and 4.35% for 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 years. The floating rate is the 6-month rate from this curve, and the dealer borrows at it. The floating and fixed rates both use semiannual compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Find the fair fixed rate, describe how the dealer hedges, and find the value of the dealer's quote at inception.
>>The discount factors are $\text{DF}_i = e^{-z_it_i}$, which gives 0.980689, 0.959829, 0.938240, and 0.916677. They sum to 3.795435, so the annuity factor is $A = 0.5 \times 3.795435 = 1.897717$. On one curve the fair fixed rate is the swap rate,
>>$$R_S^e = \frac{1 - 0.916677}{1.897717} = \frac{0.083323}{1.897717} = 4.3907\%.$$
>>The 6-month forward rates are 3.9383%, 4.3466%, 4.6022%, and 4.7045%, and $R_S^e$ is their weighted average.
>>
>>The dealer pays the index return and receives 4.70% fixed. It hedges in two steps. First, it borrows \$50 million at the floating rate each period and holds the index. The index return it earns matches what it pays the fund. That leaves it receiving 4.70% and paying the floating rate. Second, it enters an interest rate swap that pays $R_S^e$ fixed and receives the floating rate. The floating payments cancel. With the unrounded swap rate, the dealer locks in a fixed amount every six months,
>>$$L\big(0.047 - R_S^e\big)(0.5) = 50{,}000{,}000 \times \big(0.047 - 0.0439069\big) \times 0.5 \approx \$77{,}327.24.$$
>>These four payments are worth $L(0.047 - R_S^e)A = \$293{,}490.50$ today. That is the value of the quote to the dealer at inception, and the fund holds $-\$293{,}490.50$.
