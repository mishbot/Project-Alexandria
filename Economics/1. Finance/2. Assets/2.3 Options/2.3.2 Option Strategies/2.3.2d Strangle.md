>[!note] Strangle
>A **long strangle** buys a European put with strike $K_1$ and a European call with strike $K_2$, where $K_1 < K_2$. Both options are on the same stock and have the same expiry $T$. The strikes are usually chosen with $K_1 < S_0 < K_2$, so both options start out of the money. Let $p_1$ and $c_2$ be the two premiums. The payoff at $T$ is
>$$\max(K_1 - S_T, 0) + \max(S_T - K_2, 0) = \begin{cases} K_1 - S_T, & S_T \le K_1, \\ 0, & K_1 < S_T < K_2, \\ S_T - K_2, & S_T \ge K_2. \end{cases}$$
>The first term is the put and the second is the call. The payoff is zero on the whole band between the strikes. The strangle costs $c_2 + p_1$ today. Ignoring interest, the profit at $T$ is the payoff minus $c_2 + p_1$. The break-even prices are $K_1 - (c_2 + p_1)$ and $K_2 + (c_2 + p_1)$. The maximum loss is $c_2 + p_1$, for any $S_T$ between the strikes. The gain has no upper limit when the stock rises. When the stock falls, the gain is at most $K_1 - (c_2 + p_1)$, reached at $S_T = 0$. A strangle bets on a very large move in either direction. A [[2.3.2c Straddle|straddle]] with its strike $K_0$ between $K_1$ and $K_2$ costs more. It starts to pay as soon as $S_T$ moves away from $K_0$.

# Intuition

A straddle pays for moves of every size, including small moves that rarely cover its premium. A trader who expects only a very large move can drop that part of the payoff. Moving the put strike down and the call strike up makes both options cheaper. The stock must then leave the band $[K_1, K_2]$ before either option pays anything. So the trader loses the whole premium more often and needs a larger move to profit.

# Properties

#### *(Cheaper Than a Straddle)* A strangle costs less than any straddle with its strike between $K_1$ and $K_2$.

Let $K_0$ lie between the strikes. Calls get cheaper as the strike rises, so $c(K_0) \ge c(K_2)$. Puts get cheaper as the strike falls, so $p(K_0) \ge p(K_1)$. Adding the two shows that the straddle at $K_0$ costs at least as much as the strangle. Now put $K_0$ at the midpoint, and let $w = K_2 - K_0 = K_0 - K_1$ be the half-width. The straddle minus the strangle pays
$$|S_T - K_0| - \max(K_1 - S_T, 0) - \max(S_T - K_2, 0) = \min(|S_T - K_0|, w).$$
This payoff lies between $0$ and $w$ in every state. By no arbitrage, the saving $s$ (the straddle premium minus the strangle premium) satisfies $0 \le s \le we^{-rT}$. Outside the band, the straddle therefore earns $w - s > 0$ more than the strangle.

#### *(Wider Strikes, Farther Break-Evens)* Moving the strikes apart lowers the premium but always pushes both break-even prices further out.

The upper break-even price is $K_2 + c_2 + p_1$. Raise $K_2$ by an amount $h$. By the bound on [[2.3.2a Vertical Spread|vertical spreads]], the call price falls by at most $he^{-rT}$. The break-even price therefore rises by at least $h(1 - e^{-rT}) > 0$. The same argument applies to the lower break-even price when $K_1$ falls. A wider strangle loses less when the stock stays inside the band. It also needs a larger move to profit and earns less on any large move.

#### *(Short Strangle)* Selling a strangle collects $c_2 + p_1$ and keeps all of it whenever the stock ends between the strikes, with no limit on the loss.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules cover the long strangle only.

A **short strangle** writes the put and the call. The seller keeps the full premium for any $S_T$ in $[K_1, K_2]$. A short straddle keeps its full premium only at the single price $K_0$. The short strangle collects less premium in exchange for this wider band. A large rise still creates a loss with no upper limit. Both legs are written options, so the seller needs a margin account.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Profit From a Strangle
>A stock trades at $\$40$. A three-month European put with strike $\$36$ costs $\$1.01$, and a three-month European call with strike $\$44$ costs $\$1.47$. A trader buys the strangle.
>
>>[!example]- Find the cost, the break-even prices, and the profit at expiry if the stock ends at $\$30$, $\$40$, or $\$50$.
>>The strangle costs $1.01 + 1.47 = \$2.48$. The break-even prices are $36 - 2.48 = \$33.52$ and $44 + 2.48 = \$46.48$.
>>
>>At $S_T = 30$ the put pays $\$6$, and the profit is $6 - 2.48 = \$3.52$. At $S_T = 40$ neither option pays, and the trader loses $\$2.48$. At $S_T = 50$ the call pays $\$6$, and the profit is again $\$3.52$.

>[!example] (Medium) Choosing the Width
>The stock from the previous example trades at $\$40$. The trader can also buy wider options. A three-month put with strike $\$34$ costs $\$0.55$, and a three-month call with strike $\$46$ costs $\$0.99$.
>
>>[!example]- Compare the $36$ / $44$ strangle with the $34$ / $46$ strangle: cost, break-even prices, the move needed to break even, and the profit at $S_T = 30$, $39$, and $50$.
>>The wide strangle costs $0.55 + 0.99 = \$1.54$, against $\$2.48$ for the narrow one. Its break-even prices are $34 - 1.54 = \$32.46$ and $46 + 1.54 = \$47.54$. The narrow strangle breaks even at $\$33.52$ and $\$46.48$. Measured from $\$40$, the wide strangle needs a move of $18.85\%$, and the narrow one needs $16.20\%$.
>>
>>At $S_T = 30$ the wide strangle pays $\$4$ and earns $\$2.46$, while the narrow one earns $\$3.52$. At $S_T = 39$ the wide strangle loses $\$1.54$, and the narrow one loses $\$2.48$. At $S_T = 50$ the results match those at $\$30$, $\$2.46$ against $\$3.52$. The wide strangle loses less when the stock stays still and earns $\$1.06$ less on a large move.

>[!example] (Hard) When the Strangle Beats the Straddle
>Use the $36$ / $44$ strangle from the first example, which costs $\$2.48$. A straddle at $K_0 = \$40$ on the same stock costs $\$2.98 + \$2.58 = \$5.56$. The risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Find the final prices at which the strangle earns more than the straddle, and the profit of each trade there.
>>The saving is $s = 5.56 - 2.48 = \$3.08$, and the half-width is $w = 4$. The straddle minus the strangle pays $\min(|S_T - 40|, 4)$. So the straddle's profit minus the strangle's profit is
>>$$\min(|S_T - 40|, 4) - 3.08.$$
>>This is negative exactly when $|S_T - 40| < 3.08$. The strangle earns more only for $36.92 < S_T < 43.08$.
>>
>>In that range the strangle loses its full $\$2.48$. The straddle loses $5.56 - |S_T - 40|$, which is more than $\$2.48$. For example, at $S_T = 41$ the straddle loses $\$4.56$. So the strangle wins only in outcomes where both trades lose money. Outside $[36, 44]$ the straddle earns $4 - 3.08 = \$0.92$ more. At $S_T = 52$, for instance, the straddle earns $\$6.44$ and the strangle earns $\$5.52$. The saving $s = 3.08$ lies below $we^{-rT} = 4e^{-0.01} = 3.96$, as the first property requires.
