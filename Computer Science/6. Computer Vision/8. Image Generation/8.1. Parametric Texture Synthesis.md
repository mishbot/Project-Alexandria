>[!note] Parametric Texture Synthesis
>**Parametric texture synthesis** creates a new image of the same texture as an input sample by matching a fixed set of statistics. The statistics are the parameters of the model, which is where the name comes from. *Analysis* computes them once from the input. *Synthesis* starts from random noise and changes it until its statistics equal the input's. The goal is a generative model of the "infinite texture" that the sample was cropped from. Any image with the right statistics is a valid output. The method returns one member of the set of **images with equal model response**.
>
>Heeger & Bergen (SIGGRAPH 1995) match marginal statistics: the histogram of every subband of an oriented multiscale pyramid, plus the pixel histogram. Step 1 builds the pyramid of the input and of a noise image. Step 2 matches the histogram of each noise subband to the input's (the slides call each subband a channel). Step 3 collapses the pyramid and repeats. The method tests **Bergen's conjecture**. Matching the mean amplitude of band-pass filter responses mimics human texture perception a little. Matching their full histograms (the marginal statistics) should then do better.
>
>Portilla & Simoncelli (2000) show that marginal statistics are not enough. Neighboring filter responses are correlated, since an edge at low resolution causes an edge at high resolution. Their model also matches second-order (joint) statistics: correlations between coefficients at neighboring positions, orientations and scales of a complex wavelet pyramid.
>
>Gatys et al. (NIPS 2015) use a pretrained CNN (VGG) as the filter bank. At layer $l$, the $N_l$ feature maps, each flattened to $M_l$ positions, form a matrix $F^l \in \mathbb{R}^{N_l \times M_l}$. The texture statistics are the **Gram matrices**
>$$G^l_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{M_l} F^l_{ik} F^l_{jk}, \qquad G^l = F^l (F^l)^{\top},$$
>at layers conv1_1, pool1, pool2, pool3 and pool4. Entry $G^l_{ij}$ measures how strongly features $i$ and $j$ respond together, summed over all positions $k$. With $\hat{G}^l$ the Gram matrices of the image being synthesized, that image minimizes
>$$\mathcal{L} = \sum_l w_l E_l, \qquad E_l = \sum_{i,j} \big(\hat{G}^l_{ij} - G^l_{ij}\big)^2,$$
>where $w_l$ weights layer $l$. The network stays fixed, and gradient descent changes the pixels.
>
>All three models use statistics that ignore position. They reproduce stationary textures (whose statistics are the same at every location) well and fail on long-range structure such as wood grain or marble. Adding a content term to the Gram loss gives [[8.2. Neural Style Transfer|neural style transfer]].

>[!warning] Normalization of the Gram Loss
>The slides write $E_l$ without constants. The paper divides $E_l$ by $4 N_l^2 M_l^2$, so layers of different sizes contribute on similar scales. The constant does not change which image matches one layer best. It does change the effective weight $w_l$ of each layer in the sum.

# Intuition

A texture sample is a small crop of something that could continue forever, such as gravel, bark or a brick wall. Copying the crop cannot produce a larger or different patch. A parametric model summarizes the texture with a fixed list of numbers instead. If the list captures what human vision uses to judge texture, then any image with the same numbers looks like the same texture. Synthesis becomes a search. Start from white noise and change it until its numbers match the input's.

The choice of statistics sets the difficulty. With too few statistics, many images that look nothing like the texture share them, as with plain pixel histograms in [[9.3. Texture Histograms]]. With too many, only the input itself matches, and the method copies instead of generating. Heeger and Bergen use per-subband histograms of a [[2.5. Image Pyramids|pyramid]], because each subband isolates one scale and orientation, like the V1-style [[9.2. Filter Banks|filter banks]]. The figure shows their loop. Portilla and Simoncelli add joint statistics, and Gatys et al. replace the hand-designed pyramid with learned [[5.2.4. CNNs|CNN]] features.

# Properties

#### *(Histogram Matching)* Each noise subband is remapped through two cumulative histograms, which keeps the rank order of its values.

Let $C_{\text{noise}}$ be the cumulative histogram of a noise subband and $C_{\text{tgt}}$ that of the same subband of the input. Each noise coefficient $r$ is replaced by
$$r' = C_{\text{tgt}}^{-1}\big(C_{\text{noise}}(r)\big).$$
In words, the $k$-th smallest noise coefficient takes the value of the $k$-th smallest target coefficient. This is the CDF trick behind histogram equalization in [[2.8. Point Processing|point processing]], followed by the inverse of the target's CDF. The map is monotone, so large noise values stay where they were and only the distribution of values changes.

#### *(Why the Loop Repeats)* Editing the subbands and collapsing does not produce an image whose subbands match.

The pyramid is overcomplete, meaning it has more coefficients than the image has pixels. An arbitrary set of edited subbands is the pyramid of no image at all. Collapsing maps the edits back to an image, and the subbands of that image have drifted from the matched histograms. Heeger and Bergen also match the pixel histogram after each collapse. The loop repeats until the histograms stop changing much.

#### *(Marginals Miss Alignment)* Matching each subband on its own keeps how many strong responses there are but not where they line up across scales.

A sharp edge in the input gives large coefficients at the same location in every scale band. Histogram matching treats each band separately, so nothing places the large fine-scale values on top of the large coarse-scale values. The result has soft blobs where the input has crisp boundaries. Heeger and Bergen's own failure cases include wood grain, red coral, hay and marble. An inhomogeneous input (one whose statistics change across the image) also fails, since one set of histograms averages its regions into a blotchy mix. Portilla and Simoncelli restore the alignment by matching correlations across positions, orientations and scales.

#### *(Reading the Gram Matrix)* The diagonal of $G^l$ measures how strongly each feature fires, and the off-diagonal entries measure which features fire together.

The diagonal entry $G^l_{ii} = \sum_k (F^l_{ik})^2$ is the energy of feature map $i$, close to the mean amplitude in Bergen's conjecture. An off-diagonal entry $G^l_{ij}$ is large when features $i$ and $j$ are active at the same positions. It plays the role of Portilla and Simoncelli's joint statistics. Each entry is an [[3.4a Inner Product|inner product]] of two rows of $F^l$, and the sum over $k$ removes all position information. Permuting the positions, for example by a circular shift, leaves $G^l$ unchanged. Its size, $N_l \times N_l$, does not depend on the image size. With $64, 64, 128, 256, 512$ maps at the five layers, the symmetric matrices hold $176{,}640$ distinct numbers in total.

#### *(Optimizing Pixels)* Gatys synthesis backpropagates the loss to the input pixels and keeps every network weight fixed.

The gradient $\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \hat{x}$ comes from the same backpropagation used in training, carried one step further to the image. The paper starts from random noise and runs the L-BFGS optimizer, a gradient-based relative of [[6.4. Gradient Descent|gradient descent]] that also estimates curvature. Different noise seeds give different textures with the same statistics. Every new texture needs its own optimization of many forward and backward passes, so synthesis is slow.

#### *(Synthesis as Projection)* The lecture reads later generative models as texture synthesis with better statistics.

The lecture conjectures that GANs might be learning the "right" features to match for natural images. In this view a trained discriminator replaces a fixed statistic. Diffusion and [[10.3.2. Flow Matching|flow matching]] models move noise step by step onto the set of natural images. The lecture draws both as projecting noise onto an image manifold. Texture synthesis fits the same picture, with noise moved onto the set of images with equal model response. [[8.6. Flow-Based Image Editing]] uses this projection to edit drawings and photos.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Stripes Versus Crossings
>A CNN layer has two feature maps along one image row of four positions. Feature 1 detects vertical edges and feature 2 detects horizontal edges. A striped fabric gives $F_A$, where the two features fire at different positions. A woven grid gives $F_B$, where they fire at the same positions:
>$$F_A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad F_B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$
>Each row lists one feature's responses at the four positions.
>
>>[!example]- Compare the per-feature histograms and the Gram matrices of the two textures. What happens to $G_B$ after a circular shift by one position?
>>Each feature takes the values $\{0, 0, 2, 2\}$ in both maps, so all per-feature histograms agree. A model that matches only these marginals cannot tell the fabric from the grid. The Gram matrices are
>>$$G_A = F_A F_A^{\top} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 0 \\ 0 & 8 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad G_B = F_B F_B^{\top} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 8 \\ 8 & 8 \end{bmatrix}.$$
>>The diagonals agree because each feature has energy $2^2 + 2^2 = 8$ in both. The off-diagonal entry is $0$ for the stripes, since no position has both features active. It is $8$ for the grid, since both features fire together at two positions. The Gram loss between the two textures is $E = 8^2 + 8^2 = 128$.
>>
>>A circular shift turns $F_B$ into $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. The sum over positions does not depend on their order, so $G_B$ stays the same. The Gram matrix records which features co-occur and discards where.

>[!example] (Medium) Matching a Brick Subband
>One subband of a brick-wall texture is sparse. Most coefficients are near $0$, and a few are large where mortar lines cross the filter. Eight of its coefficients are $t = (-3, -1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 3)$. The same subband of the noise image holds $n = (0.5, -0.2, 1.1, -0.9, 0.3, -1.4, 0.8, 0.1)$.
>
>>[!example]- First apply the idea that Bergen's conjecture starts from: rescale $n$ so its mean absolute value matches the target's. Then apply full histogram matching. Compare the two results.
>>The target has mean amplitude $\frac{1}{8}(3 + 1 + 1 + 3) = 1$, and the noise has $5.3 / 8 = 0.6625$. Rescaling by $1 / 0.6625$ gives about $(0.75, -0.30, 1.66, -1.36, 0.45, -2.11, 1.21, 0.15)$, with mean amplitude $1$. The shape is still that of Gaussian noise. No coefficient is $0$, while half of the target's are.
>>
>>Histogram matching sorts the noise values and hands them the sorted target values. The smallest, $-1.4$, becomes $-3$, and the next, $-0.9$, becomes $-1$. The four middle values, $-0.2$, $0.1$, $0.3$ and $0.5$, become $0$. Then $0.8$ becomes $1$ and $1.1$ becomes $3$. In the original order the matched subband is
>>$$n' = (0, 0, 3, -1, 0, -3, 1, 0).$$
>>It has the target's exact histogram, including the sparsity that the rescaled noise lacks. The positions of the large values still come from the noise. Nothing aligns them with the large values in the other subbands, which is the failure that joint statistics address.
