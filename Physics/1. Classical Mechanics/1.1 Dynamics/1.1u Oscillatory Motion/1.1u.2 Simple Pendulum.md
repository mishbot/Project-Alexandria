>[!note] Simple Pendulum
>A simple pendulum is a [[1.1u.1 Simple Harmonic Motion|simple harmonic oscillator]] consisting of a small, massive objet (called the **bob**) suspended from the end of a lightweight cord, which swings back and forth under the influence of gravity. Its angular displacement follows the formula,
>$$\theta(t)=\theta_{\text{max}}\cos(\omega_{n}t+\phi),$$
>where $\omega_{n}=\sqrt{ \frac{g}{\ell } }$ is the natural frequency of the pendulum with $\ell$ being the length of the string, and $\theta_{\text{max}}$ is its peak angle of displacement.

# Intuition
The restoring force exerted on a pendulum when it is displaced from its equilibrium (resting) position can be modeled as
$$F=-mg\sin\theta,$$
where $\theta$ is the angle between the pendulum's equilibrium position and current position. For small angles, the following holds:
$$\sin \theta \approx \theta \enspace \text{ (for } \theta < 15\degree\text{)}.$$
Therefore, 
$$F=-mg\theta \enspace \text{ (for } \theta < 15\degree\text{)}.$$
A swinging pendulum always makes an arc with its equilibrium position. Therefore, $s=\theta \ell,$ where $\ell$ is the length of the pendulum's string (and the radius if hypothetically the pendulum makes a circle). We get:
$$F=-\frac{mg}{\ell}s.$$
This formula roughly resembles $-kx$. Therefore, we can approximate $k=\frac{mg}{\ell}$. Since a simple pendulum follows SHM, we get $\omega_{n}=\sqrt{ \frac{mg}{\ell m} }=\sqrt{ \frac{g}{\ell} }$. This means that the equation for angular displacement becomes
$$\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}+\frac{g}{\ell}\theta=0 \iff \theta(t)=\theta_{\text{max}}\cos(\omega_{n}t+\phi).$$
The amplitude equals $\theta_{\text{max}}$ since it is assumed that SHM starts when the pendulum is at $\theta_{\text{max}}$ before being released for the first time.
