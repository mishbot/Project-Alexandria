>[!note] Black-Scholes-Merton Equation
>The **Black-Scholes-Merton equation** is a partial differential equation for the price of a derivative on a stock. Let $f(S, t)$ be the value at time $t$ of a derivative that pays off at expiry $T$, when the stock price is $S$. Let $r$ be the continuously compounded risk-free rate, $q$ the continuous dividend yield of the stock, and $\sigma$ its volatility. For $0 \le t < T$ and $S > 0$, the price satisfies
>$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + (r - q)S\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial S^2} = rf.$$
>Each term is measured in dollars per year. The right side is the interest that the amount $f$ would earn at rate $r$. A stock with no dividends has $q = 0$, and the middle coefficient is then $rS$.
>
>The equation assumes a frictionless market. There are no transaction costs, taxes, bid-ask spreads, or market impact. Stock can be borrowed without limit at zero cost, and anyone can borrow and lend at the same rate $r$. Each share pays dividends continuously, $qS\,dt$ dollars over a short interval $dt$. The price follows [[7.6 Geometric Brownian Motion|geometric Brownian motion]],
>$$dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t.$$
>Here $\mu$ is the expected growth rate of the price, and $W_t$ is a standard Brownian motion, the process written $B_t$ in [[4.6 Brownian Motion]]. A shareholder expects to earn $\mu + q$ per year, the price growth plus the dividends. The price moves in small steps with no jumps, and log returns are normal, which rules out fat tails (extreme moves that occur far more often than a normal distribution allows). The parameters $r$, $q$, and $\sigma$ are constants.
>
>The same equation holds for every derivative whose value depends only on $S$ and $t$. Write $f_T(S)$ for the payoff at expiry. The final condition $f(S, T) = f_T(S)$ picks out the derivative.
>- A European call has $f_T(S) = \max(S - K, 0)$.
>- A European put has $f_T(S) = \max(K - S, 0)$.
>
>The derivation holds $m = \partial f/\partial S$ shares per derivative, which removes all risk over each instant. The drift $\mu$ does not appear in the equation, which leads to [[2.3.3b Risk-Neutral Valuation|risk-neutral pricing]]. The equation is the continuous-time version of the hedge in a [[2.3.3c Binomial Tree|binomial tree]].
>
>Four changes of variables turn the equation into the heat equation of physics. Let $\tau = T - t$ be the time to expiry.
>- The substitution $f = e^{-r\tau}g$ removes the term $rf$.
>- The log price $Y = \ln S$ makes every coefficient constant.
>- The time to expiry $\tau$ replaces $t$ and turns the final condition at $T$ into an initial condition at $\tau = 0$.
>- The shifted log price $X = Y + (r - q - \sigma^2/2)\tau$ removes the first-derivative term.
>
>Written in $X$ and $\tau$, the function $g$ becomes $h(X, \tau)$, which solves $\partial h/\partial \tau = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\,\partial^2 h/\partial X^2$ and starts at the payoff, $h(X, 0) = f_T(e^X)$. The solution, written back in $S$ and $t$, is a Gaussian average of the payoff,
>$$f(S, t) = e^{-r\tau}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_T\big(e^{X'}\big)\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\tau}\,\sigma}\,e^{-(X - X')^2/(2\sigma^2\tau)}\,dX', \qquad X = \ln S + \Big(r - q - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)\tau.$$
>The weight is the **Green's function** of the heat equation, a normal density in $X'$ with mean $X$ and variance $\sigma^2\tau$. For calls and puts the integral gives the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula]].

# Intuition

A derivative's price moves with the stock, so holding it carries risk. In a [[2.3.3a One-Step Binomial Model|one-step binomial model]], a position in $\Delta$ shares offsets that risk, and a riskless portfolio must earn the risk-free rate. The Black-Scholes-Merton equation applies the same argument in continuous time. The stock now moves at every instant, so the hedge is reset at every instant. Over each short interval the hedged portfolio is riskless and grows at $r$. Written for every pair $(S, t)$, that condition is the equation.

The figure shows where the second derivative comes from. The red curve is the value of a European call with $K = \$100$, $r = 6\%$, $\sigma = 35\%$ and six months to expiry. Holding $m = \partial f/\partial S$ shares hedges along the blue tangent line. A small move $dS$ changes the call's value by about $m\,dS$, and the shares offset that part. The curve bends away from the tangent, so a gap of about $\frac{1}{2}(\partial^2 f/\partial S^2)(dS)^2$ remains. Under geometric Brownian motion, Itô calculus gives $(dS)^2 = \sigma^2 S^2\,dt$, which is not random. So the gap is a known amount per unit of time, and it becomes the term $\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\,\partial^2 f/\partial S^2$ in the equation.

A pricer solves the equation backward from expiry. At expiry the value is the payoff, which has a corner at the strike. With time $\tau$ left, the value at each price is a discounted weighted average of the payoff over nearby log prices. The weights follow a normal curve whose width $\sigma\sqrt{\tau}$ grows with the time left. So the corner smooths out, and the value rises above the payoff near the strike. The heat equation describes the same spreading for the temperature along a metal bar.

# Properties

#### *(Hedge Ratio)* The hedge is riskless only over an instant, so the share count must be reset continuously.

The number $m = \partial f/\partial S$ is the delta of the derivative (the number of shares held per derivative). It is the continuous-time version of $\Delta = (f_u - f_d)/(uS - dS)$ in the binomial tree. After a step $dt$, both $S$ and $t$ have changed, so $\partial f/\partial S$ has changed too. A hedger who resets at discrete times, for example once a day, keeps a small risk that shrinks as the resets become more frequent ([[2.3.3d Delta Hedging]]). A jump in $S$ also breaks the hedge, because the tangent line matches the curve only for small moves. This is why the derivation needs the assumption of no jumps.

#### *(Drift Drops Out)* The expected return $\mu$ cancels, so investors who disagree about $\mu$ agree on the price.

In the change of the derivative's value, $\mu$ enters only through the term $\mu S\,(\partial f/\partial S)\,dt$. The $m$ shares contribute $m\mu S\,dt$ through their price change, and their dividends $mqS\,dt$ do not involve $\mu$. With $m = \partial f/\partial S$ the two $\mu$ terms cancel in the hedged portfolio, and so do the $dW_t$ terms. Only $\sigma$ is left from the stock's dynamics. The same thing happens in the binomial tree, where the real-world probability $p^*$ never enters the price. Since the price does not depend on $\mu$, we may compute it in a world where a shareholder's expected total return is $r$. Dividends supply $q$ of that return, so in that world the price grows at $r - q$. The price is then the expected payoff discounted at $r$,
$$f(S, t) = e^{-r(T-t)}\,\hat{\mathbb{E}}\big[f_T(S_T)\big].$$
Here $\hat{\mathbb{E}}$ is the expectation under risk-neutral probabilities, under which the stock price grows at the rate $r - q$ instead of $\mu$. The Gaussian average in the callout is this expectation written as an integral. [[2.3.3b Risk-Neutral Valuation]] develops the idea, and [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula]] reads the weight as the risk-neutral density of $S_T$.

#### *(Dividend Yield)* A dividend yield lowers the drift coefficient from $r$ to $r - q$ and leaves the discount rate at $r$.

The holder of the derivative receives no dividends, while the shares in the hedge do. That income, $qS\,\partial f/\partial S$ per year, enters the hedge and moves the drift coefficient. The term $rf$ stays, because cash still earns $r$. Consider a contract that delivers one share at $T$ but none of the dividends paid before $T$. Its value is $f = Se^{-q(T-t)}$, with $\partial f/\partial t = qf$, $\partial f/\partial S = e^{-q(T-t)}$, and $\partial^2 f/\partial S^2 = 0$. The left side of the equation is $qf + (r - q)f = rf$, so the contract passes. The share itself, $f = S$, fails when $q > 0$, because the left side is $(r - q)S$. The equation prices only a payment at $T$, and a share also pays dividends before $T$.

#### *(Linearity)* The equation is linear in $f$, so a portfolio of derivatives can be priced piece by piece.

If $f_1$ and $f_2$ solve the equation, then so does $a f_1 + b f_2$ for any constants $a$ and $b$, with the final condition $a$ times the first payoff plus $b$ times the second. A long call and a short put with the same strike have the final condition $\max(S_T - K, 0) - \max(K - S_T, 0) = S_T - K$, the payoff of a forward contract. Their prices therefore differ by the forward's value, which is [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity]] inside the model. For the same reason, a pricing formula for the call gives the put with no new work. The derivation below uses the same property for the heat equation, adding up Green's functions that start at different points.

#### *(Width of the Average)* The Green's function has standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{\tau}$ in log price, so the payoff is averaged over a range that grows with the square root of time.

With $\sigma = 35\%$ and three months to expiry, $\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = 0.175$. About $68\%$ of the weight then lies within $0.175$ of the center $X$ in log price. In prices, that range runs from $0.84$ to $1.19$ times $e^X = Se^{(r - q - \sigma^2/2)\tau}$, the center of the average. Quadrupling the time to expiry only doubles the width. For every $\tau > 0$ the average smooths the corner of a call payoff, so the value has a second derivative in $S$. As $\tau \to 0$ the width shrinks to zero, and the curvature at $S = K$ grows without bound. A hedger close to expiry therefore trades most heavily near the strike. This curvature is called gamma ([[2.3.4b Gamma and Theta]]).

#### *(Self-Financing)* The replicating portfolio never needs new money after the start.

In the replication derivation below, the portfolio holds $m = \partial f/\partial S$ shares and a cash amount $A = f - S\,\partial f/\partial S$ that earns $r$. Its value $V$ changes by the gains on the shares, their dividends, and the interest on the cash,
$$dV = \frac{\partial f}{\partial S}\,(dS + qS\,dt) + rA\,dt = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial S^2}\right)dt + \frac{\partial f}{\partial S}\,dS.$$
The second equality uses the equation to replace $rA$, and the two dividend terms cancel. By Itô's lemma the right side is $df$. A portfolio whose value changes only through gains on its shares, their dividends, and interest on its cash is self-financing, as in [[2.3.3d Delta Hedging]]. No cash is added or taken out at any rebalance. For a call, the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|formula]] gives $A = -Ke^{-r(T-t)}N(d_2)$, which is negative. So the replicating portfolio borrows cash to buy shares.

# Derivation

We derive the equation twice and then solve it. Both derivations start from [[7.5 Ito's Lemma|Itô's lemma]] for $f(S, t)$ when $S$ follows geometric Brownian motion:
$$df = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \mu S\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial S^2}\right)dt + \sigma S\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial S}\,dW_t.$$
The $dt$ term is the predictable change, and the $dW_t$ term is the random change. A share held over $dt$ gains $dS$ in price and also pays the dividend $qS\,dt$.

## Riskless Hedge

Sell one derivative and buy $m$ shares. The portfolio is worth $\Pi = -f + mS$. Over a short interval $m$ is held fixed. The shares gain $m\,dS$ in price and receive $mqS\,dt$ in dividends, so the change in value is
$$d\Pi = -df + m(dS + qS\,dt) = -\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \mu S\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial S} - m\right) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial S^2} - mqS\right)dt - \sigma S\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial S} - m\right)dW_t.$$
The only random part is the $dW_t$ term. Choosing $m = \partial f/\partial S$ removes it, and the $\mu$ term vanishes at the same time. What is left is
$$d\Pi = -\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial S^2} - qS\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial S}\right)dt.$$
The last term is the dividend income of the hedge. This change is known at the start of the interval, so the portfolio is riskless. With no [[4.2.2a Arbitrage|arbitrage]], a riskless portfolio earns the risk-free rate, so $d\Pi = r\Pi\,dt = r(-f + S\,\partial f/\partial S)\,dt$. Setting the two expressions for $d\Pi$ equal and moving all terms to one side gives the equation in the callout.

## Replication

Now build the derivative from $m$ shares and a cash amount $A = V - mS$ that earns $r$. The portfolio is worth $V$. Over a short interval the shares gain $m\,dS$ plus dividends $mqS\,dt$, and the cash earns $r(V - mS)\,dt$, so
$$dV = rV\,dt + m(\mu + q - r)S\,dt + m\sigma S\,dW_t.$$
The first term is growth at the risk-free rate, and the rest is the excess return on the shares. Discounting at $r$ removes the first term. The factor $e^{-rt}$ is not random, so $d(e^{-rt}V) = e^{-rt}(dV - rV\,dt)$, and the same rule applies to $f$. The two discounted changes are
$$d(e^{-rt}V) = e^{-rt}\big[m(\mu + q - r)S\,dt + m\sigma S\,dW_t\big],$$
$$d(e^{-rt}f) = e^{-rt}\left[\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \mu S\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial S^2} - rf\right)dt + \sigma S\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial S}\,dW_t\right].$$
The first line is the discounted gain on the portfolio, and the second is the discounted change in the derivative. If $V = f$ at the start and the two discounted values change by the same amount at every instant, then $V = f$ until expiry, and $V$ replicates $f$. Matching the $dW_t$ terms gives $m = \partial f/\partial S$. Matching the $dt$ terms then gives
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \mu S\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial S^2} - rf = (\mu + q - r)S\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial S}.$$
The terms $\mu S\,\partial f/\partial S$ on the two sides cancel, and what remains is the equation in the callout.

## Heat Equation

The goal now is the $f$ that satisfies the equation and the final condition $f(S, T) = f_T(S)$. Four substitutions turn the problem into the heat equation.

First, write $f(S, t) = e^{-r(T-t)}g(S, t)$, so $g$ is the value carried forward to $T$ at rate $r$. The time derivative of the factor produces $rf$, which cancels the right side and leaves
$$\frac{\partial g}{\partial t} + (r - q)S\,\frac{\partial g}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\,\frac{\partial^2 g}{\partial S^2} = 0.$$
The coefficients still depend on $S$.

Second, use the log price $Y = \ln S$. The chain rule gives $S\,\partial g/\partial S = \partial g/\partial Y$ and $S^2\,\partial^2 g/\partial S^2 = \partial^2 g/\partial Y^2 - \partial g/\partial Y$. So
$$\frac{\partial g}{\partial t} + \left(r - q - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)\frac{\partial g}{\partial Y} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\,\frac{\partial^2 g}{\partial Y^2} = 0,$$
and every coefficient is now a constant.

Third, measure time by $\tau = T - t$, so $\partial g/\partial t = -\partial g/\partial \tau$. The equation becomes
$$\frac{\partial g}{\partial \tau} = \left(r - q - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)\frac{\partial g}{\partial Y} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\,\frac{\partial^2 g}{\partial Y^2},$$
and the final condition at $t = T$ becomes an initial condition at $\tau = 0$.

Fourth, set $X = Y + (r - q - \sigma^2/2)\tau$, and let $h(X, \tau)$ be $g$ written in $X$ and $\tau$. The chain rule gives $\partial h/\partial \tau = \partial g/\partial \tau - (r - q - \sigma^2/2)\,\partial g/\partial Y$, which cancels the first-derivative term. So $h$ solves the heat equation,
$$\frac{\partial h}{\partial \tau} = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\,\frac{\partial^2 h}{\partial X^2}, \qquad h(X, 0) = h_0(X) = f_T\big(e^X\big), \qquad -\infty < X < \infty,\ \tau \ge 0.$$
The initial value $h_0$ is the payoff written in log price. A call has $h_0(X) = \max(e^X - K, 0)$, and a put has $h_0(X) = \max(K - e^X, 0)$.

## Green's Function

Physics texts often solve the heat equation with a Fourier transform. Here we look for a solution that spreads out from one point $X'$. Try
$$h(X, \tau) = \frac{u(\eta)}{\sqrt{\tau}}, \qquad \eta = \frac{X - X'}{\sqrt{\tau}}.$$
Such a trial form is called a **similarity solution**. Apart from the factor $1/\sqrt{\tau}$, it depends on $X$ and $\tau$ only through the ratio $\eta$. With primes for derivatives in $\eta$, the two derivatives the heat equation needs are
$$\frac{\partial h}{\partial \tau} = -\frac{u + \eta u'}{2\tau^{3/2}} = -\frac{(\eta u)'}{2\tau^{3/2}}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 h}{\partial X^2} = \frac{u''}{\tau^{3/2}}.$$
Both carry the factor $\tau^{-3/2}$. Substituting into the heat equation and multiplying by $-2\tau^{3/2}$ leaves an ordinary differential equation (ODE) in $\eta$ alone,
$$\sigma^2 u'' + (\eta u)' = 0.$$
Integrating once gives $\sigma^2 u' + \eta u = c_0$. The choice $c_0 = 0$ gives one particular solution, $u = c\,e^{-\eta^2/(2\sigma^2)}$. Choosing $c$ so that $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} u\,d\eta = 1$ gives $c = 1/(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma)$. Back in $X$ and $\tau$, the solution is the Green's function
$$G(X, \tau; X') = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\tau}\,\sigma}\,e^{-(X - X')^2/(2\sigma^2\tau)}.$$
As a function of $X$, it is a normal density with mean $X'$ and variance $\sigma^2\tau$. Its total area is $1$ for every $\tau$. It describes heat that starts at the single point $X'$ and spreads.

## Solution

The heat equation is linear, so a weighted sum of solutions is again a solution. Weighting the Green's function for each starting point $X'$ by the payoff $h_0(X')$ and adding gives
$$h(X, \tau) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h_0(X')\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\tau}\,\sigma}\,e^{-(X - X')^2/(2\sigma^2\tau)}\,dX'.$$
This $h$ solves the heat equation for every $\tau > 0$, and it remains to check the initial value. As $\tau \to 0$, the Green's function tends to the **Dirac delta** $\delta(X - X')$, a spike that is zero for $X' \neq X$ and has total area $1$. The integral then picks out the value of $h_0$ at $X$,
$$\lim_{\tau \to 0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h_0(X')\,G(X, \tau; X')\,dX' = h_0(X).$$
So $h$ starts at the payoff, and it is the solution.

Undoing the substitutions gives the price. Put $\tau = T - t$ and $X = \ln S + (r - q - \sigma^2/2)(T - t)$, replace $h_0(X')$ by $f_T(e^{X'})$, and multiply by $e^{-r(T-t)}$. The result is the Gaussian average in the callout. The integral runs over final log prices $X'$, and the weight is centered at $X$. [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula]] changes the variable to the final price $S' = e^{X'}$ and computes the integral for calls and puts.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Forward Contract
>A forward contract on a stock that pays no dividends has delivery price $K = \$98$ and expires in six months. The stock trades at $\$100$, and $r = 4\%$ per year with continuous compounding. The value of the long forward is $f(S, t) = S - Ke^{-r(T-t)}$ ([[2.2.2c Value of a Forward Contract]]).
>
>>[!example]- Show that $f$ satisfies the Black-Scholes-Merton equation and the right final condition, and check the balance with the numbers.
>>The partial derivatives are
>>$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} = -rKe^{-r(T-t)}, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial S} = 1, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial S^2} = 0.$$
>>The time derivative is negative because the discounted delivery price rises toward $K$ as expiry nears. Substituting into the left side of the equation gives
>>$$-rKe^{-r(T-t)} + rS = r\big(S - Ke^{-r(T-t)}\big) = rf.$$
>>At $t = T$ the value is $S_T - K$, the forward's payoff, so the final condition holds too.
>>
>>With the numbers, $Ke^{-r(T-t)} = 98e^{-0.02} = \$96.06$ and $f = \$3.94$. The left side is $-0.04 \times 96.06 + 0.04 \times 100 = -3.84 + 4.00 = 0.16$ dollars per year, and $rf = 0.04 \times 3.94 = 0.16$. The hedge ratio $\partial f/\partial S = 1$ never changes, so one share hedges a short forward with no rebalancing. The volatility $\sigma$ plays no role because the curvature term is zero.

>[!example] (Medium) Testing Candidate Prices
>A stock trades at $\$50$ with volatility $\sigma = 30\%$ and pays no dividends, and $r = 4\%$. An analyst proposes three price functions for contracts that expire in one year. The first is $f = S$, for the stock itself. The second is $f = 100e^{-r(T-t)}$, for a zero-coupon bond that pays $\$100$ at $T$. The third is $f = S^2$, for a contract that pays $S_T^2$ at $T$.
>
>>[!example]- Decide which candidates satisfy the equation, and correct the one that fails. Then confirm the correction with the Gaussian average.
>>For $f = S$, the derivatives are $\partial f/\partial t = 0$, $\partial f/\partial S = 1$, and $\partial^2 f/\partial S^2 = 0$. The left side is $rS = rf$, so the stock passes. For the bond, only the time derivative is nonzero, and it equals $r \cdot 100e^{-r(T-t)} = rf$. So the bond passes too.
>>
>>For $f = S^2$, the left side is $0 + rS(2S) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 (2) = (2r + \sigma^2)S^2$, while the right side is $rS^2$. The two differ by $(r + \sigma^2)S^2$, so $S^2$ is not an arbitrage-free price. It has the right final condition but ignores the time left to expiry.
>>
>>Try $f = S^2 \phi(t)$ with $\phi(T) = 1$. Substituting gives $\phi'(t) + (2r + \sigma^2)\phi(t) = r\phi(t)$, so $\phi'(t) = -(r + \sigma^2)\phi(t)$. Solving with $\phi(T) = 1$ gives $\phi(t) = e^{(r + \sigma^2)(T-t)}$, so the corrected price is
>>$$f(S, t) = S^2 e^{(r + \sigma^2)(T-t)}.$$
>>It equals $S^2$ at expiry and is larger before. With the numbers, $f = 2500\,e^{0.13} = \$2{,}847.07$, against $\$2{,}500$ from the first guess.
>>
>>The Gaussian average gives the same price. In log price the payoff is $f_T(e^{X'}) = e^{2X'}$. Under the weight, $X'$ is normal with mean $X$ and variance $\sigma^2\tau$, so the average of $e^{2X'}$ is $e^{2X + 2\sigma^2\tau}$. With $q = 0$, $X = \ln S + (r - \sigma^2/2)\tau$, and so $e^{2X} = S^2e^{(2r - \sigma^2)\tau}$. Discounting gives $e^{-r\tau}S^2e^{(2r - \sigma^2)\tau + 2\sigma^2\tau} = S^2e^{(r + \sigma^2)\tau}$, the corrected price.

>[!example] (Hard) The Call Formula Solves the Equation
>For a stock with no dividends, the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula]] gives the call price $c = SN(d_1) - Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2)$, where $\tau = T - t$, $d_1 = [\ln(S/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)\tau]/(\sigma\sqrt{\tau})$, and $d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau}$. Here $N$ is the standard normal CDF, the function written $\Phi$ in [[2.2c.3 Gaussian Distribution]], and $n(x) = N'(x) = e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ is its density. A trader wants to confirm the formula before using it on a call with $S = \$110$, $K = \$100$, $r = 5\%$, $\sigma = 25\%$, and six months to expiry.
>
>>[!example]- Prove that $c$ satisfies the equation and the final condition $\max(S_T - K, 0)$, using the identity $Sn(d_1) = Ke^{-r\tau}n(d_2)$. Then check the balance with the numbers.
>>Start with the identity. The ratio of the two densities is
>>$$\frac{n(d_2)}{n(d_1)} = e^{(d_1^2 - d_2^2)/2} = e^{(d_1 - d_2)(d_1 + d_2)/2}.$$
>>Here $d_1 - d_2 = \sigma\sqrt{\tau}$ and $d_1 + d_2 = 2d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau}$. So the exponent is $\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\,d_1 - \sigma^2\tau/2 = \ln(S/K) + r\tau$, and the ratio is $Se^{r\tau}/K$. Rearranging gives $Sn(d_1) = Ke^{-r\tau}n(d_2)$.
>>
>>Next come the derivatives of $d_1$ and $d_2$. Both satisfy $\partial d_i/\partial S = 1/(S\sigma\sqrt{\tau})$, and their difference satisfies $\partial(d_1 - d_2)/\partial t = -\sigma/(2\sqrt{\tau})$.
>>
>>Differentiate $c$ in $S$. The product rule gives $N(d_1)$ plus the two terms $Sn(d_1)\,\partial d_1/\partial S$ and $-Ke^{-r\tau}n(d_2)\,\partial d_2/\partial S$. By the identity these two cancel. The first and second derivatives are
>>$$\frac{\partial c}{\partial S} = N(d_1), \qquad \frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial S^2} = \frac{n(d_1)}{S\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}.$$
>>So the hedge ratio of a call is $N(d_1)$. Now differentiate in $t$. The factor $e^{-r\tau}$ gives $-rKe^{-r\tau}N(d_2)$. The identity combines the two density terms into $Sn(d_1)\,\partial(d_1 - d_2)/\partial t$. The time derivative is
>>$$\frac{\partial c}{\partial t} = -\frac{S\sigma n(d_1)}{2\sqrt{\tau}} - rKe^{-r\tau}N(d_2).$$
>>Both terms are negative, so the call loses value as time passes when $S$ is held fixed. Substitute everything into the left side of the equation. The curvature term is $\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \cdot n(d_1)/(S\sigma\sqrt{\tau}) = S\sigma n(d_1)/(2\sqrt{\tau})$, which cancels the first term of $\partial c/\partial t$. What remains is $rSN(d_1) - rKe^{-r\tau}N(d_2) = rc$.
>>
>>For the final condition, let $t \to T$. If $S > K$, then $d_1, d_2 \to +\infty$ and $c \to S - K$. If $S < K$, then $d_1, d_2 \to -\infty$ and $c \to 0$. If $S = K$, both tend to $0$ and $c \to \frac{1}{2}S - \frac{1}{2}K = 0$. So $c \to \max(S - K, 0)$.
>>
>>With the numbers, $d_1 = 0.7690$, $d_2 = 0.5922$, and $c = \$15.17$. The identity reads $110 \times 0.2968 = 100e^{-0.025} \times 0.3348 = 32.65$. The three terms on the left side are $\partial c/\partial t = -9.2984$, $rS\,\partial c/\partial S = 4.2847$, and $\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\,\partial^2 c/\partial S^2 = 5.7720$. They sum to $0.7583$ per year, and $rc = 0.05 \times 15.1664 = 0.7583$. The put satisfies the equation as well. It equals the call minus a forward, $p = c - (S - Ke^{-r\tau})$, and the equation is linear. In the language of [[2.3.4 Greeks]], the three terms are theta, $rS$ times delta, and $\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2$ times gamma.
