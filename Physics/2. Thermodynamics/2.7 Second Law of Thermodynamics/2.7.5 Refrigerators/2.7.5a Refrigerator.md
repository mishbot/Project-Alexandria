>[!note] Refrigerator
>A **refrigerator** is a [[2.7.1 Heat Engine|heat engine]] run backward. Work $W$ is put in, heat $Q_L$ is taken from the cold reservoir, and heat $Q_H$ is delivered to the hot reservoir. Energy conservation over a cycle gives
>$$Q_H = Q_L + W,$$
>with all three positive. The kitchen receives more heat than the food inside gave up, by the amount of electrical work the compressor used. An air conditioner is the same machine with the room as the cold reservoir and the outdoors as the hot one. The [[2.7.2 Second Law of Thermodynamics|second law]] in the Clausius form says heat does not flow from cold to hot on its own, so $W$ can never be zero. The measure of merit is the **coefficient of performance**, what you want divided by what you pay for,
>$$\text{COP} = \frac{Q_L}{W} = \frac{Q_L}{Q_H - Q_L}.$$
>Unlike an efficiency, the COP is usually larger than one: a household refrigerator moves several joules of heat out of its interior for every joule of work. The largest possible value between two temperatures belongs to a reversed [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot cycle]],
>$$\text{COP}_{\text{ideal}} = \frac{T_L}{T_H - T_L}.$$
>It follows from $Q_L / Q_H = T_L / T_H$ for the Carnot cycle, after dividing top and bottom by $Q_L$,
>$$\frac{Q_L}{Q_H - Q_L} = \frac{1}{Q_H/Q_L - 1} = \frac{1}{T_H/T_L - 1} = \frac{T_L}{T_H - T_L}.$$
>Every refrigerator between the same two reservoirs obeys
>$$\text{COP} \leq \frac{T_L}{T_H - T_L},$$
>with equality only for a reversible one. The COP is large when the two temperatures are close and falls toward zero as the gap widens. Real refrigerators reach a fraction of the ideal value.
>
>Every reversible refrigerator between the same two reservoirs has the same COP, for the same reason every reversible engine has the same efficiency. There are many possible engines with many efficiencies. Only a reversible one can be run backward, and it has the Carnot value. Every real refrigerator has a lower COP. The [[2.7.7 Entropy Statement of the Second Law|entropy statement]] at the end of the section says the same thing in one equation.

# Intuition

Take the reservoir diagram of an engine and reverse every arrow. Heat now leaves the cold reservoir, work enters from outside, and their sum enters the hot reservoir. On the $P$-$V$ diagram the cycle runs counterclockwise, so the net work done by the gas is negative. Work is done on the gas.

Reversing a cycle is only possible for a reversible cycle. An irreversible engine cannot be run backward, because friction and heat flow across temperature gaps go the same way in both directions. So the ideal refrigerator, like the ideal engine, is the Carnot cycle, and the two ideals share the same relation $Q_L / Q_H = T_L / T_H$.

The sketch below puts the same bookkeeping on a kitchen refrigerator. Heat $Q_L$ leaves the food. The compressor takes work $W$ from the outlet. The coils on the back release $Q_L + W$ into the kitchen. The inside gets colder and the kitchen gets warmer, by more than the inside cooled. An air conditioner does the same to a room and the outdoors.

A real refrigerator moves the heat with a working fluid. The fluid evaporates in tubes inside the cold box and absorbs its [[2.4.4 Latent Heat|latent heat]] from the food. The compressor then squeezes the vapor, and it condenses in the coils on the back. There it releases that heat plus the compressor's work into the kitchen. The kitchen and the food are the two reservoirs, and the fluid is the working substance.

# Properties

#### *(Never Free)* Moving heat uphill in temperature always costs work, and the minimum cost is set by the temperatures.

From the ideal COP, the least work to remove $Q_L$ from a cold reservoir is $W_{\min} = Q_L (T_H - T_L) / T_L$. Removing heat from a freezer at $-18\ ^\circ\text{C}$ into a $22\ ^\circ\text{C}$ kitchen costs at least $40/255 = 0.16\ \text{J}$ per joule removed. A refrigerator with no power cord that stays cold would violate the Clausius statement.

#### *(COP Is Not an Efficiency)* The coefficient of performance can exceed one, and an ideal value of ten or more is normal.

The heat removed is not created by the work; the work only drives it up the temperature hill. A COP of $4$ means each joule of electricity moves four joules of heat out of the box and delivers five to the room. Advertised ratings quote the COP or its cousin the energy efficiency ratio, and a higher number means a cheaper machine to run. The number falls as the gap between inside and outside grows, so an air conditioner works hardest, and least well, on the hottest days.

#### *(The Kitchen Warms)* A refrigerator with the door open warms the room, because $Q_H > Q_L$ always.

The heat dumped out the back exceeds the heat taken in through the front by the compressor's work. Leaving the door open makes the machine run continuously and adds $W$ to the room's energy every cycle. An air conditioner cools a room only because its hot side is outside the window. Set it on a table indoors and it heats the room at the rate of its power consumption.

#### *(All Reversible Refrigerators Agree)* Between fixed reservoirs, every reversible refrigerator has the ideal COP, and every irreversible one does worse.

The argument is the mirror image of Carnot's theorem for engines. A refrigerator with a better COP than the Carnot value could be paired with a Carnot engine to move heat from cold to hot with no net work. The reversible refrigerator is the Carnot cycle run backward, so it inherits the Carnot relation $Q_L/Q_H = T_L/T_H$, and the ideal COP follows from it. Once [[2.7.6 Entropy|entropy]] is defined, the same relation becomes the statement that a reversible refrigerator leaves the total entropy unchanged.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Ideal COP of a Kitchen Refrigerator
>The inside of a refrigerator is at $4\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and the kitchen is at $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the ideal coefficient of performance.
>>In kelvins, $T_L = 277\ \text{K}$ and $T_H = 298\ \text{K}$,
>>$$\text{COP}_{\text{ideal}} = \frac{T_L}{T_H - T_L} = \frac{277}{21} = 13.$$
>>An ideal machine would move $13\ \text{J}$ of heat out of the box for each joule of work. Real refrigerators manage about $3$ to $5$, because the working fluid must be colder than $4\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to absorb heat and hotter than $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to reject it, which widens the effective temperature gap.

>[!example] (Medium) Heat Delivered to the Room
>A refrigerator with a COP of $4.0$ removes $500\ \text{J}$ of heat from its interior per cycle.
>
>>[!example]- Find the work per cycle and the heat delivered to the kitchen.
>>From the definition, $W = Q_L / \text{COP} = 500 / 4.0 = 125\ \text{J}$. Energy conservation gives
>>$$Q_H = Q_L + W = 500 + 125 = 625\ \text{J}.$$
>>The kitchen receives $625\ \text{J}$ per cycle, more than the $500\ \text{J}$ the food lost. The extra $125\ \text{J}$ is the electrical energy the compressor turned into heat.

>[!example] (Hard) Sizing an Air Conditioner
>An air conditioner must remove heat from a room at $22\ ^\circ\text{C}$ at a rate of $5.0\ \text{kW}$ and reject it outdoors at $35\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>

>
>>[!example]- Find the minimum electrical power required, and the power for a real unit with a COP of $3.0$.
>>The ideal COP is $T_L / (T_H - T_L) = 295 / 13 = 22.7$, so the least power is
>>$$P_{\min} = \frac{5000\ \text{W}}{22.7} = 220\ \text{W}.$$
>>A real unit with COP $3.0$ draws $5000 / 3.0 = 1.7\ \text{kW}$, about eight times the ideal, and dumps $6.7\ \text{kW}$ outdoors. The ideal number is far from reach, because reaching it would require the refrigerant to exchange heat across vanishing temperature differences and therefore infinitely slowly. On a $40\ ^\circ\text{C}$ day the ideal COP falls to $295/18 = 16$, and a real unit's COP falls with it.
