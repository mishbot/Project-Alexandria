>[!note] Electrostatic Potential Energy of a System of Charges
>The **electrostatic potential energy** of a group of point charges is the work an outside agent does to assemble it. The charges start at rest and infinitely far apart. The agent brings them in slowly, so they gain no kinetic energy. For two charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ a distance $r_{12}$ apart,
>$$U = \frac{q_1 q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r_{12}}.$$
>The energy is zero when the charges are infinitely far apart. Its sign has a meaning:
>- $U > 0$ for like charges. Work is needed to push them together. Released, they move apart and speed up.
>- $U < 0$ for unlike charges. Work is needed to pull them apart, so the pair is bound.
>
>For several charges, $U$ is a sum over every pair, with each pair counted once:
>$$U = \sum_{i < j}\frac{q_i q_j}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r_{ij}}.$$
>Here $r_{ij}$ is the distance between charges $q_i$ and $q_j$. Three charges have three pairs, $U = U_{12} + U_{13} + U_{23}$. The order of assembly does not change the total.
>
>The pair formula is $U = qV$ from [[3.4.2. Electric Potential]]. The charge $q_1$ makes the potential $V_1 = q_1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_{12})$ at the spot where $q_2$ ends up. Bringing $q_2$ there from infinity takes the work $q_2 V_1$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover the potential energy of a group of charges. This note follows Giancoli section 23.8.

# Intuition

Each charge already in place makes a potential. A new charge brought in from infinity changes its potential energy by its charge times that potential. An agent moving it slowly supplies exactly that energy. After all the charges arrive, the stored energy is the sum of these steps. Each pair contributes once, when its second member arrives.

The three charges from the Medium example arrive one at a time. Each new pair adds its term $q_iq_j/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_{ij})$ to a running total. A bar chart builds the total $U$.

# Derivation

Bring the charges in one at a time. The first charge, $q_1$, moves through empty space with no field, so it needs no work. The second charge, $q_2$, comes in from infinity to a point where $q_1$ makes the potential $V_1 = q_1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_{12})$. It moves slowly, so its kinetic energy stays zero. By the [[1.3c Work-Energy Principle|work-energy principle]], the agent's work equals the rise in potential energy:
$$W_2 = q_2 V_1 = \frac{q_1 q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r_{12}}.$$
The third charge, $q_3$, moves in the potentials of both charges already in place:
$$W_3 = q_3\left(\frac{q_1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r_{13}} + \frac{q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r_{23}}\right).$$
The total $W_2 + W_3$ contains each of the three pairs once. A fourth charge adds three more pairs, and so on.

# Properties

#### *(Count Each Pair Once)* Adding $q_i V_i$ over all the charges counts every pair twice.

Let $V_i$ be the potential at charge $q_i$ due to all the other charges. The sum $\sum_i q_i V_i$ contains $q_1 q_2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_{12})$ once from charge 1 and again from charge 2. The correct total is half of it, $U = \frac{1}{2}\sum_i q_i V_i$. The pair sum avoids the factor $\frac{1}{2}$ by listing each pair a single time.

#### *(Energy Released on Separation)* Released charges turn their potential energy into kinetic energy.

Two like charges released from rest move apart. When they are far apart, their total kinetic energy equals the starting $U$. Two unlike charges cannot separate on their own, since that would need a negative kinetic energy. An outside agent must supply at least $|U|$ to pull them apart. That amount is the pair's binding energy.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Bound Pair
>Charges of $+3.0\ \text{nC}$ and $-5.0\ \text{nC}$ sit $4.0\ \text{cm}$ apart.
>
>>[!example]- Find their electrostatic potential energy.
>>The pair formula gives
>>$$U = \frac{q_1 q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r_{12}} = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(3.0 \times 10^{-9})(-5.0 \times 10^{-9})}{0.040} = -3.4 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{J}.$$
>>The energy is negative, so the pair is bound. Separating them to a large distance takes $3.4 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{J}$ of work.

>[!example] (Medium) Three Charges on a Triangle
>Charges of $+3.0\ \text{nC}$, $+3.0\ \text{nC}$, and $-2.0\ \text{nC}$ sit at the corners of an equilateral triangle with sides of $10\ \text{cm}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the work needed to assemble them from far apart.
>>All three pairs are $0.10\ \text{m}$ apart. The products of the charges are $(3.0)(3.0) = 9.0$, $(3.0)(-2.0) = -6.0$, and $(3.0)(-2.0) = -6.0$, in $\text{nC}^2$. Their sum is $-3.0\ \text{nC}^2$, so
>>$$U = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(-3.0 \times 10^{-18})}{0.10} = -2.7 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{J}.$$
>>The negative charge pulls on both positive ones. The agent does negative work, so the assembly releases $2.7 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{J}$ overall.

>[!example] (Hard) Closest Approach to a Nucleus
>An alpha particle, with charge $2e$, heads straight at a gold nucleus, with charge $79e$. Far away it has a kinetic energy of $8.0 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J}$. Treat the heavy nucleus as fixed.
>
>>[!example]- How close does the alpha particle get?
>>The alpha particle slows as it approaches and stops for an instant at the closest distance $r$. All its kinetic energy has then become potential energy:
>>$$K = \frac{(2e)(79e)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r}.$$
>>Solving for $r$,
>>$$r = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(158)(1.602 \times 10^{-19})^2}{8.0 \times 10^{-13}} = 4.6 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{m}.$$
>>That distance is about $2000$ times smaller than an atom, which is about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ across.
