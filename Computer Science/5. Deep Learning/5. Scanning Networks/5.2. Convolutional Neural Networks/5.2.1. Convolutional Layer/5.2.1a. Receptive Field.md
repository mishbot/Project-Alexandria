>[!note] Receptive Field
>The **receptive field** of a unit is the region of the original input that can affect it. A unit in a first [[5.2.1. Convolutional Layer|convolutional layer]] with a $K \times K$ kernel sees $K \times K$ pixels. Each later layer looks at a neighborhood of the layer below, so the receptive field grows with depth. Along one axis, start from $r_0 = 1$ and $j_0 = 1$. A layer with kernel size $K_\ell$, dilation $d_\ell$ and stride $s_\ell$ updates them as
>$$r_\ell = r_{\ell-1} + (K_\ell - 1)\, d_\ell\, j_{\ell-1}, \qquad j_\ell = j_{\ell-1}\, s_\ell.$$
>Here $r_\ell$ is the receptive field width of layer $\ell$. The **jump** $j_\ell$ is the spacing between its neighboring units, measured in input pixels. A [[5.2.2. Pooling|pooling]] layer with window $K$ and stride $s$ follows the same rule with $d = 1$. The receptive field grows in three ways:
>- **Depth**: $L$ stride-1 layers with kernel size $K$ give $r_L = L(K-1)+1$, which grows linearly in $L$.
>- **Downsampling**: a stride or a pooling layer multiplies $j$, so every later layer adds more input pixels.
>- **Dilation**: a kernel with dilation $d$ covers $d(K-1)+1$ inputs with only $K$ weights.

# Intuition

To recognize a dog, a unit must see the whole dog, or at least enough of it. A first-layer unit with a $3 \times 3$ kernel sees nine pixels, enough for an edge but not for an ear. Each layer looks at a neighborhood of the layer below, so the windows nest. In the figure a unit in $\mathbf{H}_2$ sees three units of $\mathbf{H}_1$, which together see five inputs. Units deep in a network see most of the image even though every kernel is small.

Growing the window one layer at a time is slow. A $224 \times 224$ image would need more than a hundred stride-1 $3 \times 3$ layers before one unit could see all of it. Downsampling fixes this. After a stride-2 layer, neighboring units sit two pixels apart. The next kernel then reaches twice as far with the same number of weights.

# Properties

#### *(Downsampling Multiplies Growth)* Stride and pooling multiply the rate at which the receptive field grows, while kernel size and dilation only add to it.

The update adds $(K-1)\,d\,j$ at each layer and multiplies $j$ by the stride. Without downsampling $j$ stays at $1$, and each $3 \times 3$ layer adds only $2$ pixels. A $2 \times 2$ pooling with stride $2$ adds only $j$ itself, but it doubles $j$ for every layer that follows. After $m$ stride-2 layers or poolings, $j = 2^m$, and each later $3 \times 3$ convolution adds $2^{m+1}$ pixels. This is why the receptive field of a CNN grows roughly exponentially in the number of downsampling stages.

#### *(Stacked Small Kernels)* Two stacked $3 \times 3$ layers see the same $5 \times 5$ window as one $5 \times 5$ layer, with fewer weights and an extra nonlinearity.

With $C$ channels in and out, a single $5 \times 5$ layer has $25\, C^2$ weights. Two $3 \times 3$ layers have $2 \cdot 9\, C^2 = 18\, C^2$. Three $3 \times 3$ layers reach $7 \times 7$ with $27\, C^2$ weights instead of $49\, C^2$. The stack also puts a ReLU between the layers. It can therefore compute functions that one filter followed by one ReLU cannot. The VGG networks in [[5.2.4. CNNs]] are built on this observation. A dilated $3 \times 3$ kernel with $d = 2$ reaches the same $5 \times 5$ window with nine weights. It skips every other pixel inside that window.

#### *(The Window Is an Upper Bound)* The receptive field marks the inputs that can matter, and two effects shrink the part that does matter.

The first effect is the border. Near the edge of the image, padding fills part of the window. Edge units therefore see less real image than central units. The second effect concerns paths through the network. Every layer of a stack offers several routes from an input pixel to the output unit. A central pixel reaches the unit through many routes, while a pixel at the edge of the window reaches it through one. The influence is therefore concentrated near the center, a pattern known as the **effective receptive field**.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The effective receptive field comes from Luo et al. (2016), not from lecture or Prince.

Take $L$ stride-1 layers whose kernels are all $[1, 1, 1]$. The number of routes from input offset $t$ is the coefficient of $z^t$ in $(1 + z + z^2)^L$. These counts approach a Gaussian with standard deviation $\sqrt{2L/3}$, which grows like $\sqrt{L}$ while the window grows like $L$. With $L = 10$ the window is $21$ pixels wide, yet the central $11$ pixels carry $97\%$ of the routes.

# Examples

>[!example] (Medium) Receptive Field of a Stack
>A network applies four convolutions in order: $3 \times 3$ with stride $1$, $3 \times 3$ with stride $2$, $3 \times 3$ with dilation $2$ and stride $1$, and $5 \times 5$ with stride $1$.
>
>>[!example]- What is the receptive field of a unit in the last layer, and how many plain stride-1 $3 \times 3$ layers would give the same receptive field?
>>Apply $r \leftarrow r + (K-1)\,d\,j$ and then $j \leftarrow j\,s$, starting from $r = j = 1$.
>>
>>| layer | $K$ | $d$ | $s$ | $r$ | $j$ |
>>|---|---|---|---|---|---|
>>| 1 | $3$ | $1$ | $1$ | $1 + 2 = 3$ | $1$ |
>>| 2 | $3$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3 + 2 = 5$ | $2$ |
>>| 3 | $3$ | $2$ | $1$ | $5 + 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 13$ | $2$ |
>>| 4 | $5$ | $1$ | $1$ | $13 + 4 \cdot 2 = 21$ | $2$ |
>>
>>The last layer sees a $21 \times 21$ window. Plain stride-1 $3 \times 3$ layers add $2$ each, so they would need $(21 - 1)/2 = 10$ layers, all at full resolution.

>[!example] (Medium) Two Linear Layers as One
>Two stride-1 1D convolutions with kernel $[1, 1, 1]$, no bias and no activation are applied in a row.
>
>>[!example]- Which single kernel computes the same function, and what does it say about the receptive field?
>>The first layer gives $h[r] = x[r-1] + x[r] + x[r+1]$. The second sums three neighboring $h$ values:
>>$$h[r-1] + h[r] + h[r+1] = x[r-2] + 2x[r-1] + 3x[r] + 2x[r+1] + x[r+2].$$
>>The stack equals one cross-correlation with the kernel $[1, 2, 3, 2, 1]$. This kernel is the convolution $\boldsymbol{\omega} * \boldsymbol{\omega}$ of the two kernels. Its width is $5 = 2(3-1)+1$, matching the receptive field formula. The weights show the effective receptive field: the center pixel counts three times as much as either end. Without a nonlinearity in between, the two layers add no expressive power over a single $5$-tap layer.

>[!example] (Hard) Designing for a Target Receptive Field
>A network for $64 \times 64$ images must have units whose receptive field covers at least $64$ pixels along each axis. It uses only $3 \times 3$ convolutions with stride $1$ and $2 \times 2$ max poolings with stride $2$.
>
>>[!example]- Compare a network with no pooling to one that pools after every two convolutions.
>>Without pooling, $r_L = 2L + 1 \ge 64$ needs $L = 32$ layers, all at $64 \times 64$ resolution. With pooling, each stage of two convolutions and one pooling updates the receptive field as follows:
>>
>>| stage | after two convs | after pooling | $j$ after pooling |
>>|---|---|---|---|
>>| 1 | $1 + 2 + 2 = 5$ | $5 + 1 = 6$ | $2$ |
>>| 2 | $6 + 4 + 4 = 14$ | $14 + 2 = 16$ | $4$ |
>>| 3 | $16 + 8 + 8 = 32$ | $32 + 4 = 36$ | $8$ |
>>| 4 | $36 + 16 + 16 = 68$ | | |
>>
>>The two convolutions of stage $4$ reach $r = 68$ after eight convolutions and three poolings. The design needs a quarter of the convolutions. Most of them also run at low resolution, down to $8 \times 8$ in the last stage. They cost far less than full-resolution layers.
