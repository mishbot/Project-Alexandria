>[!note] Manipulator Equations of Motion
>For any mechanical system whose kinetic energy is a quadratic form in joint velocities, $T = \tfrac12\,\dot q^\top M(q)\,\dot q$ — which holds for every robot built from rigid links — the [[6.1. Lagrangian Dynamics|Euler–Lagrange equations]] collapse into the **standard manipulator form**:
>$$\Upsilon = \underbrace{M(q)\,\ddot q}_{\text{inertia}} + \underbrace{C(q,\dot q)\,\dot q}_{\text{velocity terms}} + \underbrace{G(q)}_{\text{gravity \& springs}},$$
>where $q\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is the joint vector, $\Upsilon\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is the joint torque/force vector, and the three coefficient objects are:
>- $M(q)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ — the **system inertia matrix**, symmetric and positive definite. It captures the aggregate mass and rotational inertia of all links and depends on configuration $q$ but not velocity. For a serial chain with body Jacobians $J_i^b(q)$,
>$$M(q) = \sum_{i=1}^n J_i^b(q)^\top\,\mathcal{M}_i\,J_i^b(q),$$
>where $\mathcal{M}_i$ is the $6\times 6$ generalized inertia of link $i$ from [[6.2. Kinetic Energy of a Rigid Body]].
>- $C(q,\dot q)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ — the **Coriolis matrix**, capturing all velocity-dependent fictitious forces. Diagonal contributions ($\dot q_i^2$) are centrifugal; off-diagonal cross terms ($\dot q_i\dot q_j$, $i\neq j$) are Coriolis proper. The standard formula uses **Christoffel symbols of the first kind**:
>$$C_{ij}(q,\dot q) = \sum_{k=1}^n \tfrac12\!\left(\frac{\partial M_{ij}}{\partial q_k} + \frac{\partial M_{ik}}{\partial q_j} - \frac{\partial M_{jk}}{\partial q_i}\right)\dot q_k.$$
>- $G(q)\in\mathbb{R}^n$ — the **gravity vector**, derived purely from potential energy: $G(q) = \partial V/\partial q$. It captures gravity and conservative spring forces and depends on $q$ but not $\dot q$.
>
>The standard form is the workhorse of robot dynamics and control: it cleanly separates the three physical contributions, and each piece has a different role in computed-torque control, gravity compensation, and trajectory planning.
>
>>[!warning] $M(q)$ is not the same as $\mathcal{M}_i$
>>$M(q) \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is the *system* inertia matrix in joint space, aggregating all links via Jacobians. $\mathcal{M}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{6\times 6}$ is the *generalized inertia* of a single link in body-twist space. They have different sizes, units, and physical meanings — confusing them is a major source of derivation errors.

# Intuition
Every Lagrangian-derived equation of motion for a manipulator factors into the same three pieces: how hard it is to **accelerate** the joints (the inertia term), what **velocity-induced** forces the system experiences as it moves (the Coriolis/centrifugal term), and what **static** torque is needed just to hold a configuration against gravity and springs (the gravity term). Reading $\Upsilon = M\ddot q + C\dot q + G$ left to right is reading off these three contributions one at a time.

The factorization is also what makes the equations *useful for control*. Computed-torque control commands
$$\Upsilon_{\text{cmd}} = M(q)\,\ddot q_{\text{des}} + C(q,\dot q)\,\dot q + G(q),$$
which, substituted into the dynamics, gives $\ddot q = \ddot q_{\text{des}}$ — perfect tracking, modulo modeling error. None of this works without isolating $M$, $C$, and $G$ explicitly.

The Coriolis term being velocity-dependent is the part students most often miss. Calling it "fictitious" is misleading: $C(q,\dot q)\dot q$ is mandatory, not optional. It is the dynamical signature of describing a multi-body rotating mechanism in non-inertial generalized coordinates, and dropping it produces a dynamically incorrect model that fails at any non-trivial joint speed.

# Properties

## System Inertia Matrix $M(q)$

#### (*Symmetric Positive-Definite*) $M(q) = M(q)^\top \succ 0$ for any physical configuration.
Symmetry follows from $M(q) = \sum_i J_i^{b\top}\mathcal{M}_i J_i^b$ and the symmetry of each $\mathcal{M}_i$. Positive definiteness follows from the kinetic energy $T = \tfrac12\,\dot q^\top M\,\dot q$ being strictly positive whenever $\dot q \neq 0$ for a body of positive mass. Consequence: $M$ is always invertible, so the forward-dynamics map $\ddot q = M^{-1}(\Upsilon - C\dot q - G)$ is well-defined.

#### (*Configuration Dependence Only*) $M(q)$ depends on $q$ alone, not on $\dot q$.
The $\dot q$-dependence of $T$ is entirely in the explicit $\dot q^\top M(q)\dot q$ factor, so $M$ inherits no velocity dependence. This is what makes the standard form *linear in $\ddot q$*.

#### (*Read Off From Kinetic Energy*) $M_{ij} = \partial^2 T / \partial \dot q_i\,\partial \dot q_j$.
After computing $T$ in the Lagrangian procedure, extract $M$ directly by inspection — no need to invoke Euler–Lagrange first. Coefficients of $\dot q_i^2$ give diagonal entries (with a factor of 2); coefficients of $\dot q_i\dot q_j$ ($i\neq j$) give off-diagonal entries.

## Coriolis Matrix $C(q,\dot q)$

#### (*Christoffel Construction*) Once $M(q)$ is in hand, $C$ is built directly from partial derivatives of $M$ via
$$C_{ij}(q,\dot q) = \sum_{k=1}^n \tfrac12\!\left(\partial_{q_k} M_{ij} + \partial_{q_j} M_{ik} - \partial_{q_i} M_{jk}\right)\dot q_k.$$
No return to the full Euler–Lagrange machinery is required. This particular construction (out of several valid choices) yields the skew-symmetry property below.

#### (*Skew-Symmetry of $\dot M - 2C$*) With Christoffel-defined $C$, the matrix $N(q,\dot q) := \dot M(q) - 2C(q,\dot q)$ is skew-symmetric, i.e. $N + N^\top = 0$.
Equivalently, $\dot q^\top N \dot q = 0$ for every $\dot q$. This is the dynamical statement of energy conservation when no torque is applied: it forces $\tfrac{d}{dt}(\tfrac12\dot q^\top M\dot q) = \dot q^\top(\Upsilon - G)$ — power in equals power out. Lyapunov-based controllers (PD-plus-gravity, passivity-based) rely on this property.

#### (*Centrifugal vs. Coriolis Terms*) Inside $C(q,\dot q)\dot q$, $\dot q_i^2$ contributions are centrifugal; $\dot q_i\dot q_j$ ($i\neq j$) contributions are Coriolis proper.
A single-joint arm spinning about a fixed axis has only centrifugal terms. Multi-joint arms with simultaneous joint motion produce mixed Coriolis cross terms. Both arise mechanically from the same Christoffel construction.

#### (*Quadratic Velocity Scaling*) $\|C(q,\dot q)\dot q\|$ scales as $\|\dot q\|^2$.
At low speeds, the Coriolis term is quadratic-small and can sometimes be ignored for back-of-envelope analysis. At design speeds it cannot be ignored — this is why high-speed manipulators need careful Coriolis modeling and slow assembly arms can sometimes get away without it.

#### (*Constant $M$ ⇒ $C = 0$*) If $M(q)$ does not depend on $q$, the Christoffel formula gives $C \equiv 0$.
For Cartesian (prismatic-only) mechanisms moving in a horizontal plane, $M$ is a constant matrix, $C \equiv 0$, and $G \equiv 0$ — the dynamics reduce to $\Upsilon = M\ddot q$. This is the simplest possible manipulator dynamics.

## Gravity Vector $G(q)$

#### (*Gradient of Potential*) $G(q) = \partial V/\partial q$, where $V$ sums gravitational $m_i g h_i$ over links and elastic $\tfrac12 k_j x_j^2$ over springs.
No derivatives of $T$ are needed to compute $G$. Unlike $C$, the gravity term is linear in nothing — it is purely configuration-dependent.

#### (*Static Hold Torque*) Setting $\dot q = \ddot q = 0$ gives $\Upsilon = G(q)$ — the static torque to hold the configuration against gravity.
Plotting $G(q)$ over the workspace gives the static "gravity load" map; this is the first thing to check when sizing motors or designing gravity-compensation control.

## Exam & Homework Hacks

#### (*Term-Size Triage*) The three terms scale very differently with $\dot q$:
$\Upsilon \approx G(q)$ at rest; $\Upsilon \approx M(q)\ddot q + G(q)$ at low speed; all three matter at high speed. Use this hierarchy to decide which terms to compute first.

#### (*Dimensions Cheat Sheet*) For a system with $n$ DoF: $M$ is $n\times n$, $C$ is $n\times n$, $G$ is $n\times 1$, $\Upsilon$ is $n\times 1$, $\dot q$ and $\ddot q$ are $n\times 1$.
Quick dimension checks catch most algebra errors. If $C\dot q$ ends up the wrong size, the most common bug is a misindexed Christoffel sum.

#### (*Sign Check on $G$ at Extremes*) For a planar arm at $\theta = 0$ (horizontal) gravity load is maximal; at $\theta = \pi/2$ (vertical) it should be zero or sign-flipped.
A quick limiting-case check on $G$ catches sign errors in the height function inside $V$.

#### (*Verify Skew-Symmetry of $\dot M - 2C$*) Compute $\dot M - 2C$ symbolically and check the diagonal is zero and off-diagonals satisfy $a_{ij} = -a_{ji}$.
This is a cheap, powerful sanity check on the Christoffel computation — and an exam favorite.

#### (*Positive-Definiteness Check on $M$*) For an $n\times n$ symmetric $M$, verify all leading principal minors are strictly positive.
For a 2-link arm $M = \bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} a + 2b\cos\theta_2 & b\cos\theta_2 + d \\ b\cos\theta_2 + d & d \end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$, both diagonal entries and $\det M$ should be strictly positive over the workspace.

#### (*Recognize the Forms*) $T$ that contains $\dot\theta_i\dot\theta_j$ cross products ⇒ $M$ has off-diagonal entries ⇒ Coriolis cross terms exist.
$T$ that contains explicit $\cos\theta_j$ or $\sin\theta_j$ multipliers ⇒ $M$ depends on $q$ ⇒ Christoffel derivatives are nonzero ⇒ $C \neq 0$.

# Derivation

>[!example]- Standard form from $L = \tfrac12\dot q^\top M(q)\dot q - V(q)$
>For a system of rigid bodies, $T(q,\dot q) = \tfrac12\,\dot q^\top M(q)\,\dot q$, so $L = \tfrac12\dot q^\top M(q)\dot q - V(q)$. Compute the two derivatives that enter Euler–Lagrange. First,
>$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_i} = \sum_j M_{ij}(q)\,\dot q_j, \quad\text{so}\quad \frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_i}\right) = \sum_j M_{ij}\,\ddot q_j + \sum_{j,k} \frac{\partial M_{ij}}{\partial q_k}\,\dot q_k\,\dot q_j.$$
>Second,
>$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = \tfrac12\sum_{j,k}\frac{\partial M_{jk}}{\partial q_i}\,\dot q_j\,\dot q_k - \frac{\partial V}{\partial q_i}.$$
>Subtracting and identifying $G_i = \partial V/\partial q_i$,
>$$\Upsilon_i = \sum_j M_{ij}\,\ddot q_j + \sum_{j,k}\!\left(\frac{\partial M_{ij}}{\partial q_k} - \tfrac12\frac{\partial M_{jk}}{\partial q_i}\right)\dot q_j\,\dot q_k + G_i.$$
>Symmetrizing the velocity term in $j\leftrightarrow k$ produces $\Upsilon_i = \sum_j M_{ij}\ddot q_j + \sum_j C_{ij}\dot q_j + G_i$, the row form of $\Upsilon = M\ddot q + C\dot q + G$. The Christoffel formula for $C_{ij}$ falls out of the symmetrization step.

# Examples

>[!example]- Two-link planar arm — extracting $M$, $C$, $G$
>Continuing the [[6.1. Lagrangian Dynamics#Examples|two-link arm example]], the kinetic energy after collecting terms is
>$$T = \tfrac12\bigl[\alpha + 2\beta\cos\theta_2\bigr]\dot\theta_1^2 + \tfrac12\,\delta\,\dot\theta_2^2 + \bigl[\delta + \beta\cos\theta_2\bigr]\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2,$$
>with $\alpha = (m_1+m_2)\ell_1^2 + m_2\ell_2^2$, $\beta = m_2\ell_1\ell_2$, $\delta = m_2\ell_2^2$.
>>[!example]- System inertia matrix $M(q)$
>>Read off $M_{ij} = \partial^2 T/\partial\dot\theta_i\partial\dot\theta_j$:
>>$$M(q) = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha + 2\beta\cos\theta_2 & \delta + \beta\cos\theta_2 \\ \delta + \beta\cos\theta_2 & \delta\end{bmatrix}.$$
>
>>[!example]- Gravity vector $G(q)$
>>From $V = (m_1+m_2)g\ell_1\sin\theta_1 + m_2 g\ell_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)$:
>>$$G(q) = \begin{bmatrix} (m_1+m_2)g\ell_1\cos\theta_1 + m_2 g\ell_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ m_2 g\ell_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\end{bmatrix}.$$
>
>>[!example]- Coriolis matrix $C(q,\dot q)$ via Christoffel
>>Only $\partial M/\partial \theta_2$ is nonzero ($\partial M_{11}/\partial\theta_2 = -2\beta\sin\theta_2$, $\partial M_{12}/\partial\theta_2 = -\beta\sin\theta_2$). Plugging into the Christoffel formula:
>>$$C(q,\dot q) = \begin{bmatrix} -\beta\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_2 & -\beta\sin\theta_2\,(\dot\theta_1 + \dot\theta_2) \\ \beta\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1 & 0\end{bmatrix}.$$
>>
>>The Coriolis term $C\dot q$ contains both centrifugal ($-\beta\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_2^2$) and Coriolis-proper ($-2\beta\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2$) contributions. Verifying $\dot M - 2C$ is skew-symmetric is a useful check on the algebra.

>[!example]- Static gravity compensation
>A 2-link arm at rest at $(\theta_1,\theta_2) = (\pi/2, 0)$ — first link straight up, second link extending the same direction. With $\dot q = \ddot q = 0$ the standard form collapses to $\Upsilon = G(q)$:
>$$G(\pi/2, 0) = \begin{bmatrix} (m_1+m_2)g\ell_1\cos(\pi/2) + m_2 g\ell_2\cos(\pi/2) \\ m_2 g\ell_2\cos(\pi/2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$
>Both joint torques are zero — the arm is in a vertical, balanced configuration where gravity contributes no moment about either joint (modulo stability, a separate question).

>[!example]- Cartesian gantry — degenerate case
>A two-axis Cartesian gantry has prismatic joints $q = (x, y)$ moving frictionlessly in a horizontal plane. Let $m_x$ be the total mass moved by the $x$-axis and $m_y$ the mass riding on the $x$ stage. Then
>$$T = \tfrac12 (m_x + m_y)\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 m_y \dot y^2,$$
>$$M = \begin{bmatrix} m_x + m_y & 0 \\ 0 & m_y\end{bmatrix}, \quad C \equiv 0, \quad G \equiv 0.$$
>$M$ is constant (no $q$-dependence) and gravity is absent for purely horizontal motion, so the dynamics reduce to the decoupled linear system $F_x = (m_x+m_y)\ddot x$, $F_y = m_y\ddot y$. The simplest manipulator dynamics possible — and the textbook illustration of why Coriolis effects vanish whenever inertia is configuration-independent.
