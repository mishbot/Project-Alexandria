>[!note] Dielectric Breakdown
>**Dielectric breakdown** happens when the field in an insulator becomes strong enough to make it conduct. Charge then crosses the gap as a spark. The **dielectric strength** $E_{\max}$ of a material is the largest field it can hold without breaking down. For dry air at atmospheric pressure, $E_{\max} \approx 3 \times 10^{6}\ \text{V/m}$. Breakdown in a gas is an **avalanche** of electrons:
>- A few free electrons are always present.
>- The field accelerates one of them. If it hits a molecule with enough energy, it knocks off another electron.
>- Now two free electrons accelerate and collide. Two become four, four become eight, and the number keeps doubling.
>- The free electrons and the ions they leave behind form a conducting path, and a spark crosses the gap.
>
>A parallel-plate capacitor with gap $d$ has the field $E = V/d$. It breaks down when $V$ exceeds the **maximum voltage**
>$$V_{\max} = E_{\max}\,d.$$
>A smaller gap raises $C = \varepsilon_0 A/d$ but lowers $V_{\max}$, so the gap cannot shrink without limit. A dielectric helps in two ways. It raises $C$ by the factor $K$. Most solid dielectrics also have a larger dielectric strength than air.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture gave no numerical dielectric strengths. The value for air and the values for paper below follow Giancoli Table 24–1 in section 24.5.

# Intuition

Air insulates because almost all of its electrons are bound to molecules. A strong field changes that. A free electron gains kinetic energy as the field accelerates it between collisions. Above about $3 \times 10^{6}\ \text{V/m}$, it gains enough to free another electron at the next collision. Each collision then turns one free electron into two. Within a very short time, the air between the conductors holds enough free charge to conduct.

A capacitor designer wants a small gap for a large capacitance. Breakdown sets the smallest safe gap for a given voltage. A thin layer of a strong solid dielectric allows a smaller gap and adds the factor $K$ as well.

The potential difference across the Easy example's air gap rises until the field reaches $E_{\max}$. Then one free electron starts an avalanche. Each round of collisions doubles the number of free electrons, and a spark crosses the gap.

# Properties

#### *(Spark Demonstrations)* Classroom demonstrations showed breakdown in air between charged conductors brought too close together.

Sparks jumped between a charged Van de Graaff generator and a nearby metal sphere. Two charged plates pushed close together sparked across the gap. The capacitor discharge in [[3.5.3. Energy Stored in an Electric Field]] made the same kind of spark. A spark channel is hotter than the Sun's surface, which is about $5800\ \text{K}$. Its bluish color comes mostly from excited nitrogen molecules, raised to higher energy by collisions in the spark. The heat vaporizes small amounts of metal where the spark meets a conductor. It left small pits in the metal sphere used for the capacitor discharge.

#### *(Maximum Charge Does Not Depend on the Gap)* For a parallel-plate capacitor, the largest charge before breakdown is $Q_{\max} = K\varepsilon_0 A E_{\max}$, whatever the gap.

The largest charge is $Q_{\max} = CV_{\max} = (K\varepsilon_0 A/d)(E_{\max} d)$, and the gap cancels. Shrinking the gap lowers the voltage needed for a given charge. The maximum charge stays the same. For air, $Q_{\max}/A = \varepsilon_0 E_{\max} = 2.66 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{C/m}^2$. Paper, with $K = 3.7$ and $E_{\max} \approx 15 \times 10^{6}\ \text{V/m}$, allows $4.9 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{C/m}^2$, about $18$ times more.

#### *(Energy Density Limit)* Breakdown also caps the energy that each cubic meter of a capacitor can store.

With a dielectric, $U = \tfrac12 CV^2 = \tfrac12(K\varepsilon_0 A/d)(Ed)^2 = \tfrac12 K\varepsilon_0E^2\,(Ad)$. The largest energy per unit volume is therefore $\tfrac12 K\varepsilon_0 E_{\max}^2$. For air this is $\tfrac12(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(3 \times 10^{6})^2 = 40\ \text{J/m}^3$. For paper it is about $3.7 \times 10^{3}\ \text{J/m}^3$, about $90$ times more. Practical capacitors therefore use thin solid dielectrics.

#### *(Small Spheres Spark at Low Voltage)* An isolated sphere of radius $a$ reaches the breakdown field at its surface when its potential is $E_{\max}\,a$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not relate breakdown to the size of a conductor. Giancoli chapter 23 has a similar example.

The sphere's potential is $V = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 a)$, and its surface field is $E = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^2)$, from [[3.4.4. Electric Potential of a Point Charge]]. Their ratio gives $V = Ea$. A sphere of radius $15\ \text{cm}$ in air can reach $(3 \times 10^{6})(0.15) = 4.5 \times 10^{5}\ \text{V}$. A sphere of radius $1\ \text{mm}$ sparks at only $3\ \text{kV}$. A Van de Graaff generator uses a large, smooth dome to reach high voltages.

#### *(Blown Capacitors)* A breakdown inside a capacitor usually destroys it.

The spark heats the dielectric enough to vaporize it. Older capacitors used paper, which burned and burst out of the case. Modern capacitors use polymers, and the vapor from a burning polymer can be toxic. The gas pressure bulges or splits the top of the case. A burnt smell from a failed electronic device often comes from a blown capacitor.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Largest Voltage Across an Air Gap
>A parallel-plate capacitor has an air gap of $0.20\ \text{mm}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the largest potential difference it can hold.
>>The maximum voltage is
>>$$V_{\max} = E_{\max}\,d = (3 \times 10^{6})(0.20 \times 10^{-3}) = 600\ \text{V}.$$

>[!example] (Medium) Air or Paper?
>A $50\ \text{nF}$ parallel-plate capacitor must hold $500\ \text{V}$ without breaking down.
>
>>[!example]- Find the smallest gap and the plate area needed with air, and with paper ($K = 3.7$, $E_{\max} = 15 \times 10^{6}\ \text{V/m}$).
>>With air, the smallest gap is $d = V/E_{\max} = 500/(3 \times 10^{6}) = 1.67 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m}$. The area follows from $C = \varepsilon_0 A/d$:
>>$$A = \frac{Cd}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{(50 \times 10^{-9})(1.67 \times 10^{-4})}{8.85 \times 10^{-12}} = 0.94\ \text{m}^2.$$
>>With paper, the smallest gap is $500/(15 \times 10^{6}) = 3.33 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{m}$. The area follows from $C = K\varepsilon_0 A/d$:
>>$$A = \frac{Cd}{K\varepsilon_0} = \frac{(50 \times 10^{-9})(3.33 \times 10^{-5})}{(3.7)(8.85 \times 10^{-12})} = 0.051\ \text{m}^2.$$
>>Paper needs an area $18.5$ times smaller. The factor $3.7$ comes from $K$, and the factor $5$ comes from the thinner gap that paper allows.

>[!example] (Hard) Voltage Limit in Series
>Two capacitors, $C_1 = 2.0\ \mu\text{F}$ and $C_2 = 4.0\ \mu\text{F}$, are each rated for at most $100\ \text{V}$. They are connected in series.
>
>>[!example]- Find the largest supply voltage the pair can take. Compare it with the same two in parallel.
>>In series both carry the same charge $Q$. Then $V_1 = Q/C_1$ and $V_2 = Q/C_2$, so $V_1 = 2V_2$. The $2.0\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor reaches $100\ \text{V}$ first, at
>>$$Q = C_1 V_1 = (2.0 \times 10^{-6})(100) = 2.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{C}.$$
>>At that charge, $V_2 = (2.0 \times 10^{-4})/(4.0 \times 10^{-6}) = 50\ \text{V}$. The pair can take at most $150\ \text{V}$. Two equal capacitors would split the voltage evenly and could take $200\ \text{V}$.
>>
>>In parallel, each capacitor has the full supply voltage, so the limit is $100\ \text{V}$.
