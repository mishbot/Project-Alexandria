>[!note] Work-Energy Principle
>The **Work–Energy Principle** states that when a net force acts on an object and changes its speed, the total work done by the force equals the change in the object’s kinetic energy.
>
>If an object of mass $m$ is subject to a net force that causes it to speed up or slow down from an initial speed $v_i$ to a final speed $v_f$, then the net work $W$ done on the object is
>$$\sum W = \Delta K = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_i^2.$$

# Proof
Proceed using Newton's Second Law, such that $F_{\text{net}}=m\vec{a}=m\dfrac{d\vec{v}}{dt}$. Work is then defined as
$$\begin{align}
W&=\int_{\mathcal{P}} F_{\text{net}} \cdot d\vec{r} \\
&=m\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}} \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} \cdot d\vec{r} \\
&=m \int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}} \vec{v} \cdot d\vec{v} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}m\vec{v}^2\Big|_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}m\vec{v}_{f}^{2} - \frac{1}{2}m\vec{v}_{i}^{2} \\
&= \Delta K.
\end{align}$$
# Examples

>[!example] Hemisphere Pulling Problem
>A small sphere of mass $m$ is attached to a string being pulled from the bottom of a hemisphere $\theta=0$ to the top $\theta = \pi / 2$. It is pulled slowly, such that at every moment the sphere is nearly in equilibrium. Throughout the entire process, the string is tangent to the hemisphere.
>
>![[Screenshot 2025-08-01 at 21.19.51.png|300]]
>
>>[!example]- When the sphere is at an angle $\theta$, what is magnitude of the string’s tension?
>>In the $x$ direction of the tangent, there is tension $T$, which is opposed by gravity with an $x$ component of $mg\cos\theta$. Given that the sphere is "nearly" at equilibrium, $a=0$, then $T-mg\cos\theta=0 \iff T=mg\cos \theta$.
>
>>[!example]- What is the work done by the string on the sphere throughout the process?
>>It holds that $W = \int |\vec{F}| \cdot |d\vec{r}|$. From the previous part, $|\vec{F}|=T=mg\cos\theta$. Here, $|d\vec{r}|=Rd\theta$ since $|\vec{r}|=R\theta$, which is the arc length. Since both $T$ and $d\vec{r}$ are in the same direction, the angle between them is $0$, and therefore $\cos0 = 1$:
>>$$\begin{align}
>>W_{T} &=\int T \cdot dr \cdot \cos0 \\
>>&= \int mg \cos\theta \cdot R \cdot d\theta \\
>>&= mgR \sin\theta \Big |^{\pi / 2}_{0} \\
>>&= mgR.
>>\end{align}$$

>>[!example]- What is the work done by the normal force throughout the process?
>>The normal force exerted on the ball by the hemisphere is upward, whereas the vertical component of gravity in terms of the ball's reference frame is $mg \sin\theta$. Once again, because the sphere is in quasi-equilibrium, then $N-mg\sin\theta=0 \iff N=mg\sin\theta$. However, because the angle between $N$ and $dr$ is $90 \degree$, it turns out that
>>$$\begin{align}
>>W_{N} &=\int N \cdot dr \cdot \cos \pi / 2 \\
>>&= \int mg \sin\theta \cdot R \cdot d\theta \cdot 0 \\
>>&= 0
>>\end{align}$$
>
>>[!example]- What is the work done by gravity throughout the process?
>>Opposite to the direction of displacement, the horizontal component relative to the ball's reference frame is $F_{G}=mg\cos\theta$. Then, 
>>$$\begin{align}
>>W_{G} &=\int F_{G} \cdot dr \cdot \cos \pi \\
>>&= -mgR\int \cos\theta \; d\theta \\
>>&= -mgR \sin\theta \Big|^{\pi / 2}_{0} \\
>>&= -mgR.
>>\end{align}$$
>
>>[!example]- Is there a relationship between parts 2 and 4?
>>Since the sphere moves slowly and remains in quasi-equilibrium, its speed does not change, so $\Delta K = 0$. By the work–energy principle:
>>$W_{\text{net}} = W_T + W_G = \Delta K = 0$
>>
>>As such, the work done by tension exactly cancels the work done by gravity ($W_T = -W_G$).
