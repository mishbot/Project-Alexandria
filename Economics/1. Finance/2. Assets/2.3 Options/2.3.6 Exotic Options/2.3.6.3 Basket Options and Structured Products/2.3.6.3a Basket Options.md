>[!note] Basket Options
>A **basket option**, also called a **dispersion option**, pays on the separate performances of several assets $S^1, S^2, \dots, S^n$. An option on a stock index treats the whole basket as one asset with a continuous dividend yield. A dispersion option pays on the differences between the assets instead. Each asset enters through its **normalized price** $X^i_T = S^i_T/S^i_0$, its price at expiry $T$ divided by its price today. Its return is $R^i_T = X^i_T - 1$. The strike $K$ is a fraction of the starting levels, so $K = 1$ is at the money.
>- A **worst-of** option pays on the asset with the lowest normalized price. A **best-of** option pays on the highest.
>- A **rainbow option** pays a weighted sum of the returns, ranked from best to worst.
>- A **spread option** pays when one return beats another by more than $K$. With $K = 0$ it is an **outperformance option**, also called an **exchange option**.
>
>Per unit of notional, the worst-of and best-of payoffs are
>$$\begin{aligned} \text{worst-of call} &= \max\big(\min(X^1_T, \dots, X^n_T) - K,\ 0\big), & \text{worst-of put} &= \max\big(K - \min(X^1_T, \dots, X^n_T),\ 0\big), \\ \text{best-of call} &= \max\big(\max(X^1_T, \dots, X^n_T) - K,\ 0\big), & \text{best-of put} &= \max\big(K - \max(X^1_T, \dots, X^n_T),\ 0\big). \end{aligned}$$
>A worst-of call pays only if every asset ends above $K$. The rainbow and spread payoffs are
>$$\text{rainbow} = \max\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m} w_j\{R_T\}_j,\ 0\Big) \ \text{ with } \ \sum_{j=1}^{m} w_j = 1, \qquad \text{spread} = \max\big(R^2_T - R^1_T - K,\ 0\big).$$
>Here $\{R_T\}_j$ is the $j$-th best of the returns $R^1_T, \dots, R^n_T$, and $w_j$ is its weight. So $\{R_T\}_1$ is the best return. Volatility, correlation and volatility skew all matter for pricing and hedging these options.
>Among the [[2.3.6 Exotic Options|exotic options]], basket options are the ones written on several assets at once.

# Intuition

A vanilla option pays on one price at one date. Many investors hold views about several prices at once. One investor expects three bank stocks to all stay above today's levels. Another expects one stock to beat another. A dealer writes a payoff that matches each view and prices it with the same tools as a vanilla option. A dispersion option depends on how the assets move together, so correlation becomes a pricing input. The first investor can buy a worst-of call, which pays only if all three stocks end above their strike. The second investor can buy an outperformance option on the two stocks.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules state that correlation matters for these options. The identity below and the sign of the correlation effect go beyond them.

The larger of two numbers plus the smaller one equals their sum. A similar identity holds for the call payoffs in every state,
$$\max\big(\max(X^1_T, X^2_T) - K, 0\big) + \max\big(\min(X^1_T, X^2_T) - K, 0\big) = \max(X^1_T - K, 0) + \max(X^2_T - K, 0).$$
The left side is a best-of call plus a worst-of call. The right side is a vanilla call on each asset. When both assets end above $K$, both sides equal $X^1_T + X^2_T - 2K$. When only one does, both sides equal that asset's excess over $K$. A vanilla call on one asset does not depend on the correlation $\rho$ between the two assets. So a change in $\rho$ moves the best-of call and the worst-of call by equal and opposite amounts. A lower $\rho$ makes a wide gap between the two prices more likely. The minimum then tends to be lower and the maximum higher. So the worst-of call loses value as $\rho$ falls, and the best-of call gains. At $\rho = 1$, with equal volatilities and dividend yields, the two prices move together, and both options become the vanilla call. The same identity holds for puts. With three or more assets it fails in general, as the (Easy) example shows.

#### *(Exchange Option)* With $K = 0$, the spread option exchanges one asset for another, and correlation enters only through the volatility of their ratio.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules say exchange options have closed forms under constant parameters. The formula below is Margrabe's formula, in the form Hull gives in Chapter 26.

The normalized payoff is $\max(R^2_T - R^1_T, 0) = \max(X^2_T - X^1_T, 0)$. Its holder can give up $1/S^1_0$ units of asset 1 and receive $1/S^2_0$ units of asset 2 at $T$. Hull prices an option to give up an asset $U$ and receive an asset $V$ at $T$ as
$$f = V_0e^{-q_VT}N(d_1) - U_0e^{-q_UT}N(d_2), \qquad d_1 = \frac{\ln(V_0/U_0) + (q_U - q_V + \hat\sigma^2/2)T}{\hat\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \hat\sigma\sqrt{T}.$$
Here $U_0$ and $V_0$ are today's values, $q_U$ and $q_V$ the dividend yields, and $\sigma_U$ and $\sigma_V$ the volatilities. The volatility of the ratio $V/U$ is $\hat\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma_U^2 + \sigma_V^2 - 2\rho\sigma_U\sigma_V}$. The formula is the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]] with asset $U$ in place of cash. The risk-free rate drops out, because the holder pays with an asset instead of cash. For the normalized spread option, $U_0 = V_0 = 1$. Take $\sigma_1 = 25\%$, $\sigma_2 = 30\%$, no dividends and $T = 1$. At $\rho = 0.6$, $\hat\sigma = 25\%$ and the option is worth $0.0995$, or \$99,476 per \$1 million of notional. At $\rho = 0$ it is worth \$154,808, and at $\rho = 0.9$ it is worth \$52,737. Higher correlation makes the outperformance option cheaper.

#### *(Rainbow Weights)* With $K = 1$, the best-of, worst-of and basket calls are special cases of the rainbow.

The rainbow payoff changes with its weights $w_j$. Take $m = n$, so that every asset has a rank. Three choices of weights give familiar payoffs.
- With $w_1 = 1$ and every other weight $0$, it pays $\max(\{R_T\}_1, 0)$. That is the best-of call with $K = 1$, since $\{R_T\}_1 = \max_i X^i_T - 1$.
- With $w_n = 1$ and every other weight $0$, it pays the worst-of call with $K = 1$.
- With equal weights $w_j = 1/n$, the ranking no longer matters. It pays $\max(\bar X_T - 1, 0)$, a call on the equal-weighted basket. Here $\bar X_T$ is the average normalized price.

Now take any nonnegative weights. The weighted return then lies between the worst return and the best return. So in every state, the rainbow pays between the worst-of call and the best-of call, both with $K = 1$. By [[4.2.2a Arbitrage|no arbitrage]], its price lies between their prices. More weight on the top ranks moves its price toward the best-of call. In the (Easy) example the rainbow pays $0.091$. The worst-of call pays $0$, and the best-of call pays $0.15$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Payoffs on a Three-Stock Basket
>A one-year note references three stocks. Their prices today are \$80, \$150 and \$45. At expiry they are \$92, \$135 and \$50.40. Each option below has a notional of \$1,000,000, and $K = 1$ where it has a strike.
>
>>[!example]- Find the worst-of and best-of call and put payoffs, and a rainbow option's payoff with weights $0.5$, $0.3$ and $0.2$. Add a spread option of stock 3 over stock 2 with $K = 0$. Compare them with a call on the equal-weighted basket.
>>The normalized prices are $X^1_T = 92/80 = 1.15$, $X^2_T = 135/150 = 0.90$ and $X^3_T = 50.40/45 = 1.12$. The returns are $+15\%$, $-10\%$ and $+12\%$.
>>
>>Stock 2 is the worst asset at $0.90$. The worst-of call pays $\max(0.90 - 1, 0) = 0$. The worst-of put pays $\max(1 - 0.90, 0) = 0.10$, or \$100,000. Stock 1 is the best asset at $1.15$. The best-of call pays $0.15$, or \$150,000, and the best-of put pays $0$.
>>
>>The rainbow option ranks the returns as $15\%$, $12\%$ and $-10\%$. It pays
>>$$\max\big(0.5 \times 0.15 + 0.3 \times 0.12 + 0.2 \times (-0.10),\ 0\big) = 0.091,$$
>>or \$91,000. The spread option pays $\max(0.12 - (-0.10), 0) = 0.22$, or \$220,000.
>>
>>The equal-weighted basket ends at $(1.15 + 0.90 + 1.12)/3 = 1.0567$. A call on it pays $0.0567$, or \$56,667. The basket call sees only the average. The dispersion options pay on the gaps between the stocks. Here the best-of call plus the worst-of call pays $0.15$. The three vanilla calls pay $0.15 + 0 + 0.12 = 0.27$, so the two-asset identity fails with three assets.

>The modules say exchange options have closed forms but do not state one. This example uses Margrabe's formula as Hull gives it in Chapter 26.

>[!example] (Medium) Pricing an Outperformance Option
>A fund buys a two-year outperformance option on two stocks, $U$ and $V$. At expiry it receives $\$5{,}000{,}000 \times \max(R^V_T - R^U_T, 0)$, where $R^U_T$ and $R^V_T$ are the two stocks' returns. Stock $U$ has a volatility of $28\%$ and a dividend yield of $1\%$. Stock $V$ has a volatility of $22\%$ and a dividend yield of $3\%$. The correlation of their returns is $0.45$. Dividend yields are continuously compounded.
>
>>[!example]- Find the option's value with Margrabe's formula. Then find its value at a correlation of $0.8$.
>>The payoff is $\max(X^V_T - X^U_T, 0)$ per unit of notional, so $U_0 = V_0 = 1$. The variance of the ratio is
>>$$\hat\sigma^2 = 0.28^2 + 0.22^2 - 2(0.45)(0.28)(0.22) = 0.0784 + 0.0484 - 0.05544 = 0.07136.$$
>>So $\hat\sigma = 0.26713$ and $\hat\sigma\sqrt{2} = 0.37778$. Since $\ln(V_0/U_0) = 0$,
>>$$d_1 = \frac{(0.01 - 0.03 + 0.03568) \times 2}{0.37778} = \frac{0.03136}{0.37778} = 0.08301, \qquad d_2 = 0.08301 - 0.37778 = -0.29477.$$
>>With $N(d_1) = 0.53308$ and $N(d_2) = 0.38408$, the value per unit of notional is
>>$$f = e^{-0.06}(0.53308) - e^{-0.02}(0.38408) = 0.50203 - 0.37648 = 0.12556.$$
>>On \$5,000,000 the option costs \$627,779. The risk-free rate appears nowhere. A simulation of one million price pairs gives $0.12582 \pm 0.00019$. Without dividends the value would be $0.14982$. Stock $V$'s higher yield lowers its expected growth against stock $U$, so the option is worth less.
>>
>>At a correlation of $0.8$, $\hat\sigma^2 = 0.1268 - 0.09856 = 0.02824$, so $\hat\sigma = 0.16805$. The same steps give $d_1 = -0.04948$, $d_2 = -0.28714$ and $f = 0.07296$, or \$364,793. The price falls by $42\%$. The two stocks now move together more, so a wide gap between their returns is less likely.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The worst-of prices below come from a two-asset lognormal model, valued by numerical integration. The modules give no pricing method for worst-of options.

>[!example] (Hard) Correlation Risk in Worst-of and Best-of Calls
>A dealer sells a one-year worst-of call with $K = 1$ on two stocks, with a notional of \$10,000,000. Stock 1 has a volatility of $25\%$ and a dividend yield of $1\%$. Stock 2 has a volatility of $35\%$ and a dividend yield of $2\%$. The risk-free rate is $4\%$. All rates are continuously compounded. The dealer's model values the worst-of call at $0.04635$ per unit of notional when the correlation is $0.3$. It values the call at $0.07214$ when the correlation is $0.7$.
>
>>[!example]- Price an at-the-money vanilla call on each normalized stock. Use them to find the best-of call at both correlations. Then find the dealer's dollar loss on the worst-of call if the correlation rises from $0.3$ to $0.7$.
>>Each vanilla call has spot $1$, strike $1$ and $\tau = 1$, so $\ln(S/K) = 0$. For stock 1,
>>$$d_1 = \frac{0.04 - 0.01 + 0.03125}{0.25} = 0.24500, \qquad d_2 = 0.24500 - 0.25 = -0.00500.$$
>>With $N(d_1) = 0.59677$ and $N(d_2) = 0.49801$, the call is $c_1 = e^{-0.01}(0.59677) - e^{-0.04}(0.49801) = 0.59083 - 0.47848 = 0.11236$. For stock 2,
>>$$d_1 = \frac{0.04 - 0.02 + 0.06125}{0.35} = 0.23214, \qquad d_2 = 0.23214 - 0.35 = -0.11786.$$
>>With $N(d_1) = 0.59179$ and $N(d_2) = 0.45309$, the call is $c_2 = e^{-0.02}(0.59179) - e^{-0.04}(0.45309) = 0.58007 - 0.43532 = 0.14474$.
>>
>>A best-of call plus a worst-of call pays the same as the two vanilla calls in every state. So the best-of call costs $0.25710 - 0.04635 = 0.21075$ at a correlation of $0.3$. At $0.7$ it costs $0.25710 - 0.07214 = 0.18496$. Neither vanilla call depends on the correlation.
>>
>>On \$10,000,000 the worst-of call is worth \$463,524 at $0.3$ and \$721,363 at $0.7$. The dealer who sold it loses \$257,839 when the correlation rises. The best-of call falls by the same amount. Higher correlation makes a wide gap between the stocks less likely. The weaker stock then tends to end higher, and the worst-of call pays on the weaker stock. At $0.3$ the worst-of call costs $41\%$ of the cheaper vanilla call. A simulation of four million price pairs at $0.3$ gives $0.04630 \pm 0.00005$ for the worst-of call.
