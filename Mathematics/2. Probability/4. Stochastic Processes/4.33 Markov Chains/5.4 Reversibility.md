>[!note] Reversibility of Markov Chains
>Let $(X_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ be an irreducible [[5.1 Markov Chains|Markov chain]] with transition matrix $P$ and invariant distribution $\pi^*$. Run the chain backward by defining $Y_n = X_{N - n}$ for $n = 0, \dots, N$ under a starting distribution $X_0 \sim \pi^*$. The **reverse transition matrix** $\hat P$ is defined by
>$$\hat P(x, y) \;=\; \frac{\pi^*(y)\,P(y, x)}{\pi^*(x)},$$
>which is the unique stochastic matrix making $(Y_n)$ a Markov chain with the same stationary distribution as $(X_n)$.
>
>The chain is **reversible** with respect to $\pi^*$ if $\hat P = P$, i.e., the forward and backward chains are statistically identical. Reversibility is equivalent to the **detailed balance equation (DBE)**:
>$$\pi^*(x)\,P(x, y) \;=\; \pi^*(y)\,P(y, x) \qquad \text{for all } x, y \in \mathcal{X}.$$
>
>Detailed balance implies stationarity (summing both sides over $x$ gives the balance equation $\pi^* = \pi^* P$), but the converse does not hold in general. A chain can be stationary without being reversible; however, if a distribution $\pi$ satisfies the DBE for some $P$, then $\pi$ is automatically the invariant distribution of that $P$. This one-way implication is the workhorse of [[3.11 MCMC|MCMC]]: design a transition $P$ that satisfies the DBE with the target $\pi$, and $\pi$ is automatically stationary.
>
>>[!warning] DBE Is a Local Condition
>>Detailed balance is *local*: it equates pairwise flow between any two states. The balance equation $\pi^* = \pi^* P$ is *global*: it equates total flow into and out of each state. Every reversible chain is stationary, but not every stationary chain is reversible. The forward-reverse transformation $\hat P(x, y) = \pi^*(y)P(y, x)/\pi^*(x)$ always gives a valid reverse chain; reversibility requires the additional identity $\hat P = P$.

# Intuition

"Reversibility" means the movie of the chain looks the same played forward and backward. Imagine watching a chain in stationarity and being shown a video of state transitions: if the chain is reversible, there is no statistical test that distinguishes the video from its time-reversed counterpart. The DBE is the precise condition: the probability flow from $x$ to $y$ in one step, $\pi^*(x) P(x, y)$, exactly matches the flow from $y$ to $x$, $\pi^*(y) P(y, x)$.

The one-way implication "detailed balance $\Rightarrow$ stationarity" is why MCMC algorithms are designed to satisfy the DBE rather than the balance equation directly. Checking pairwise detailed balance is an algebraic identity between $P$ and $\pi^*$ that can be verified (or enforced, as in Metropolis-Hastings' accept-reject rule) without computing $\pi^*$'s normalizing constant. Checking the balance equation requires summing over all states of the chain, which is usually intractable when $\pi^*$ is known only up to a constant.

# Properties

#### *(DBE $\Rightarrow$ Stationarity)* Any distribution satisfying the detailed balance equation with $P$ is automatically invariant for $P$.

Summing the DBE $\pi^*(x)P(x, y) = \pi^*(y)P(y, x)$ over $x$ gives
$$\sum_x \pi^*(x)\,P(x, y) = \pi^*(y)\sum_x P(y, x) = \pi^*(y),$$
since the rows of $P$ sum to $1$. The left-hand side is the $y$-th entry of $\pi^* P$, so $\pi^* P = \pi^*$. This is the implication that underwrites MCMC: to sample from $\pi^*$, it suffices to build any irreducible $P$ satisfying the DBE, and $\pi^*$ will be the long-run distribution of the chain regardless of the starting state.

#### *(Stationarity $\not\Rightarrow$ DBE)* A stationary chain need not be reversible.

The balance equation $\pi^*(y) = \sum_x \pi^*(x)\,P(x, y)$ equates the *total* probability flow into and out of $y$ from all states; the DBE equates *pairwise* flow between each pair. A cyclic chain illustrates the difference: let $\mathcal{X} = \{1, 2, 3\}$ with $P(1,2) = P(2,3) = P(3,1) = 1$ and all other transitions $0$. The uniform distribution $\pi^* = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$ is stationary, but $\pi^*(1)P(1,2) = 1/3 \ne 0 = \pi^*(2)P(2,1)$, violating the DBE. Probability flows around the cycle one direction only; played backward the chain looks different, and is therefore not reversible.

#### *(Reverse Chain Always Well-Defined)* Every irreducible chain has a reverse chain with the same stationary distribution; reversibility is the special case where it equals the forward chain.

The reverse transition $\hat P(x, y) = \pi^*(y)P(y, x)/\pi^*(x)$ is a stochastic matrix whenever $\pi^* > 0$: its rows sum to $1$ because $\sum_y \hat P(x, y) = \sum_y \pi^*(y)P(y, x)/\pi^*(x) = \pi^*(x)/\pi^*(x) = 1$ using $\pi^* = \pi^* P$. The reverse chain has the same stationary distribution $\pi^*$ and the same communicating classes as the forward chain. Reversibility is then the single extra condition $\hat P = P$, equivalent to the DBE.

#### *(Metropolis Construction)* Any irreducible proposal $Q$ can be turned into a $\pi^*$-reversible chain via the accept-reject rule.

Given any irreducible proposal $Q(x, y)$, define the [[3.11.1 Metropolis-Hastings|Metropolis-Hastings]] transition $P(x, y) = Q(x, y)\,\alpha(x, y)$ for $y \ne x$, with
$$\alpha(x, y) = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{\pi^*(y)\,Q(y, x)}{\pi^*(x)\,Q(x, y)}\right).$$
This $P$ satisfies the DBE with $\pi^*$ by construction (see MH note's derivation), so $\pi^*$ is stationary. The entire MCMC toolkit (random-walk Metropolis, Gibbs, MALA, HMC) is built on this reversibility-by-design principle.

#### *(Symmetric $P$ Implies Uniform Is Reversible)* If $P(x, y) = P(y, x)$ for all $x, y$, the uniform distribution over $\mathcal{X}$ satisfies the DBE.

Plugging $\pi^*(x) = 1/|\mathcal{X}|$ into the DBE gives $P(x, y) = P(y, x)$, which holds by assumption. Symmetric transition matrices arise for random walks on undirected graphs (transition to each neighbor with probability $1/\deg(x)$ is symmetric iff the graph is regular) and for Ising-style models with symmetric flip probabilities. The reverse is also useful: to design a reversible chain with uniform target, make $P$ symmetric.

# Examples

>[!example]- Birth-death chain is always reversible
>A birth-death chain on $\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ has transitions only to immediate neighbors: $P(n, n+1) = p_n$, $P(n, n-1) = q_n$, $P(n, n) = 1 - p_n - q_n$, with $p_n + q_n \le 1$ and $q_0 = 0$. Every such chain with a proper stationary distribution is reversible.
>
>The DBE $\pi^*(n)\,p_n = \pi^*(n+1)\,q_{n+1}$ gives the recursion
>$$\pi^*(n+1) = \pi^*(n)\,\frac{p_n}{q_{n+1}},$$
>so
>$$\pi^*(n) = \pi^*(0)\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{p_k}{q_{k+1}}.$$
>If $\sum_n \pi^*(n) < \infty$, normalize to get the stationary distribution. Because the DBE is the *only* source of probability flow between adjacent states (there are no jumps skipping neighbors), every birth-death chain that has a stationary distribution is automatically reversible. This is a special structural feature that makes queueing theory computations tractable.

>[!example]- Random walk on a graph
>Let $G = (V, E)$ be a finite connected undirected graph and let $(X_n)$ be the simple random walk: from state $x$, move to a uniformly chosen neighbor. The transition probability is $P(x, y) = 1/\deg(x)$ if $(x, y) \in E$ and $0$ otherwise.
>
>The DBE becomes $\pi^*(x)/\deg(x) = \pi^*(y)/\deg(y)$ whenever $(x, y) \in E$, so $\pi^*(x) \propto \deg(x)$ is a solution. Normalizing,
>$$\pi^*(x) = \frac{\deg(x)}{2|E|},$$
>using $\sum_x \deg(x) = 2|E|$. The random walk on an undirected graph is reversible with this degree-proportional stationary distribution, a standard tool in spectral graph theory and Monte Carlo algorithms.

>[!example]- Cyclic chain: stationary but not reversible
>Let $\mathcal{X} = \{1, 2, 3\}$ with deterministic cyclic transitions: $P(1, 2) = P(2, 3) = P(3, 1) = 1$. The uniform distribution $\pi^* = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$ is stationary: each state has exactly one state flowing into it (with probability $1/3$) and the total inflow balances the probability $1/3$ of being at that state.
>
>But the DBE fails: $\pi^*(1)P(1, 2) = 1/3 \ne 0 = \pi^*(2)P(2, 1)$. Played backward, probability flows $1 \to 3 \to 2 \to 1$, which is *not* the forward pattern. Concretely, if you're shown the sequence $(1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3, \dots)$ and asked whether it is forward or backward, you can tell immediately from the direction of the cycle, so the forward and backward chains are statistically distinguishable.
