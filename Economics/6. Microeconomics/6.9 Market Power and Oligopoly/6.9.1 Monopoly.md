>[!note] Monopoly
>A **monopoly** is a market with one large firm that faces the whole market demand $y = D(p)$. Unlike a price taker, it sets its price. Choosing $p$ is the same as choosing the quantity $y$ and letting the price be the inverse demand $p(y) = D^{-1}(y)$. The firm solves
>$$\max_y\ p(y)\,y - c(y).$$
>- The first-order condition sets **marginal revenue** equal to marginal cost, $MR(y^*) = p'(y^*)\,y^* + p(y^*) = c'_y(y^*)$. MR lies below the price, because $p'(y) < 0$.
>- The second-order condition is $p''(y^*)\,y^* + 2p'(y^*) - c''_{yy}(y^*) < 0$. It holds for linear demand with constant or rising marginal cost.
>- The firm picks $y^*$ where MR meets MC, then charges the price on the demand curve, $p^* = p(y^*)$.
>- Dividing the FOC by $p$ gives the **markup rule**
>$$\frac{p(y^*) - c'_y(y^*)}{p(y^*)} = -p'(y^*)\,\frac{y^*}{p(y^*)} = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{y,p}},$$
>where $\varepsilon_{y,p} = \frac{\partial y}{\partial p}\frac{p}{y} < 0$ is the price elasticity of demand. Very elastic demand gives a low markup. Relatively inelastic demand gives a high markup. If $c'_y > 0$, the price lies where demand is elastic, $\varepsilon_{y,p} < -1$.
>- With linear demand $p = a - by$ and cost $C(y) = cy$, where $a > c > 0$ and $b > 0$, the output, price, and profit are
>$$y^* = \frac{a-c}{2b}, \qquad p^* = \frac{a+c}{2}, \qquad \pi^* = \frac{(a-c)^2}{4b}.$$
>The competitive benchmark is $y = (a-c)/b$ and $p = c$, so the monopoly produces half as much. A rise of $1$ in $c$ or in $a$ raises $p^*$ by $1/2$.
>- Output is too low and the price too high. Units between $y^*$ and the competitive quantity are worth more to buyers than they cost, yet are not produced. The lost surplus is the **deadweight loss**, $(a-c)^2/(8b)$ in the linear case.
>- A price taker is the special case $p'(y) = 0$, where $MR = p$ and the FOC becomes $p = c'_y$. [[6.9.3 Oligopoly]] lies between the two extremes. [[6.9.2 Price Discrimination]] drops the rule that every unit sells at one price.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The elastic-part result in the markup bullet and the closed-form areas such as $(a-c)^2/(8b)$ are extensions. The Properties and Derivation flag the rest.

# Intuition

A price-taking firm can sell one more unit at the market price. A monopolist cannot. It faces the whole demand curve, so one more sale requires a lower price. The lower price applies to every unit it already sells, not only to the last one. Marginal revenue is the price of the extra unit minus the revenue lost on the other $y$ units, $p + p'(y)\,y$. The firm expands while MR exceeds MC and stops where they meet. The tension is between selling more units and keeping a high price on the units already sold.

The diagram uses the market $p(y) = 5 - y$ with constant marginal cost $c = 1$. MR $= 5 - 2y$ starts at the same height as demand and falls twice as fast. It meets MC at $y^*_M = 2$. The price is read off the demand curve above that point, $p^*_M = 3$. A competitive industry with the same costs would produce where demand meets MC, at $y = 4$ and $p = 1$.

# Properties

#### *(Price Comes From Demand)* MR = MC fixes the quantity, and the demand curve fixes the price.

MR is not a price that anyone pays. It measures the change in revenue, so a price read off the MR curve is too low. At $y^*$ it would equal marginal cost. For linear demand $p = a - by$, revenue is $ay - by^2$, so $MR = a - 2by$. MR has the same intercept as demand and twice the slope. It reaches zero at half the quantity where demand reaches zero, which is where revenue peaks.

#### *(Too Little Output)* The monopoly stops while buyers still value extra units above their cost.

At $y^*$ the price exceeds marginal cost. A buyer willing to pay less than $p^*$ but more than MC does not buy, although the trade would benefit both sides. The firm does not cut the price for that buyer, because the cut would apply to all buyers. In the linear case, profit is the rectangle $(p^* - c)\,y^* = (a-c)^2/(4b)$ and consumer surplus is the triangle $(a-c)^2/(8b)$ ([[6.8.5 Consumer and Producer Surplus]]). The deadweight loss is the triangle between demand and MC from $y^*$ to $(a-c)/b$, also $(a-c)^2/(8b)$. The three sum to the competitive total surplus $(a-c)^2/(2b)$. Monopoly turns half of it into profit, leaves a quarter to buyers, and destroys a quarter.

#### *(Half Pass-Through)* With linear demand and constant marginal cost, the price moves by half of any change in marginal cost or in the demand intercept.

The linear formulas give $\partial y^*/\partial c = -1/(2b)$ and $\partial p^*/\partial c = 1/2$, and also $\partial y^*/\partial a = 1/(2b)$ and $\partial p^*/\partial a = 1/2$. Under perfect competition with constant MC, $p = c$, so buyers bear the whole cost increase. The monopolist already prices above cost, and passing on the full increase would cost it too many sales. With linear demand the best compromise is exactly half. The animation raises $c$ from $1$ to $3$ in the market of the diagram. Each step of $1$ in $c$ cuts $y^*$ by $1/2$ and raises $p^*$ by $1/2$.

#### *(Profit Is Not Guaranteed)* A monopoly earns $y^*\,(p^* - AC(y^*))$, which can be large or zero.

Being the only seller does not ensure a profit. Profit per unit is the gap between the price and average cost at $y^*$ ([[6.7.4 Cost Curves]]). With a small fixed cost, AC lies far below demand at $y^*$ and profit is high. With a large fixed cost, the best the firm can reach may be zero. This happens when the demand curve touches the AC curve at $y^*$ and lies below it everywhere else. If demand lies below AC at every output, every positive output loses money and the firm does not produce.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture states the markup rule and that elastic demand means a low markup. The result below and the general comparative statics in the Derivation are extensions.

#### *(Never on the Inelastic Part)* With positive marginal cost, a monopolist always sets a price where $|\varepsilon_{y,p}| > 1$.

When $c'_y > 0$, the left side of the markup rule is below $1$. So $-1/\varepsilon_{y,p} < 1$, which means $\varepsilon_{y,p} < -1$. On the inelastic part, a $1\%$ price rise cuts quantity by less than $1\%$, so revenue rises. Output falls, so cost falls too. Profit rises on both counts, and the firm keeps raising the price until demand becomes elastic. With $c'_y = 0$ the firm maximizes revenue and stops exactly at $\varepsilon_{y,p} = -1$. The revenue rule $\varepsilon_{R,p} = 1 + \varepsilon_{y,p}$ of [[6.8.3 Elasticities]] gives the same conclusion.

# Derivation

We want the FOC, the markup rule, the linear solution, and the comparative statics in $c$. By the product rule, profit $\pi(y) = p(y)\,y - c(y)$ has derivative $\pi'(y) = p'(y)\,y + p(y) - c'_y(y)$. Setting it to zero gives MR = MC, as in [[7.1 Unconstrained Optimality Conditions]]. Rearranging gives $p - c'_y = -p'(y)\,y$, and dividing by $p$ gives $(p - c'_y)/p = -p'(y)\,y/p$. Along the demand curve, $p'(y) = dp/dy$ is the reciprocal of $\partial y/\partial p$. So $-p'(y)\,y/p = -1/\big(\frac{\partial y}{\partial p}\frac{p}{y}\big) = -1/\varepsilon_{y,p}$. The SOC is $\pi''(y) = p''(y)\,y + 2p'(y) - c''_{yy}(y) < 0$.

For $p = a - by$ and $C(y) = cy$, profit is $(a - by)\,y - cy$. The FOC $a - 2by - c = 0$ gives $y^* = (a-c)/(2b)$, which is positive because $a > c$. The SOC holds, since $\pi'' = -2b < 0$. Then $p^* = a - b\,\frac{a-c}{2b} = \frac{a+c}{2}$ and $\pi^* = (p^* - c)\,y^* = \frac{a-c}{2}\cdot\frac{a-c}{2b} = \frac{(a-c)^2}{4b}$.

For a general demand curve with constant marginal cost $c$, the FOC defines $y^*$ implicitly through $F(y; c) = p'(y)\,y + p(y) - c = 0$. By the [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]],
$$\frac{\partial y^*}{\partial c} = -\frac{F'_c}{F'_y} = \frac{1}{p''(y^*)\,y^* + 2p'(y^*)} < 0, \qquad \frac{\partial p^*}{\partial c} = p'(y^*)\,\frac{\partial y^*}{\partial c} = \frac{p'(y^*)}{p''(y^*)\,y^* + 2p'(y^*)}.$$
The denominator is negative by the SOC. Linear demand has $p'' = 0$, so $\partial p^*/\partial c = 1/2$. Constant-elasticity demand $y = kp^{\varepsilon}$ with $\varepsilon < -1$ gives $\partial p^*/\partial c = \varepsilon/(1+\varepsilon) > 1$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Finding the Monopoly Output
>A monopolist has $C(y) = cy$ with $c = 1$ and faces $p(y) = 5 - y$.
>
>>[!example]- Which equation gives $y^*_M$: $5 - y = cy$, $5 - y = c$, or $5 - 2y = c$? Find $y^*_M$, $p^*_M$, profit, consumer surplus, and the deadweight loss.
>>Revenue is $(5 - y)\,y = 5y - y^2$, so $MR = 5 - 2y$, and the right equation is $5 - 2y = c$. It gives $y^*_M = 2$. The price comes from demand, $p^*_M = 5 - 2 = 3$. Reading it off MR would give $1$, the marginal cost. The equation $5 - y = c$ is the competitive rule, and $5 - y = cy$ sets a price equal to a total cost. Profit is $(3 - 1)\cdot 2 = 4$ and $CS = \frac12\cdot 2\cdot(5 - 3) = 2$. Under competition $y = 4$, $p = 1$, and $CS = \frac12\cdot 4\cdot 4 = 8$. The deadweight loss is $\frac12\,(3 - 1)(4 - 2) = 2$. As a check, $4 + 2 + 2 = 8$, and the formulas $(a-c)^2/(4b) = 4$ and $(a-c)^2/(8b) = 2$ agree.

>[!example] (Medium) Two Products, Two Markups
>A software firm sells a consumer app and a business tool. Each extra license costs $c = 20$ in support. Demand for the app has a constant elasticity of $-3$, and demand for the tool a constant elasticity of $-1.5$.
>
>>[!example]- (a) Find the profit-maximizing prices. (b) A consultant reports that a third product sells at a price where its elasticity is $-0.5$. What should the firm do?
>>(a) For the app, $(p - 20)/p = 1/3$, so $p = 30$. For the tool, $(p - 20)/p = 1/1.5 = 2/3$, so $p = 60$. Business users react less to price, so their markup is $2/3$ of the price instead of $1/3$. Constant elasticity makes revenue $y^{1 + 1/\varepsilon}$ times a constant, which is concave for $\varepsilon < -1$, so the SOC holds.
>>
>>(b) Raise the price. At $\varepsilon = -0.5$, a $1\%$ price rise cuts sales by about $0.5\%$, so revenue rises by about $0.5\%$. Fewer licenses also cost less to support. Profit rises on both counts, so the current price is not optimal. The markup rule can hold only where $\varepsilon < -1$.

>[!example] (Hard) Who Bears a Cost Increase
>A drug maker has marginal cost $c = 20$ per dose and sells $y^* = 20$ thousand doses at $p^* = 60$. Two demand models fit these data. Model L is linear, $p = 100 - 2y$. Model E has constant elasticity, $y = k\,p^{-1.5}$ with $k = 20\cdot 60^{1.5} \approx 9295$. An ingredient shortage raises $c$ to $22$.
>
>>[!example]- Predict the new price under each model, and compare with a competitive industry.
>>Both models fit the current point. Under L, $y^* = (100 - 20)/4 = 20$ and $p^* = 60$, and the elasticity there is $-\frac12\cdot\frac{60}{20} = -1.5$. Under E, the markup rule gives $(p - 20)/p = 1/1.5$, so $p = 3c = 60$. Both models also have the same elasticity at that point.
>>
>>With $c = 22$, model L gives $y^* = 78/4 = 19.5$ and $p^* = 61$. The price rises by $1$, half of the cost increase. Model E gives $p^* = 3\cdot 22 = 66$ and $y^* = 9295\cdot 66^{-1.5} \approx 17.3$. The price rises by $6$, three times the cost increase. A competitive industry with constant MC sets $p = c$, so its price rises by $2$.
>>
>>Under E, the elasticity stays at $-1.5$, so the markup stays at $2/3$ of the price and $p = 3c$. Under L, demand becomes more elastic as the price rises. At $p = 61$ the elasticity is $-\frac12\cdot\frac{61}{19.5} = -1.56$, so the markup shrinks. The general formula $\partial p^*/\partial c = p'/(p''y + 2p')$ gives $1/2$ for L and $\varepsilon/(1+\varepsilon) = 3$ for E. One observed point cannot separate the models. The curvature of demand decides how much of the cost increase buyers pay.
