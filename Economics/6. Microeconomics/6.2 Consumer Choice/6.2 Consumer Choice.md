>[!note] Consumer Choice
>This section turns preferences into choices. A consumer with income $M$ faces prices $\mathbf{p} = (p_1, p_2)$, and the [[6.2.1 Budget Constraint|budget constraint]] $p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 \le M$ fixes the affordable bundles. A rise in income shifts the budget line out in parallel. A rise in one price tilts it around the intercept of the other good.
>
>In [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|utility maximization]] the consumer picks the best affordable bundle. With the Lagrangian $u - \lambda(p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 - M)$, an interior optimum satisfies the tangency condition $\text{MRS} = -p_1/p_2$, or $u'_{x_1}/p_1 = u'_{x_2}/p_2 = \lambda$. The solution is the Marshallian demand $x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)$, for example $x_1^* = \alpha M/p_1$ for Cobb-Douglas. [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions]] covers the cases where tangency fails: negative quantities, non-convex preferences, and ties. The [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function|indirect utility]] $v(\mathbf{p}, M)$ is the utility at the optimum, with $\partial v/\partial M = \lambda > 0$ and $\partial v/\partial p_i = -\lambda x_i^* \le 0$.
>
>[[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]] reverses the problem. It fixes a utility level $\bar u$ and finds the cheapest bundle, the Hicksian demand $h_i(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$, at cost $e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$. The two problems are linked by $h_i(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u))$ and by $\partial e/\partial p_i = h_i$. These identities are the input to the Slutsky equation in section 6.3.

# Contents

- [[6.2.1 Budget Constraint]]: the budget set and line, intercepts, slope as the market rate of exchange, parallel shifts and tilts. **Not taught:** the existence argument for a best bundle.
- [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]]: the consumer's problem, Lagrangian, tangency condition, bordered Hessian, Marshallian demand, and the full CES derivation. **Not taught:** the invariance of the bordered Hessian under monotone transformations.
- [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions]]: corners, tangency points that are minima (non-convex preferences, CES with $\rho > 1$), multiple optima, and an exam procedure. **Not taught:** the per-dollar inequality at a corner.
- [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function]]: $v(\mathbf{p}, M)$, the envelope results, $\lambda$ as the marginal utility of income, and why its size is arbitrary. **Not taught:** the chain-rule derivation and Roy's identity.
- [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]]: EMIN, Hicksian demand, the expenditure function, duality, Shephard's lemma, and the full Cobb-Douglas derivation. **Not taught:** $\lambda_{\text{EMIN}} = 1/\lambda_{\text{UMP}}$ and the proof that Hicksian demand slopes down without convexity.

# Recommended Path

Read the notes in order. Each note uses only earlier ones. Notes 6.2.1 and 6.2.2 are the core and carry most exam problems. Note 6.2.3 is short and worth reading before any problem with perfect substitutes, perfect complements, or an unusual utility function. Notes 6.2.4 and 6.2.5 set up the Slutsky equation, and a reader short on time should read 6.2.5 before starting [[6.3 Comparative Statics]].
