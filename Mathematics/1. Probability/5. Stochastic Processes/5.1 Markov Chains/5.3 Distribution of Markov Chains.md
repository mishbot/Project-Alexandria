>[!note] Markov Chain Distribution
>The probability mass function $\pi_{n}: \mathcal{X} \to [0, 1]$ (or $\pi_{n} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times |\mathcal{X}|}$) of the random variable $X_{n}$ represents the probability of the [[5.1 Markov Chain|Markov chain]] being in each possible state in $\mathcal{X}$ at time $n$. It can be used in tandem with the transition probability matrix $P$ to derive the $n$-th distribution in the sequence, such that $\pi_{n}=\pi_{n-1}P$, or equivalently,
>$$\pi_{n} = \pi_{0}P^n.$$
>
>If $\pi^*=\pi^*P$, then $\pi^*$ is said to be an **invariant distribution**. Per the **Big Theorem**, an irreducible Markov chain has a unique invariant distribution $\pi^*$, such that $\pi^*(x)$ is the long-term fraction of time that $X_{n}$ is equal to $x$, defined as
>$$\pi^*(x) = \lim_{ n \to \infty } \frac{1}{n}\sum^{n-1}_{i=0}\mathbb{1}_{\{ X_{i} = x \}}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[T_{x} \mid X_{0} = x]}.$$
>
>Furthermore, if the Markov chain is also [[5.2 Markov Chain Periodicity|aperiodic]], then any $\pi_{n}$ converges to $\pi$, such that $$\lim\limits_{n \to \infty}\pi_{n}(x)=\sum^{}_{y \in \mathcal{X}}\lim\limits_{n \to \infty}\pi_{0}(x)P^n(y, x)=\pi^*(x).$$

# Properties
#### (*Balance Equation*) $\pi^*(x)=\sum\limits^{}_{y \in \mathcal{X}}\pi^*(y)P(y, x)$
