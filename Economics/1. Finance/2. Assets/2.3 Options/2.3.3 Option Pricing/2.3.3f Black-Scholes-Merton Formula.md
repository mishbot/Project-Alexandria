>[!note] Black-Scholes-Merton Formula
>The **Black-Scholes-Merton formula** gives the prices of a European call and a European put on a stock that pays a continuous dividend yield $q$. Let $S$ be the stock price at time $t$, $K$ the strike, $T$ the expiry, $\tau = T - t$ the time to expiry, $r$ the continuously compounded risk-free rate, and $\sigma$ the volatility of the stock's log return. The call and put prices are
>$$c = Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1) - Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2), \qquad p = Ke^{-r\tau}N(-d_2) - Se^{-q\tau}N(-d_1).$$
>The term $Ke^{-r\tau}$ is the present value of the strike. The term $Se^{-q\tau}$ is the stock price minus the present value of the dividends paid before expiry. The numbers $d_1$ and $d_2$ are
>$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r - q + \sigma^2/2)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau}.$$
>Here $N(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x} e^{-u^2/2}\,du$ is the standard normal CDF (cumulative distribution function), the function written $\Phi(x)$ in [[2.2c.3 Gaussian Distribution]]. Its density is $n(x) = N'(x) = e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$. For a stock with no dividends, $q = 0$, and the prices are $c = SN(d_1) - Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2)$ and $p = Ke^{-r\tau}N(-d_2) - SN(-d_1)$.
>
>Both prices are discounted expected payoffs under risk-neutral probabilities. For a payoff $f_T(S_T)$ paid at $T$, the price is
>$$f(S, t) = e^{-r\tau}\,\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[f_T(S_T)\big] = e^{-r\tau}\int_0^{\infty} f_T(S')\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\tau}\,\sigma S'}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\big[\ln S' - \ln S - (r - q - \sigma^2/2)\tau\big]^2}{2\sigma^2\tau}\right)dS'.$$
>The weight is the **risk-neutral density** of $S_T$, a [[2.2c.11 Lognormal Distribution|lognormal]] density. Under it, $\ln S_T$ is normal with mean $\ln S + (r - q - \sigma^2/2)\tau$ and variance $\sigma^2\tau$.
>
>The formula needs the assumptions of the [[2.3.3e Black-Scholes-Merton Equation]]: a frictionless market, a stock that follows geometric Brownian motion with constant $\sigma$, and constant $r$ and $q$. The price depends on $K$, $T$, $t$, $S$, $r$, $q$, and $\sigma$.
>- The contract sets the strike $K$ and the expiry $T$.
>- The market quotes the stock price $S$ and the rate $r$. The yield $q$ comes from the dividends the firm is expected to pay.
>- The volatility $\sigma$ is not quoted. It is estimated from past returns or read from option prices as the [[2.3.5a Implied Volatility|implied volatility]].
>
>The stock's expected return $\mu$ is not an input. Three routes lead to the same formula.
>- The BSM equation, solved with the payoff as the final condition, gives the integral above as a Gaussian average.
>- The payoff averaged under [[2.3.3b Risk-Neutral Valuation|risk-neutral probabilities]] and discounted at $r$ is the same integral.
>- The [[2.3.3c Binomial Tree|binomial tree]] price converges to the formula as the number of steps grows.
>
>An American call on a stock with no dividends is never exercised early ([[2.3.1c Early Exercise]]), so the call formula prices it too. The American put has no formula of this kind and is priced on a tree.

# Intuition

A binomial tree prices an option with a finite number of steps, and its answer depends on how many steps it uses. As the steps shrink to zero, the tree price converges to a single number, and for European options that number has a closed form. The formula can be read as a replicating portfolio. For a stock with no dividends, a call has the same value as $N(d_1)$ shares bought with a loan of $Ke^{-r(T-t)}N(d_2)$. When the stock is far above the strike, both $N(d_1)$ and $N(d_2)$ are close to $1$. The call then behaves like one share bought with a loan of the discounted strike. When the stock is far below the strike, both are close to $0$ and the call is worth almost nothing.

# Properties

#### *(Limits)* At the edges of the inputs, the formula returns the payoff or the no-arbitrage bounds.

As $S \to 0$, both $d_1$ and $d_2$ tend to $-\infty$, so $c \to 0$ and $p \to Ke^{-r\tau}$. As $S \to \infty$, both tend to $+\infty$, so $c \to Se^{-q\tau} - Ke^{-r\tau}$ and $p \to 0$. As $t \to T$, both tend to $+\infty$ if $S > K$ and to $-\infty$ if $S < K$. So $c \to \max(S - K, 0)$ and $p \to \max(K - S, 0)$, the payoffs. As $\sigma \to 0$, the sign of $\ln(S/K) + (r - q)\tau$ decides the direction instead, and the prices tend to
$$c \to \max(Se^{-q\tau} - Ke^{-r\tau}, 0), \qquad p \to \max(Ke^{-r\tau} - Se^{-q\tau}, 0).$$
These are the [[2.3.1b Option Price Bounds|lower bounds]] for European options, with the stock price net of dividends, $Se^{-q\tau}$, in place of $S$. With no volatility, the stock price grows at exactly $r - q$, so each option is worth its discounted payoff on that one path.

#### *(Put From Call)* The put formula follows from the call formula and put-call parity.

The standard normal CDF satisfies $N(x) + N(-x) = 1$. For a stock with dividend yield $q$, [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity|put-call parity]] reads $c - p = Se^{-q\tau} - Ke^{-r\tau}$. Substituting the call formula into $p = c - Se^{-q\tau} + Ke^{-r\tau}$ gives
$$p = Se^{-q\tau}\big[N(d_1) - 1\big] - Ke^{-r\tau}\big[N(d_2) - 1\big] = Ke^{-r\tau}N(-d_2) - Se^{-q\tau}N(-d_1).$$
The right side is the put formula. So only the call needs a derivation, and pricing code needs only one formula plus the parity. Differentiating the parity in $S$ shows that the put's [[2.3.4a Delta|delta]] equals the call's delta minus $e^{-q\tau}$.

#### *(Risk-Neutral Reading)* $N(d_2)$ is the risk-neutral probability that the call ends in the money, and $Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1)$ is the present value of the expected stock received on exercise.

The derivation below splits the call price into a stock part and a cash part,
$$c = e^{-r\tau}\,\hat{\mathbb{E}}\big[S_T\,\mathbf{1}\{S_T > K\}\big] - Ke^{-r\tau}\,\hat{\mathbb{E}}\big[\mathbf{1}\{S_T > K\}\big].$$
Here $\mathbf{1}\{S_T > K\}$ equals $1$ when the call ends in the money (above the strike) and $0$ otherwise. The second expectation is the risk-neutral probability of exercise, and it equals $N(d_2)$. The first term equals $Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1)$, and it counts the stock only in the states where the holder exercises. Both expectations use risk-neutral probabilities, so $N(d_2)$ is not the real-world probability of exercise. Under real-world probabilities the stock price grows at $\mu$, and the chance of exercise is $N$ evaluated at $d_2$ with $r - q$ replaced by $\mu$. For the call in the (Easy) example below, $N(d_2) = 0.3962$. A stock whose price grows at $\mu = 12\%$ ends above the strike with probability $0.4938$.

#### *(Binomial Limit)* The CRR binomial tree price converges to the formula as the number of steps grows.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules state this limit without proof. The argument below is a sketch.

This note writes the number of tree steps as $n$, because $N$ already denotes the normal CDF. [[2.3.3c Binomial Tree]] writes the same number as $N$. For a stock with no dividends, split $T - t$ into $n$ steps of length $\Delta t = (T - t)/n$, with $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$, $d = 1/u$, and $p = (e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)$. Each step adds $+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}$ or $-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}$ to $\ln S$. Under $p$, one step has mean close to $(r - \sigma^2/2)\Delta t$ and variance close to $\sigma^2 \Delta t$. The log of the final price is a sum of $n$ independent steps. By the [[3.3 Central Limit Theorem]] it becomes normal as $n \to \infty$, with mean $\ln S + (r - \sigma^2/2)(T - t)$ and variance $\sigma^2(T - t)$. This is the risk-neutral distribution in the derivation below, so the tree's discounted expected payoff tends to the formula. The convergence is not monotone. In the (Medium) example below, the tree price is below the formula for $99$ steps, above it for $100$, and below it again for $101$.

#### *(Volatility Input)* The call price rises with $\sigma$, so a market price of the call matches exactly one value of $\sigma$.

There are two ways to choose $\sigma$. The first estimates it from past returns of the stock, as in [[3.1f Historical Volatility]]. The second reads it from option prices. The sensitivity of the price to $\sigma$, the vega of [[2.3.4c Vega and Rho]], is positive for every call and put. As $\sigma$ runs from $0$ to $\infty$, the call price rises from $\max(Se^{-q\tau} - Ke^{-r\tau}, 0)$ to $Se^{-q\tau}$. The value of $\sigma$ that reproduces a quoted call price inside these bounds is the [[2.3.5a Implied Volatility|implied volatility]]. Put-call parity does not involve $\sigma$, so the put with the same strike and expiry implies the same value. In real markets, implied volatilities differ across strikes and expiries ([[2.3.5b Volatility Surface]]). This shows that a single constant $\sigma$ is only an approximation.

#### *(Dividend Yield)* A dividend yield lowers the call price and raises the put price, because an option holder receives no dividends.

The stock price and the yield enter the formula only through $Se^{-q\tau}$, since $\ln(S/K) - q\tau = \ln(Se^{-q\tau}/K)$. So a call on a stock with yield $q$ is priced as a call on a stock with no dividends whose price today is $Se^{-q\tau}$. That amount equals $e^{-r\tau}F_0$, where $F_0 = Se^{(r - q)\tau}$ is the [[2.2.2b Forward Price|forward price]] of the stock. Paying dividends lowers the share price, so the forward price falls as $q$ rises. A higher $q$ thus lowers the call and raises the put. In the (Easy) example below, a $2.5\%$ yield lowers the call from $\$3.61$ to $\$3.32$ and raises the put from $\$4.62$ to $\$4.93$. On a dividend stock, early exercise of an American call can pay, so the call formula no longer prices it ([[2.3.1c Early Exercise]]).

# Derivation

We first read the solution of the BSM equation as an expectation, and then compute that expectation for a call.

## Risk-Neutral Density

The solution of the [[2.3.3e Black-Scholes-Merton Equation|BSM equation]] is a Gaussian average of the payoff over final log prices $X'$,
$$f(S, t) = e^{-r\tau}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_T\big(e^{X'}\big)\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\tau}\,\sigma}\,e^{-(X - X')^2/(2\sigma^2\tau)}\,dX', \qquad X = \ln S + \Big(r - q - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)\tau.$$
The weight is a normal density in $X'$ with mean $X$ and variance $\sigma^2\tau$. Change the variable to the final stock price $S' = e^{X'}$. Then $dX' = dS'/S'$, and $X'$ from $-\infty$ to $\infty$ becomes $S'$ from $0$ to $\infty$. The price becomes the integral in the callout,
$$f(S, t) = e^{-r\tau}\int_0^{\infty} f_T(S')\,p(S')\,dS', \qquad p(S') = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\tau}\,\sigma S'}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\big(\ln S' - X\big)^2}{2\sigma^2\tau}\right).$$
The function $p$ is the density of a lognormal variable whose log is normal with mean $X$ and variance $\sigma^2\tau$.

Now compare $p$ with the stock. Under risk-neutral probabilities the stock follows $dS = (r - q)S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t$. By the solution of [[7.6 Geometric Brownian Motion|geometric Brownian motion]], the price at expiry is
$$S_T = S\exp\!\left(\left(r - q - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)\tau + \sigma\sqrt{\tau}\,Z\right),$$
where $Z$ is a standard normal random variable. So $\ln S_T$ is normal with mean $X$ and variance $\sigma^2\tau$, and $p$ is the density of $S_T$ under risk-neutral probabilities. The integral is therefore an expectation, $f(S, t) = e^{-r\tau}\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t[f_T(S_T)]$. The hedging argument behind the equation and the risk-neutral average give the same price. [[7.8 Feynman-Kac Formula]] states this link in general.

## Call Price

With the density known, the goal is
$$c = e^{-r\tau}\,\hat{\mathbb{E}}\big[\max(S_T - K, 0)\big] = Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1) - Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2).$$
The expectation $\hat{\mathbb{E}}$ is taken over the stock price at expiry, given the price $S$ today.

Split the payoff into a stock part and a cash part, $\max(S_T - K, 0) = S_T\,\mathbf{1}\{S_T > K\} - K\,\mathbf{1}\{S_T > K\}$. The call price is then the difference of two discounted expectations.

The call is exercised when $\ln S_T > \ln K$. Solving for $Z$ shows this happens exactly when
$$Z > -\frac{\ln(S/K) + (r - q - \sigma^2/2)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} = -d_2.$$
The middle expression equals $-d_2$ because $d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau}$ subtracts $\sigma^2\tau$ from the numerator of $d_1$. The normal distribution is symmetric, so $\hat{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}\{S_T > K\}] = \mathbb{P}[Z > -d_2] = N(d_2)$. The cash part is therefore $Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2)$.

For the stock part, write the expectation as an integral against the normal density over the exercise region $z > -d_2$:
$$\hat{\mathbb{E}}\big[S_T\,\mathbf{1}\{S_T > K\}\big] = \int_{-d_2}^{\infty} S\,e^{(r - q - \sigma^2/2)\tau + \sigma\sqrt{\tau}\,z}\;\frac{e^{-z^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,dz.$$
The integrand is the stock price in state $z$ times the density of that state. Completing the square in the exponent gives
$$\left(r - q - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)\tau + \sigma\sqrt{\tau}\,z - \frac{z^2}{2} = (r - q)\tau - \frac{(z - \sigma\sqrt{\tau})^2}{2}.$$
Both sides expand to the same quadratic in $z$. The factor $e^{(r - q)\tau}$ moves out of the integral, and what remains is the density of a normal variable with mean $\sigma\sqrt{\tau}$ and variance $1$. Its integral over $z > -d_2$ is $\mathbb{P}[Z + \sigma\sqrt{\tau} > -d_2] = \mathbb{P}[Z > -d_1] = N(d_1)$. So the stock part is $Se^{(r - q)\tau}N(d_1)$, and discounting at $r$ gives $Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1)$.

Subtracting the cash part from the stock part gives $c = Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1) - Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2)$. The put follows from the same steps with the indicator $\mathbf{1}\{S_T < K\}$, or from put-call parity.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Pricing a Call and a Put
>A stock trades at $\$48$ and pays a continuous dividend yield of $2.5\%$ per year. Its volatility is $30\%$ per year, and the risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A trader wants prices for six-month European options with strike $\$50$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the call and put prices with the formula, and check that they satisfy put-call parity.
>>Here $\tau = 0.5$. The three pieces of $d_1$ are $\ln(48/50) = -0.04082$, $(r - q + \sigma^2/2)\tau = (0.04 - 0.025 + 0.045)(0.5) = 0.03000$, and $\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = 0.30 \times 0.7071 = 0.21213$. So
>>$$d_1 = \frac{-0.04082 + 0.03000}{0.21213} = -0.0510, \qquad d_2 = -0.0510 - 0.2121 = -0.2631.$$
>>A normal table gives $N(d_1) = 0.4797$ and $N(d_2) = 0.3962$. The stock price net of dividends is $48e^{-0.0125} = \$47.40$, and the discounted strike is $50e^{-0.02} = \$49.01$. The call price is
>>$$c = 47.40 \times 0.4797 - 49.01 \times 0.3962 = 22.74 - 19.42 = \$3.32.$$
>>For the put, $N(-d_1) = 0.5203$ and $N(-d_2) = 0.6038$, so $p = 49.01 \times 0.6038 - 47.40 \times 0.5203 = 29.59 - 24.66 = \$4.93$.
>>
>>Parity for a stock with dividend yield $q$ requires $c - p = Se^{-q\tau} - Ke^{-r\tau}$. The left side is $3.32 - 4.93 = -\$1.61$, and the right side is $47.40 - 49.01 = -\$1.61$. The put costs more because the stock price net of dividends is below the discounted strike. Without the dividend yield, the same options would cost $\$3.61$ and $\$4.62$.

>[!example] (Medium) Tree Against Formula
>A stock trades at $\$60$, has volatility $25\%$ per year, and pays no dividends. The risk-free rate is $5\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A desk prices a six-month European call with strike $\$55$ on a CRR tree and wants to know how many steps it needs.

>>$$p = \frac{1.012578 - 0.882497}{1.133148 - 0.882497} = 0.5190.$$
>>The three final prices are $Su^2 = \$77.04$, $Sud = \$60.00$, and $Sd^2 = \$46.73$, with call payoffs $\$22.04$, $\$5.00$, and $\$0$. One step back, the discount factor is $e^{-0.0125} = 0.9876$, and
>>$$f_u = 0.9876\,(0.5190 \times 22.04 + 0.4810 \times 5.00) = \$13.67, \qquad f_d = 0.9876\,(0.5190 \times 5.00) = \$2.56.$$
>>Today the tree gives $0.9876\,(0.5190 \times 13.67 + 0.4810 \times 2.56) = \$8.22$.
>>
>>For the formula, $d_1 = 0.7220$ and $d_2 = 0.5452$, so $N(d_1) = 0.7649$ and $N(d_2) = 0.7072$. The discounted strike is $55e^{-0.025} = \$53.64$, and $c = 45.892 - 37.936 = \$7.96$. The two-step tree overprices the call by more than $3\%$.
>>
>>A 100-step tree, computed with the same backward recursion, gives $\$7.96$, within one cent of the formula. The error does not shrink smoothly. With $99$ or $101$ steps the tree gives $\$7.95$. For this call, about $100$ steps bring the error below one cent.

>A stock trades at $\$80$ and pays no dividends. A one-year European call has strike $\$80$, so it is at the money ($S = K$). The volatility is $40\%$ per year, and the risk-free rate is $5\%$.

>>$$c = 80 \times 0.6274 - 80e^{-0.05} \times 0.4701 = 50.19 - 35.77 = \$14.42.$$
>>
>>The number $N(d_2) = 0.4701$ is the risk-neutral probability that the stock ends above $\$80$. It is below one half even though the stock starts at the strike and grows at $5\%$ on average. Under risk-neutral probabilities the median of $S_T$ is $Se^{(r - \sigma^2/2)\tau} = 80e^{-0.03} = \$77.64$, below the strike. The mean is higher, $80e^{0.05} = \$84.10$, because a lognormal price has a long right tail. The number $N(d_1) = 0.6274$ is the hedge ratio. A dealer who sells the call holds $0.6274$ shares per call. The product $SN(d_1) = \$50.19$ is the present value of the expected stock delivered on exercise.
>>
>>As $T - t \to 0$, both $d_1$ and $d_2$ shrink in proportion to $\sqrt{\tau}$, so $N(d_1)$ and $N(d_2)$ both tend to one half. Over a very short horizon the stock is equally likely to end above or below the strike. With $\tau = 0.01$, $N(d_1) = 0.5130$, $N(d_2) = 0.4970$, and $c = \$1.30$. The price tends to zero in proportion to $\sqrt{\tau}$. When $r\tau$ is small, $c \approx S\,[N(d_1) - N(d_2)] \approx S \cdot n(0)\,\sigma\sqrt{\tau}$, and $n(0) = 1/\sqrt{2\pi} \approx 0.4$. This gives $c \approx 0.4 \times 80 \times 0.40 \times 0.1 = \$1.28$.
>>
>>As $\sigma \to 0$, the numerator of $d_1$ and $d_2$ tends to $r\tau = 0.05 > 0$ while the denominator tends to $0$. So both tend to $+\infty$, and $N(d_1)$ and $N(d_2)$ tend to $1$. With no risk, the stock grows for certain to $\$84.10$, above the strike, so the call is exercised for certain. Its price tends to $S - Ke^{-r\tau} = 80 - 76.10 = \$3.90$, the interest saved by paying the strike a year later. At $\sigma = 5\%$, $N(d_2)$ is already $0.8352$ and $c = \$4.23$.
