>[!note] Logistic Regression
>**Logistic regression** models a binary label $y \in \{0, 1\}$ as a Bernoulli variable. Its probability is a sigmoid of a linear function of the input $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$:
>$$p(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{P}(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}) = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} + b), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}.$$
>Here $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are the weights and $b$ is the bias. The **sigmoid** $\sigma$ maps any real number into $(0, 1)$. Inverting it shows that the model is linear in the **log-odds**:
>$$\log\frac{p(\mathbf{x})}{1 - p(\mathbf{x})} = \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} + b.$$
>The left side is the **logit** of $p$, and $p/(1-p)$ is the **odds**. Raising $x_j$ by one unit multiplies the odds by $e^{w_j}$. Predicting $y = 1$ when $p(\mathbf{x}) \ge 1/2$ is the same as predicting it when $\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} + b \ge 0$. The decision boundary is therefore a hyperplane, as for any [[4.2. Linear Discriminants|linear discriminant]].
>
>The parameters are fit by [[1.2.1 Maximum Likelihood Estimation|maximum likelihood]]. With $p_i = p(\mathbf{x}_i)$, label $y_i$ has Bernoulli probability $p_i^{y_i}(1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i}$. The negative log-likelihood of $n$ independent samples is the **binary cross-entropy** loss:
>$$L(\mathbf{w}, b) = -\sum_{i=1}^n \big[y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i)\big].$$
>Each sample contributes $-\log p_i$ when $y_i = 1$ and $-\log(1 - p_i)$ when $y_i = 0$. Absorb $b$ into $\mathbf{w}$ by adding a column of ones to the design matrix $\mathbf{X}$, whose rows are the samples. Let $\mathbf{p}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ be the vectors of the $p_i$ and the $y_i$. The gradient and the Hessian are
>$$\nabla L = \mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}), \qquad \nabla^2 L = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{X}, \qquad \mathbf{S} = \operatorname{diag}\big(p_i(1 - p_i)\big).$$
>The Hessian is positive semidefinite, so $L$ is [[2.2 Convex Functions|convex]] and every stationary point is a global minimum. Setting the gradient to zero gives nonlinear equations with **no closed-form solution**. Two solvers are standard:
>
>- **Gradient methods**: [[6.4. Gradient Descent|gradient descent]] or [[6.4.1. Stochastic Gradient Descent|SGD]] take steps $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\,\mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y})$ with learning rate $\eta$.
>- **Newton's method**: each step sets $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y})$ and then recomputes $\mathbf{p}$ and $\mathbf{S}$ ([[6.2. Newton's Method]]). For this loss it is called **iteratively reweighted least squares** (IRLS).
>
>The minimizer is unique and finite when $\mathbf{X}$ has full column rank and the two classes overlap. Overlap here means that no hyperplane separates the classes, even with some points lying on it. On **separable data** $L$ has no finite minimizer, and the weights grow without bound. An $L_2$ penalty fixes this. The penalized fit minimizes $L(\mathbf{w}, b) + \lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$ with $\lambda > 0$, usually leaving $b$ unpenalized, and adds $2\lambda\mathbf{w}$ to the gradient. When both classes appear in the data, the penalized loss is strictly convex and has a unique finite minimizer. This fit is the MAP estimate under a Gaussian prior on $\mathbf{w}$. [[3.8.3 Bayesian Logistic Regression]] treats the full posterior.

# Intuition

Linear regression on $0/1$ labels predicts numbers outside $[0, 1]$, and points far from the boundary pull its fit. Logistic regression keeps the linear score $\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x} + b$ and passes it through the sigmoid, which turns any score into a probability. Large positive scores give probabilities near $1$, and large negative scores give probabilities near $0$. A score of $0$ gives $1/2$. The name comes from the logistic function $\sigma$. The model counts as a "regression" because it fits the log-odds as a linear function of $\mathbf{x}$.

The fit asks which weights make the observed labels most probable. A confident mistake costs a lot. Predicting $p = 0.01$ for a positive example costs $-\log 0.01 = 4.6$, while $p = 0.5$ costs $0.69$. The gradient $\mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y})$ has the same form as the least-squares gradient, with the residual $p_i - y_i$ in place of $\hat y_i - y_i$. Each example changes the weights in proportion to the error in its probability.

# Properties

#### *(Separable Data Diverges)* On separable data the loss approaches zero only as the weights grow without bound.

Suppose $(\mathbf{w}, b)$ puts every positive example on the positive side and every negative example on the negative side. Scaling both by $c > 0$ keeps the boundary and pushes each $p_i$ toward its label, so $L(c\mathbf{w}, cb) \to 0$ as $c \to \infty$. Every finite $\mathbf{w}$ has $L > 0$, so no minimizer exists. Gradient descent then never converges to a finite point. Soudry et al. (2018) showed that $\|\mathbf{w}\|$ grows like $\log t$ after $t$ steps. The direction $\mathbf{w}/\|\mathbf{w}\|$ converges to the hard-margin [[4.4. Support Vector Machines|SVM]] direction, with the bias treated as one more weight. The fitted probabilities approach $0$ and $1$, so the model overstates its confidence. In quasi-complete separation, a few points sit exactly on the boundary. That case also has no finite maximum likelihood estimate (Albert and Anderson 1984). An $L_2$ penalty or early stopping keeps the weights finite.

#### *(Reading the Coefficients)* The factor $e^{w_j}$ is an odds ratio, and $|w_j|/4$ bounds the slope of the probability.

The slope of the probability is $\partial p/\partial x_j = w_j\,p(1 - p)$. It peaks at $p = 1/2$, where it equals $w_j/4$. Gelman and Hill (2007) call this the "divide by 4" rule. Near $p = 0$ or $p = 1$, the same $w_j$ barely moves the probability, although it still multiplies the odds by $e^{w_j}$. Coefficients are comparable across features only when the features share a scale, so standardize them first. With correlated features, individual $w_j$ can change sign between samples, as in [[3.1a. Multiple Linear Regression|multiple linear regression]].

#### *(Newton Is Weighted Least Squares)* Each Newton step solves a weighted least-squares problem, which explains the name IRLS.

Define the **working response** $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{S}^{-1}(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{p})$. The Newton step then becomes $\mathbf{w}_{\text{new}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{z}$. This is [[6.1. Least Squares|least squares]] of $\mathbf{z}$ on $\mathbf{X}$ with weights $p_i(1 - p_i)$. Points with $p_i$ near $1/2$ get the largest weights. IRLS usually converges in under ten steps, and quadratically near the solution. Each step costs $O(nd^2 + d^3)$ for the weighted normal equations. For large $d$, quasi-Newton methods such as L-BFGS or SGD replace it. Separable or nearly separable data make $\mathbf{S}$ nearly singular, and the Newton steps blow up.

#### *(Probabilities Match Counts)* At the maximum likelihood estimate, the fitted probabilities match the observed labels along every feature.

The condition $\mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{0}$ says $\sum_i x_{ij}\,p_i = \sum_i x_{ij}\,y_i$ for every column $j$. The intercept column gives $\sum_i p_i = \sum_i y_i$. The average predicted probability on the training set therefore equals the observed rate of positives. The same balance holds within any group that a binary feature marks. This is a weak form of calibration. It says nothing about groups that the features do not encode, and it holds only on the training data. [[2.2.4. Log Loss]] covers calibration checks on new data.

#### *(Why Not Squared Error)* Cross-entropy keeps a large gradient on confident mistakes, and squared error on the probability does not.

The squared error $(p_i - y_i)^2$ has gradient $2(p_i - y_i)\,p_i(1 - p_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i$ with respect to $\mathbf{w}$. When a positive example gets $p_i \approx 0$, the factor $p_i(1 - p_i)$ is near zero, so the worst mistakes barely change the weights. Squared error composed with the sigmoid is also not convex in $\mathbf{w}$. Cross-entropy cancels the factor $p_i(1 - p_i)$ and leaves the gradient $(p_i - y_i)\mathbf{x}_i$, which is near $-\mathbf{x}_i$ for that example. Prince (*Understanding Deep Learning*, sec. 5.4) derives the same loss for networks with a sigmoid output.

# Derivation

We want the sigmoid from the log-odds, then the gradient and the Hessian. Setting $\log(p/(1 - p)) = z$ and solving for $p$ gives
$$p = \frac{e^z}{1 + e^z} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} = \sigma(z).$$
Differentiating gives $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$. Write $z_i = \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{x}_i$, with the bias inside $\mathbf{w}$. Substituting $p_i = \sigma(z_i)$ into the loss of sample $i$ gives
$$\ell_i = -y_i\log\sigma(z_i) - (1 - y_i)\log\big(1 - \sigma(z_i)\big) = \log\big(1 + e^{z_i}\big) - y_i z_i.$$
Implementations use this form, since it never takes the logarithm of a number that has rounded to $0$. Its derivative is $\partial\ell_i/\partial z_i = \sigma(z_i) - y_i = p_i - y_i$. By the chain rule, $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\ell_i = (p_i - y_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i$, and summing over samples gives $\mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y})$. Differentiating $p_i\mathbf{x}_i$ once more gives $p_i(1 - p_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{x}_i^T$, and the sum is $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{X}$. For any vector $\mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v} = \sum_i p_i(1 - p_i)(\mathbf{x}_i^T\mathbf{v})^2 \ge 0$, which proves convexity.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Odds of a Hospital Readmission
>A hospital predicts 30-day readmission with $z = -3 + 0.8\,x_1 + 0.2\,x_2$. Here $x_1$ is the number of prior admissions and $x_2$ is age in decades. A patient is 60 years old and has two prior admissions.
>
>>[!example]- What is the predicted probability? How do the odds and the probability change with one more prior admission?
>>The score is $z = -3 + 1.6 + 1.2 = -0.2$, so $p = \sigma(-0.2) = 0.450$, and the odds are $e^{-0.2} = 0.819$. One more admission multiplies the odds by $e^{0.8} = 2.23$, giving odds $1.82$ and $p = \sigma(0.6) = 0.646$. The probability rises by $0.196$. The slope at this patient is $0.8 \cdot 0.45 \cdot 0.55 = 0.198$, close to the bound $0.8/4 = 0.2$, because $p$ is near $1/2$. A patient with $p = 0.05$ would gain only about $0.8 \cdot 0.05 \cdot 0.95 = 0.04$ per admission at first.

>[!example] (Medium) Newton's Method on Exam Data
>Four students studied $x = 1, 2, 3, 4$ hours. The second and fourth passed, so $\mathbf{y} = (0, 1, 0, 1)$. The model is $p = \sigma(b + wx)$, started at $b = w = 0$.
>
>>[!example]- Take one Newton step by hand. Where does the iteration end?
>>At the start every $p_i = 1/2$, so $\mathbf{S} = \tfrac{1}{4}\mathbf{I}$. The Newton step becomes $4(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{y} - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1})$, four times the least-squares fit of $\mathbf{y} - \tfrac{1}{2}$ on $\mathbf{X}$. Here $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 10 \\ 10 & 30 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{y} - \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}) = (0, 1)$. The least-squares fit is $(-0.5, 0.2)$, so the step goes to $(b, w) = (-2, 0.8)$. The loss falls from $4\log 2 = 2.773$ to $2.353$. Three more steps converge to $b = -2.270$ and $w = 0.908$, with loss $2.347$. The fitted probabilities are $0.204, 0.388, 0.612, 0.796$. They sum to $2$, the number of students who passed.

>[!example] (Hard) Separable Exam Data
>Now the two students with the most hours passed, so $\mathbf{y} = (0, 0, 1, 1)$ for $x = 1, 2, 3, 4$.
>
>>[!example]- Show that the unpenalized loss has no minimizer. Then fit with the penalty $\lambda w^2$ for $\lambda = 0.1$.
>>Take $w = c$ and $b = -2.5c$, which puts the boundary at $x = 2.5$. The scores are $c\,(x - 2.5) = \pm 0.5c, \pm 1.5c$, each with the correct sign. The loss is $L(c) = 2\log(1 + e^{-c/2}) + 2\log(1 + e^{-3c/2})$. It equals $1.351$ at $c = 1$, $0.0134$ at $c = 10$ and $9.1 \times 10^{-5}$ at $c = 20$. It approaches $0$ but never reaches it, so no finite weights minimize it. With $\lambda = 0.1$, numerical minimization gives $w = 2.023$ and $b = -5.056$. By symmetry the boundary stays at $x = 2.5$. The fitted probabilities are $0.046, 0.267, 0.733, 0.954$. A weaker penalty, $\lambda = 0.01$, gives $w = 4.633$ and end probabilities $0.001$ and $0.999$. A larger $\lambda$ keeps the weights smaller and the probabilities farther from $0$ and $1$.
