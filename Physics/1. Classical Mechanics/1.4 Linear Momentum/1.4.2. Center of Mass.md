>[!note] Center of Mass
>The **center of mass** (CoM) is the average position of mass in a system, weighted by how much mass is at each point. It denotes where the “balance point” of the system is. For a system of particles with masses $m_i$ at positions $\vec{r}_i$, the center of mass is defined as
>$$\vec{r}_{\text{C.M.}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_i m_i \vec{r}_i \implies \vec{v}_{\text{C.M.}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_i m_i \vec{v}_i, \vec{a}_{\text{C.M.}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_i m_i \vec{a}_i,$$
>where $M = \sum_i m_i$ is the total mass of the system.
>
>For continuous objects and systems, the center of mass is similarly defined as
>$$\vec{r}_{\text{C.M.}} = \frac{1}{M} \int \vec{r} \space dm,$$
>where $dm$ represents an **infinitesimal element of mass** (the continuous analogue of $m_{i}$) and receives multiple forms depending on the dimensions of the system:
>- For 1D systems (e.g. a rod), $dm=\lambda(x)dx$, such that $\lambda$ is the linear mass density of the object (SI: $\text{kg} / \text{m}$).
>- For 2D systems (e.g. a plate), $dm = \sigma(x, y)dA$, where $\sigma$ is the surface mass density of the object (SI: $\text{kg} / \text{m}^2$).
>- For 3D systems (e.g. a sphere), $dm=\rho(x, y, z)dV$, where $\rho$ is the volume mass density of the object (SI: $\text{kg} / \text{m}^2$).
>
>The center of mass moves as if all the mass were concentrated at that point and all external forces were applied there. If **no external force** acts on a system, then the **center of mass moves at constant velocity**, regardless of internal motion or deformation.
>
>As such, the **momentum of the center of mass** equals the **total momentum** of the system, where
>$$\vec{P} = M \vec{v}_{\text{C.M.}}.$$

The constant $\lambda_{0}$ has a dimension of mass per unit length.

>>[!example]- Find the center of mass of the $L$-shaped bracket.
>>The rods are continuous, so we find that the two masses in this system are $m_{0} = \lambda_{0}L$ and $m_{1} = \frac{\lambda_{0}}{L^2} \int_{0}^{L}x^2dx = \dfrac{1}{3}\lambda L$. As such, $M=\frac{4}{3}\lambda_{0}L$ is the total mass of the system.
>>
>>Then, we calculate the first moments of both masses,
>>$$M_{x}=\frac{\lambda_{0}}{L^2} \int_{0}^{L} x^3 =\frac{1}{4}\lambda_{0}L^2,$$
>>and
>>$$M_{y} = \lambda_{0}\int_{0}^{L} x dx = \frac{1}{2}\lambda_{0}L^2$$
>>
>>Finally, we obtain $x_{\text{C.M.}} = \frac{\frac{1}{4}\lambda_{0}L^2}{\frac{4}{3}\lambda_{0}L}= \dfrac{3}{16}L$, and $y_{\text{C.M.}} = \frac{\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{0}L^2}{\frac{4}{3}\lambda_{0}L}=\dfrac{3}{8}L$.
