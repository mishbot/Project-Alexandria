>[!note] Beta-Binomial Distribution
>A discrete random variable $X \in \{0, 1, \dots, n\}$ follows the **Beta-Binomial distribution** $X \sim \text{BetaBin}(n, \alpha, \beta)$ with $n \in \mathbb{N}$ trials and shape parameters $\alpha > 0$, $\beta > 0$, defined by the [[2.2b.1 Probability Mass Function|probability mass function]]
>$$p_X(x) = \binom{n}{x}\frac{B(x + \alpha,\; n - x + \beta)}{B(\alpha, \beta)},$$
>where $B(\alpha,\beta) = \dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}$ is the [[2.2c.9 Beta Distribution|Beta function]].
>
>The Beta-Binomial distribution is a **compound distribution**: it arises from a [[2.2b.3 Binomial Distribution|Binomial]] experiment in which the success probability $p$ is not fixed, but is itself a random variable drawn from a $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ prior. Formally, if
>$$\Theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta), \qquad X \mid \Theta \sim \text{Bin}(n, \Theta),$$
>then the marginal distribution of $X$ (obtained by integrating out $\Theta$) is $\text{BetaBin}(n, \alpha, \beta)$. This can be verified by computing
>$$p_X(x) = \int_0^1 \binom{n}{x}\theta^x(1-\theta)^{n-x} \cdot \frac{\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\,d\theta = \binom{n}{x}\frac{B(x+\alpha,\; n-x+\beta)}{B(\alpha,\beta)}.$$
>
>A key characteristic of the Beta-Binomial is **overdispersion**: because the success probability varies across trials (in the sense that it is drawn once from a Beta and then held fixed), the variance of $X$ exceeds that of a Binomial with the same mean. This makes the Beta-Binomial appropriate whenever count data exhibits more variability than the Binomial alone can explain.
>
>Special cases:
>- If $\alpha = \beta = 1$, the success probability is $\text{Uniform}(0,1)$, and $p_X(x) = \frac{1}{n+1}$ for all $x$—a **discrete uniform** over $\{0, \dots, n\}$.
>- As $\alpha + \beta \to \infty$ with $\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta} = p$ held constant, the Beta prior concentrates at $p$ and $\text{BetaBin}(n, \alpha, \beta) \to \text{Bin}(n, p)$.
>
>>[!warning] Exchangeability, not Independence
>>In the Beta-Binomial model, the $n$ underlying Bernoulli trials are **exchangeable but not independent**. Conditional on $\Theta = \theta$ they are i.i.d., but marginally (after integrating out $\Theta$) they are positively correlated: observing a success makes the next trial more likely to succeed, because it shifts the posterior of $\Theta$ upward. This is a manifestation of [[1.6 Bayes' Rule|Bayes' rule]] in action.

# Intuition
The Beta-Binomial answers a natural question: what happens when you run a Binomial experiment but **don't know the true success probability**? In a standard Binomial, $p$ is fixed and known—every trial has the same chance of success. But in many real situations, $p$ is uncertain or varies across groups. The Beta-Binomial handles this by placing a [[2.2c.9 Beta Distribution|Beta]] prior on $p$ and averaging over all possible values.

Think of it as a two-stage process. First, nature draws a success probability $\theta$ from $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$. Then, $n$ Bernoulli trials are conducted with that $\theta$. Because $\theta$ is random, the resulting count $X$ has more spread than a plain Binomial—sometimes $\theta$ is high and you see many successes, sometimes it is low and you see few. This extra variability is what makes the Beta-Binomial useful for modeling **heterogeneous populations**, where different subgroups may have different underlying rates.

# Properties
#### (*Mean*) $\mathbb{E}[X \sim \text{BetaBin}(n, \alpha, \beta)] = \dfrac{n\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}$.
This is the same as $n \cdot \mathbb{E}[\Theta]$, which follows from the law of iterated expectation: $\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \Theta]] = \mathbb{E}[n\Theta] = n \cdot \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}$.

#### (*Variance*) $\text{Var}[X \sim \text{BetaBin}(n, \alpha, \beta)] = \dfrac{n\alpha\beta(\alpha + \beta + n)}{(\alpha + \beta)^2(\alpha + \beta + 1)}$.
By the law of total variance, $\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[\text{Var}(X \mid \Theta)] + \text{Var}(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \Theta])$. The first term gives the average within-group binomial variance; the second captures the between-group variability due to the random $\Theta$. The result can be rewritten as
$$\text{Var}(X) = np(1-p)\left(1 + \frac{(n-1)\rho}{1}\right), \qquad p = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}, \quad \rho = \frac{1}{\alpha+\beta+1},$$
where $\rho$ is the **intraclass correlation**. Since $\rho > 0$, the variance always exceeds the Binomial variance $np(1-p)$.

#### (*Overdispersion Factor*) The ratio $\text{Var}(X) / [np(1-p)] = 1 + (n-1)\rho > 1$ for $n \geq 2$, confirming that the Beta-Binomial is overdispersed relative to the [[2.2b.3 Binomial Distribution|Binomial]].
#### (*Binomial Limit*) As $\alpha + \beta \to \infty$ with $\alpha / (\alpha + \beta) = p$ fixed, the Beta prior concentrates at $p$, $\rho \to 0$, and $\text{BetaBin}(n, \alpha, \beta) \to \text{Bin}(n, p)$.
#### (*Symmetry*) $\text{BetaBin}(n, \alpha, \beta)$ satisfies $p_X(x) = p_X(n - x)$ if and only if $\alpha = \beta$, mirroring the symmetry of the $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha)$ prior about $1/2$.
#### (*Marginal Likelihood*) In [[1.2.2 Maximum a Posteriori Estimation|Bayesian inference]], the normalizing constant of the posterior $\Theta \mid X$ is exactly the Beta-Binomial PMF. That is, $p_X(x) = \binom{n}{x}\frac{B(x+\alpha, n-x+\beta)}{B(\alpha,\beta)}$ serves as the **marginal likelihood** (or evidence) used for Bayesian model comparison.

# Examples
>[!example] Defective Items Across Factories
>A company operates many factories, each producing $n = 20$ items per batch. The defect rate $\theta$ varies from factory to factory according to $\Theta \sim \text{Beta}(2, 18)$, so that the average defect rate is $\mathbb{E}[\Theta] = 2/20 = 0.1$. Let $X$ be the number of defective items in a randomly chosen batch.
>>[!example]- What is the distribution of $X$, and how does its variance compare to a Binomial with $p = 0.1$?
>>Since $\Theta \sim \text{Beta}(2, 18)$ and $X \mid \Theta \sim \text{Bin}(20, \Theta)$, we have $X \sim \text{BetaBin}(20, 2, 18)$. The mean is
>>$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{20 \cdot 2}{2 + 18} = 2,$$
>>which matches $\text{Bin}(20, 0.1)$. However, the variance is
>>$$\text{Var}(X) = \frac{20 \cdot 2 \cdot 18 \cdot (20 + 20)}{20^2 \cdot 21} = \frac{20 \cdot 2 \cdot 18 \cdot 40}{400 \cdot 21} = \frac{14400}{8400} \approx 3.43,$$
>>while $\text{Var}[\text{Bin}(20, 0.1)] = 20 \cdot 0.1 \cdot 0.9 = 1.8$. The Beta-Binomial variance is nearly twice as large, reflecting the factory-to-factory heterogeneity in defect rates. The overdispersion factor is $3.43 / 1.8 \approx 1.90$.

>[!example] Bayesian Prediction
>You are estimating the probability $\theta$ that a website visitor clicks a particular ad. Your prior is $\Theta \sim \text{Beta}(3, 7)$, reflecting a belief that the click-through rate is around $30\%$. You observe $n = 50$ visitors and $x = 8$ clicks.
>>[!example]- What is the posterior distribution of $\Theta$, and what is the predictive probability that the next visitor clicks?
>>By [[2.2c.9 Beta Distribution|Beta-Bernoulli conjugacy]], the posterior is
>>$$\Theta \mid X = 8 \sim \text{Beta}(3 + 8, \; 7 + 50 - 8) = \text{Beta}(11, 49).$$
>>The posterior mean is $\frac{11}{11 + 49} = \frac{11}{60} \approx 0.183$, pulled from the prior mean of $0.3$ toward the observed rate of $8/50 = 0.16$ by the weight of the data.
>>
>>The **posterior predictive probability** that the next visitor clicks is simply the posterior mean of $\Theta$:
>>$$\mathbb{P}(X_{51} = 1 \mid X = 8) = \mathbb{E}[\Theta \mid X = 8] = \frac{11}{60} \approx 0.183.$$
>>More generally, the predictive distribution for $Y$ new successes in $m$ future trials is $Y \mid X = 8 \sim \text{BetaBin}(m, 11, 49)$, which is the Beta-Binomial with the updated posterior parameters.
