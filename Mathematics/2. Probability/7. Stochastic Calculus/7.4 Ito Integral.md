>[!note] Itô Integral
>The **Itô integral** is the total trading gain on a randomly moving price when each holding is chosen before the price moves. It is defined as a limit of sums over shorter and shorter time steps. Split $[0, t]$ into $n$ steps $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = t$ of length $\Delta t = t/n$. Write $W_j = W_{t_j}$ for the [[7.1 Generalized Wiener Process|Wiener process]] at the grid times. For a regular function $f$, the Itô integral of $f$ with respect to $W$ is the limit
>$$\int_0^t f(W_s, s)\,dW_s = \lim_{n \to \infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n} f(W_{j-1}, t_{j-1})\,(W_j - W_{j-1}).$$
>In each step the integrand is evaluated at the left endpoint $t_{j-1}$, before the increment $W_j - W_{j-1}$ happens. The result is a random variable. Unlike in a Riemann-Stieltjes integral (an ordinary integral $\int f\,dg$ against a smooth function $g$), the point chosen inside each step changes the limit. With $W_0 = 0$,
>$$\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s = \frac{1}{2}W_t^2 - \frac{1}{2}t.$$
>The term $-t/2$ has no counterpart in ordinary calculus, where $\int_0^t w\,dw = w^2/2$. The sum with right endpoints $W_j$ converges to $W_t^2/2 + t/2$ instead. The sum with the average $(W_{j-1} + W_j)/2$ converges to $W_t^2/2$, and this limit is called the **Stratonovich integral**. The Itô integral gives meaning to the $dW_t$ term of an [[7.3 Ito Process|Itô process]]. In practice, [[7.5 Ito's Lemma|Itô's lemma]] computes most Itô integrals without sums. One example is $\int_0^t s\,dW_s = tW_t - \int_0^t W_s\,ds$.

# Intuition

A trader holds a position in an asset and resets it once a day, at the open. On day $j$ she holds $h_j$ units, and the price changes by $\Delta W_j$ during the day. Her gain that day is $h_j\,\Delta W_j$, and her total gain is the sum of these products. She wants this total for any trading rule, including rules that trade many times a day.

She sets each holding at the open, before she sees the day's move. So $h_j$ may depend on prices up to the start of day $j$, but not on the move it earns. Take a price that starts at $\$100$ and moves $\$1$ up or down each day. Her rule holds as many units as the price is above $\$100$, and a short position when it is below. Over four days the moves are $+1, +1, -1, +1$, so the price goes $100, 101, 102, 101, 102$. Her holdings are $0, 1, 2, 1$, her daily gains are $0, +1, -2, +1$, and her total is $\$0$.

A second trader applies the same rule at the close, after seeing the day's move. Her holdings are $1, 2, 1, 2$ and her total is $\$4$. Each day her holding differs from the first trader's by the day's own move. So each day she earns the square of that move, $\$1$, more. The gap is the number of days, whatever the order of the moves. A rule set halfway, at the average of the open and close prices, earns $\$2$. That is the ordinary-calculus answer $w^2/2$ with $w = 2$. For a random path, the point inside each day where the holding is set changes the total.

The figure shows the same timing for a Wiener path sampled at five times, with $t = 1$ and $n = 4$. The solid segments are the holdings set at the open, the left-endpoint values $W_{j-1}$. The dashed segments are the holdings set at the close, the right-endpoint values $W_j$. In each step the two differ by the step's move $W_j - W_{j-1}$. So the right-endpoint sum exceeds the left-endpoint sum by $\sum_j (W_j - W_{j-1})^2$.

With Wiener moves the squared moves are random, but each has mean $\Delta t$. [[7.2 Quadratic Variation]] shows that their sum over $[0, t]$ settles at $t$ as the steps shrink. So the gap between the two traders stays near $t$, however often they trade. For a smooth price path the squared moves add up to $0$, and the timing does not matter. This is why an ordinary integral never has to choose a point inside each step.

The Itô integral is the first trader's total gain in the limit of very frequent trading. In the callout, the integrand $f(W_{j-1}, t_{j-1})$ is the holding set at the open, and $W_j - W_{j-1}$ is the step's move. The left endpoint is the timing rule of the second paragraph. The $-t/2$ in $\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s$ is the first trader's shortfall against the rule set halfway, half of the gap $t$. The Stratonovich integral is the limit for that halfway rule.

# Properties

#### *(Why the Endpoint Matters)* The left and right sums differ by the sum of squared increments, which converges to $t$.

Subtracting the two sums for $f(W) = W$ gives
$$\sum_{j=1}^n W_j(W_j - W_{j-1}) - \sum_{j=1}^n W_{j-1}(W_j - W_{j-1}) = \sum_{j=1}^n (W_j - W_{j-1})^2.$$
The right side is the sum of squared increments of the path over $[0, t]$. [[7.2 Quadratic Variation]] shows that this sum converges to $t$ for a Wiener path and to $0$ for a smooth path. So the gap converges to $t$, and the right sum converges to $W_t^2/2 + t/2$. For a smooth integrator the gap goes to $0$, and for this reason the evaluation point does not matter in a Riemann-Stieltjes integral. The midpoint sum is the average of the left and right sums. It equals $\frac{1}{2}\sum_j (W_j^2 - W_{j-1}^2) = W_t^2/2$ for every $n$, because the sum telescopes.

#### *(Zero Mean and the Isometry)* An Itô integral has mean zero, and its variance is the time integral of the mean squared integrand.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules do not state the mean or the variance of an Itô integral, or that it is a martingale.

In each term $f(W_{j-1}, t_{j-1})(W_j - W_{j-1})$, the first factor depends only on the path up to $t_{j-1}$. The increment is independent of that path and has mean $0$. So each term has mean $0$, and
$$\mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^t f(W_s, s)\,dW_s\right] = 0, \qquad \mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_0^t f(W_s, s)\,dW_s\right)^2\right] = \int_0^t \mathbb{E}\big[f(W_s, s)^2\big]\,ds.$$
The second identity is the **Itô isometry**. In the square of the sum, products of terms from different steps have mean $0$. Each squared term has mean $\mathbb{E}[f^2]\,\Delta t$. For $f = W$ the closed form agrees. Its mean is $\mathbb{E}[W_t^2/2 - t/2] = 0$, and its variance is $\operatorname{Var}(W_t^2)/4 = t^2/2 = \int_0^t s\,ds$. A trader who cannot see future prices has zero expected gain when the price has no drift. The right-endpoint limit fails this test, since its mean is $t$. The same argument works from any later time $u < t$. Given the path up to $u$, each later term still has mean $0$, because its increment is independent of everything before it. So the best forecast of the integral up to $t$ is its value up to $u$. The Itô integral, viewed as a process in $t$, is therefore a [[4.5 Martingales|martingale]].

#### *(Deterministic Integrands)* When the integrand does not depend on $W$, the evaluation point does not matter, and the integral is normal.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The identity for $\int_0^t s\,dW_s$ is in the modules. The summation-by-parts proof, the normal distribution, and the variance $t^3/3$ are not.

Take $f(W_s, s) = s$. Rearranging the finite sum, a step called summation by parts, gives an identity that holds for every $n$:
$$\sum_{j=1}^n t_{j-1}(W_j - W_{j-1}) = t_nW_n - \sum_{j=1}^n W_j\,(t_j - t_{j-1}).$$
It uses $t_0 = 0$. The last sum is an ordinary Riemann sum for $\int_0^t W_s\,ds$, so in the limit
$$\int_0^t s\,dW_s = tW_t - \int_0^t W_s\,ds.$$
This is the integral form of $d(tW_t) = t\,dW_t + W_t\,dt$, which [[7.5 Ito's Lemma]] derives in one line. The right-endpoint sum differs from the left one by $\sum_j \Delta t\,(W_j - W_{j-1}) = \Delta t\,W_t$, which goes to $0$. Each term $t_{j-1}(W_j - W_{j-1})$ is normal with a fixed coefficient, and the terms are independent. So the integral is normal with mean $0$ and, by the isometry, variance $\int_0^t s^2\,ds = t^3/3$.

#### *(Differential Notation)* An equation in $dW_t$ is shorthand for an equation between integrals.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules work with the differential form and integrate it in examples. The general integral form below is not written out.

The Itô process $dx = a(x, t)\,dt + b(x, t)\,dW_t$ means
$$x_t = x_0 + \int_0^t a(x_s, s)\,ds + \int_0^t b(x_s, s)\,dW_s.$$
The first integral is an ordinary integral along the path. The second is an Itô integral, with $b$ evaluated at the start of each step. The simulation step $x_{k+1} = x_k + a\,\Delta t + b\,\Delta W$ in [[7.3 Ito Process]] adds one term of each sum. A step-by-step simulation therefore computes left-endpoint sums, which converge to the Itô integral.

# Derivation

We want the limit of the left-endpoint sum for $f(W) = W$, with $W_0 = 0$ and $W_n = W_t$. Expand each term:
$$\sum_{j=1}^n W_{j-1}(W_j - W_{j-1}) = \sum_{j=1}^n W_{j-1}W_j - \sum_{j=1}^n W_{j-1}^2.$$
The sums $\sum_j W_j^2$ and $\sum_j W_{j-1}^2$ share all terms except the last and the first. Since $W_0 = 0$ and $W_n = W_t$, they differ by $W_t^2$. So the second sum on the right can be written as half of both sums,
$$\sum_{j=1}^n W_{j-1}^2 = \frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^n W_{j-1}^2 + \sum_{j=1}^n W_j^2 - W_t^2\right).$$
Substitute this and collect terms. The products and squares combine into squared increments:
$$\sum_{j=1}^n W_{j-1}(W_j - W_{j-1}) = \frac{1}{2}W_t^2 - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^n (W_j - W_{j-1})^2.$$
Each increment is $\varepsilon_{j-1}\sqrt{\Delta t}$ with $\varepsilon_{j-1}$ standard normal. With $\Delta t = t/n$ the last sum is
$$\sum_{j=1}^n \varepsilon_{j-1}^2\,\Delta t = t\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\varepsilon_j^2.$$
The average of $n$ independent copies of $\varepsilon^2$ converges to $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2] = 1$ by the strong [[3.2 Laws of Large Numbers|law of large numbers]]. Each finer grid uses new increments, so this use of the law is a heuristic. The variance of the sum, $2t^2/n$, goes to $0$ ([[7.2 Quadratic Variation]]), which confirms the limit. So the sum of squares converges to $t$, and
$$\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s = \frac{1}{2}W_t^2 - \frac{1}{2}t.$$

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Left and Right Sums on Four Steps
>A Wiener path on $[0, 1]$ is sampled at $t_j = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1$. Its four increments are $0.6$, $-0.4$, $-0.5$, and $0.7$. So the sampled values are $W = 0, 0.6, 0.2, -0.3, 0.4$, the path in the figure.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the left-endpoint, right-endpoint, and midpoint sums for $\int_0^1 W_s\,dW_s$, and check the identity from the derivation.
>>The left sum is
>>$$0(0.6) + 0.6(-0.4) + 0.2(-0.5) + (-0.3)(0.7) = 0 - 0.24 - 0.10 - 0.21 = -0.55.$$
>>The sum of squared increments is $0.36 + 0.16 + 0.25 + 0.49 = 1.26$. The identity gives $W_1^2/2 - 1.26/2 = 0.08 - 0.63 = -0.55$, which matches. The right sum is
>>$$0.6(0.6) + 0.2(-0.4) + (-0.3)(-0.5) + 0.4(0.7) = 0.36 - 0.08 + 0.15 + 0.28 = 0.71.$$
>>It exceeds the left sum by $1.26$, the sum of squares. The midpoint sum is the average of the two, $(-0.55 + 0.71)/2 = 0.08 = W_1^2/2$. On a fine grid the sum of squares would be close to $t = 1$. For a path ending at $W_1 = 0.4$, the left sum would then be close to $0.08 - 0.5 = -0.42$.

>[!example] (Medium) A Momentum Strategy With No Drift
>The spread between two asset prices starts at $0$ and moves as a Wiener process, in dollars with time in years. A trader holds $W_s$ units of the spread at each time $s$. She is long when the spread is positive and short when it is negative. Her gain over two years is $G = \int_0^2 W_s\,dW_s$.
>
>>[!example]- Find her expected gain, the probability that she loses money, her largest possible loss, and the probability that she gains more than $\$1$.
>>The closed form gives $G = W_2^2/2 - 1$. Since $\mathbb{E}[W_2^2] = 2$, the expected gain is $2/2 - 1 = 0$. She loses money when $W_2^2 < 2$, that is when $|W_2| < \sqrt{2}$. Write $W_2 = \sqrt{2}\,Z$ for a standard normal $Z$. Then
>>$$\mathbb{P}[G < 0] = \mathbb{P}[|Z| < 1] = N(1) - N(-1) = 0.6827,$$
>>where $N$ is the standard normal CDF, written $\Phi$ in [[2.2c.3 Gaussian Distribution]]. The gain is smallest when $W_2 = 0$, so the largest loss is $\$1$. She gains more than $\$1$ when $W_2^2 > 4$, which has probability $\mathbb{P}[|Z| > \sqrt{2}] = 0.1573$. The loss term $-t/2$ comes from rebalancing. After each rise she buys more at the higher price, and after each fall she sells at the lower price.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The last part of the next example uses the normal distribution of $\int_0^t s\,dW_s$ from the Deterministic Integrands property.

>[!example] (Hard) A Position Built Over Time
>A trader builds a position steadily. At time $s$ she holds $s$ units of an asset whose price change follows a Wiener process, with time in years. Use the four increments of the Easy example, $0.6$, $-0.4$, $-0.5$, and $0.7$, on the grid $0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the left-endpoint sum for $\int_0^1 s\,dW_s$ and check it against the summation-by-parts identity. Find its gap to the right-endpoint sum. Then find $\mathbb{P}\left[\int_0^1 s\,dW_s > 0.5\right]$ in the limit.
>>The left sum is $0(0.6) + 0.25(-0.4) + 0.5(-0.5) + 0.75(0.7) = -0.10 - 0.25 + 0.525 = 0.175$. The identity gives
>>$$t_4W_4 - \sum_{j=1}^4 W_j\,\Delta t = 1(0.4) - 0.25\,(0.6 + 0.2 - 0.3 + 0.4) = 0.4 - 0.225 = 0.175,$$
>>the same number. The right sum is $0.25(0.6) + 0.5(-0.4) + 0.75(-0.5) + 1(0.7) = 0.275$. The gap is $0.1 = \Delta t\,W_1$, and it goes to $0$ as $\Delta t \to 0$. For $\int_0^1 W_s\,dW_s$ the gap stayed near $t = 1$.
>>
>>In the limit the integral is normal with mean $0$ and variance $\int_0^1 s^2\,ds = 1/3$. Its standard deviation is $0.5774$, so
>>$$\mathbb{P}\left[\int_0^1 s\,dW_s > 0.5\right] = 1 - N\left(\frac{0.5}{0.5774}\right) = 1 - N(0.8660) = 0.1932.$$
