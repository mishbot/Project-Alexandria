>[!note] Convection
>**Convection** is heat transfer by the bulk motion of a fluid. A parcel of liquid or gas warms at one place, moves, and gives up its heat somewhere else. Both energy and mass move, which is what separates convection from [[2.6.1 Thermal Conduction|conduction]], where only energy moves. In **natural convection** the motion is driven by [[1.7.3. Buoyancy|buoyancy]]: warm fluid expands, becomes less dense than its surroundings, and rises, while cooler fluid sinks to replace it. In **forced convection** a pump or a fan drives the flow, as in a car radiator, a hair dryer, or the blood circulating from the body's core to its skin.
>
>There is no formula for convection in this course. The rate depends on the fluid's viscosity, density, thermal expansion, and speed, on the shape and orientation of the surface, and on whether the flow is smooth or turbulent. Engineers use measured tables and empirical fits of the form $dQ/dt = h A\,\Delta T$, where the coefficient $h$ is looked up for each situation rather than derived. The qualitative facts are what matter here. Convection in air moves heat far faster than conduction in still air, which is why insulation works by trapping air and why wind makes a cold day feel colder. Convection is the main way heat leaves a warm surface into open air and the main way heat moves through the atmosphere and the oceans.

>Lecture only mentioned convection. It said that a moving fluid carries both energy and mass, that no analytical formula exists, and that engineers look rates up in tables. Everything else in this note follows Giancoli section 19.10.

# Intuition

Heat a pot of water from below. The water at the bottom warms, expands, and rises. Cooler water from the top sinks to take its place, warms in turn, and rises. A loop of circulation sets up and carries heat from the bottom to the top far faster than conduction through still water could. Air does the same thing over a radiator, above a candle, and across the whole planet, where warm air rising at the equator and sinking at the poles drives the winds. The diagram shows the loop.

# Properties

#### *(Fluids Only)* Convection needs a fluid that can move, so it does not occur inside a solid.

A solid transfers heat by conduction alone, and a vacuum by radiation alone. Only liquids and gases convect. Trapping a gas in a foam or between fibers stops the motion and leaves only the poor conduction of the still gas, which is why foam, down, and double glazing insulate. The air gap in a double-glazed window is kept narrow so that convection cannot get started across it.

#### *(Up Is Special)* Natural convection moves heat upward far more readily than downward.

Warm fluid rises, so a surface that is warmer than the fluid above it drives a vigorous loop, while a warm ceiling above cooler air sets up nothing. Heating a room from a floor radiator works, and heating it from the ceiling works badly. Ice forms on the top of a pond and the water below stays near $4\ ^\circ\text{C}$, because water is densest at that temperature and the coldest water floats rather than sinks.

#### *(Wind Chill)* Moving air removes heat faster than still air, without changing the air temperature.

Still air next to the skin warms up and forms an insulating layer. Wind strips the layer away and replaces it with cold air, so the skin loses heat faster and the air feels colder than a thermometer says. The same effect makes a fan cool a person without cooling the room, and makes a car radiator need a fan when the car is stopped. Forced convection in each case is doing what conduction through a thick air layer could not.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Why the Room Heats Evenly
>A radiator sits under a window on one wall of a room.
>
>```tikz
>\usepackage{tikz}
>\begin{document}
>\definecolor{hot}{HTML}{D9483B}
>\definecolor{cold}{HTML}{2F80ED}
>\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1cm,y=1cm,>=latex]
>\draw[very thick] (0,0) rectangle (7,3.2);
>\draw[cold,very thick] (0,1.6) -- (0,2.7);
>\node[left] at (0,2.15) {window};
>\draw[hot,very thick] (0.15,0.2) rectangle (0.55,1.2);
>\foreach \y in {0.35,0.55,...,1.1} \draw[hot] (0.15,\y) -- (0.55,\y);
>\node[right] at (0.6,0.5) {radiator};
>\node at (5.8,1.6) {far wall};
>\end{tikzpicture}
>\end{document}
>```
>
>>[!example]- Explain how the heat reaches the far side of the room.
>>Air near the radiator warms, expands, and rises to the ceiling. It flows across the ceiling, cools against the far wall and the ceiling, sinks, and returns along the floor to the radiator. This loop carries the warm air around the room in minutes. Conduction through still air, with $k = 0.023\ \text{W}/(\text{m}\cdot\text{K})$, would take days to move the same heat across a few meters. Placing the radiator under the window also warms the coldest air first, before it can sink and spread along the floor.

>[!example] (Medium) Ranking the Three Mechanisms
>A cup of hot coffee sits on a table in a still room.
>
>>[!example]- Say how each heat-transfer mechanism contributes to the cooling.
>>The coffee loses heat by [[2.6.1 Thermal Conduction|conduction]] through the cup wall and then into the table and the air. Once the heat reaches the outer surface, natural convection carries it away in rising air, and this is the largest loss from the sides. The surface of the coffee also loses heat by evaporation, which carries away the [[2.4.4 Latent Heat|latent heat]] of the escaping water, and this is the largest single loss from the top. The cup and the liquid also lose heat by [[2.6.3 Thermal Radiation|radiation]] to the cooler walls, a net of a few watts. A lid stops the evaporation and the convection from the top and slows the cooling more than any other single change.
