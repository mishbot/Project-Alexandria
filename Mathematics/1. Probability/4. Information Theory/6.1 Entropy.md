>[!note] Shannon Entropy
>**Entropy** quantifies the [[2.6a Expectation|average number]] of bits (uncertainty) required to convey the information in a random variable. For a discrete random variable $X$, its entropy $H(X)$ is defined as
>$$H(X)=\mathbb{E}\left[ \log_{2} \frac{1}{p_{X}(X)} \right],$$
>where $f(x)=\log_{2}\frac{1}{p_{X}(x)} \geq 0$ is a *concave* function, serving as a quantifier of the uncertainty associated with observing each $x$.

# Intuition
Given $f(x)=\log_{2}\frac{1}{p_{X}(x)}$, we can quickly see that $\lim_{ p_{X}(x) \to 1 }f(x)=0$. This implies that the larger the probability of some $x$ is, the smaller the uncertainty associated with it. The addition of the base 2 logarithm to the function captures the exact amount of bits of information yielded by the value $x$. While any base could be used, base 2 is preferred due to the binary nature of modern computers.

Now, take $X$ and find $f(x)$ for all values. multiplied by the probability of each of those values occurring. You get entropy.

# Properties
#### (*Concavity*) 

#### (*Joint Entropy*) $H(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}\left[ \dfrac{1}{p_{X,Y}(x,y)} \right]$.
#### (*Cond. Entropy*) $H(X \mid Y) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \dfrac{1}{p_{X \mid Y}(X \mid Y)} \right] = \large\sum_\limits{x}p_{X}(x)H(Y \mid X=x)$.
#### (*Chain Rule*) $H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y \mid X)$.
By definition, we know that $p(x,y)=p(x)p(y \mid x)$. As such,
$$\begin{align*} 
    H(X, Y) &= -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}}p(x,y)\log_2p(x,y) \\
    &= -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}}p(x,y)\log_2p(x)p(y \mid x) \\
    &= -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}}p(x, y)(\log_2p(x) +\log_2p(y \mid x)) \\
    &= -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}}p(x)\log_2p(x)-\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}}p(x)p(y \mid x)\log_2p(y \mid x) \\ 
    &= H(X) + H(Y \mid X),
\end{align*}$$
where marginal probability dictates that $-\sum\limits_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\sum\limits_{y \in \mathcal{Y}}p(x, y) = -\sum\limits_{x \in \mathcal{X}}p(x)$. Essentially, the chain rule states that the joint entropy of $X,Y$ is equivalent to the information that $X$ provides on its own, as well as the extra information provided by $Y$ given $X$.
#### (*Independence*) If $X, Y$ are independent, $H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y)$, and $H(X \mid Y) = H(X)$.
#### (Entropy Bounds) $H(X \mid Y) \leq H(X)$ and $H(X,Y) \leq H(X) + X(Y)$.
#### (*Uniform Dist.*) $H(X) \leq \log_{2}|\mathcal{X}|$. where $H(X)=\log_{2}|\mathcal{X}|$ if $X \sim U(a,b)$.


# Examples

>[!example] Text Compression
>Suppose you want to transmit a message with the letters Y and N, where Y appears 95% of the time. With naive encoding, each symbol takes 1 bit on average. However, entropy tells us that the theoretical limit for compression is
>$$H(X) = 0.95 \log_{2}\frac{1}{0.95} + 0.05 \log_{2}\frac{1}{0.05} \approx 0.286 \text{ bits per symbol.}$$

>Suppose $X_{0} \sim \operatorname{Ber}(1/2)$ and $p \neq 1/2$. Propose a scheme to represent $(X_0,\ldots,X_{n})$ in at most $n$ bits in expectation as $n \to \infty$, and state how many bits that would take.
>
>**SOLUTION:**
>We quickly find that the stationary distribution is $\pi^*=\begin{bmatrix}1/2 & 1/2\end{bmatrix}$, given the transition matrix $P$. We also find that $H(X_{0})=1$ as $X_{0} \sim \operatorname{Ber}(1/2)$. Assuming that the chain has ran for a long time given the stationary distribution above, we find that 
>$$\begin{align} H(X_{n+1} \mid X_{n})&=\sum_{x_{n} \in \mathcal{X}}p_{X_{n}}(x_{n})\sum_{x_{n+1} \in \mathcal{X}}p_{X_{n+1} \mid X_{n}}(x_{n+1} \mid x_{n})\log_{2}\left( \frac{1}{p_{X_{n+1} \mid X_{n}}(x_{n+1} \mid x_{n})} \right) \\ &= \frac{1}{2}\left[(1-p)\log_{2}\frac{1}{1-p}+p\log_{2}\frac{1}{p}\right] + \frac{1}{2}\left[(1-p)\log_{2}\frac{1}{1-p}+p\log_{2}\frac{1}{p}\right] \\ &= (1-p)\log_{2}\frac{1}{1-p}+p\log_{2}\frac{1}{p}.\end{align}$$
>
>This is a crucial result. Since $H(X_{n+1} \mid X_{n}) \leq 1$ (where the result equals $1$ if $p=1/2$), this means that if we know $X_{0}$, we will need $nH(X_{n+1} \mid X_{n})$ bits of information to determine the rest of the sequence. Recall that $H(X_{0})=1$. Putting this together, we find that we can recover, on average, the Markov Chain with $H(X_{1}, \ldots, X_{n})=nH(X_{n+1} \mid X_{n})+1 < n+1$ bits of information (strict inequality since $p \neq 1/2$.
