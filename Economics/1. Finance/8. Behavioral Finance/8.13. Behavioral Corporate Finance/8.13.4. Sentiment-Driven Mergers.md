>[!note] Sentiment-Driven Mergers
>**Sentiment-driven mergers** are the prediction (Shleifer-Vishny, $2003$) that misvaluation is a major driver of merger and acquisition activity. The argument fits naturally into Perspective 1 of [[8.13. Behavioral Corporate Finance|behavioral corporate finance]]: rational managers respond to mispricings in their own and target firms' shares, with M&A as a special form of [[8.13.1. Market Timing of Security Issuance|equity market timing]] in which the issued stock is exchanged for the target firm's assets rather than for cash.
>
>**The Shleifer-Vishny ($2003$) framework — two regimes.** When firms in an industry are heterogeneously misvalued, two distinct merger patterns emerge:
>1. **Cash-financed acquisitions of undervalued targets.** A bidder with cash or an unmispriced share price buys an undervalued target at a discount. The bidder captures the discount; the target's shareholders accept because the offer exceeds the depressed market price. These mergers create value for the bidder.
>2. **Stock-financed acquisitions by overvalued bidders of (less-overvalued) targets.** A highly overvalued bidder pays for the acquisition with overvalued stock. The bidder converts overpriced paper into real assets at a favorable exchange ratio. The bidder captures the difference between its own overvaluation and the target's lesser overvaluation. These mergers can also create value for the bidder *as long as* its shares remain less mispriced than they would be holding only its original assets.
>
>**The Dong-Hirshleifer-Richardson-Teoh ($2006$) test.** The authors test the Shleifer-Vishny predictions on $4{,}000$+ U.S. mergers from $1978$–$2000$ using cross-sectional misvaluation proxies (residual income vs. book value). The data confirm:
>- Bidders generally have higher market-to-book ratios than targets — bidders are typically the more overvalued party.
>- Higher *bidder* misvaluation is associated with greater use of *equity financing* (consistent with overvalued bidders using their stock as a transaction medium).
>- Higher *target* misvaluation is also associated with equity financing (the target prefers to receive overvalued stock rather than cash if it perceives stock as a good asset).
>
>**Reference points and bid premia — Baker-Pan-Wurgler ($2011$).** A separate behavioral pattern in mergers involves the *target's $52$-week-high stock price* as an investor reference point. Acquirers are more likely to offer bids that fall *just above* the target's $52$-week high than just below it, and bids that exceed the $52$-week high are associated with substantially higher deal-completion probabilities. The pattern reflects [[8.6.6. Anchoring|anchoring]] on the salient $52$-week-high reference point: target shareholders who own the stock at prices below the recent high feel they have already "lost" the value above that high and refuse to accept a bid that fails to recover it.
>
>**The merger-wave implication.** Shleifer-Vishny and the broader sentiment-merger literature explain why mergers come in *waves* — clusters of high deal activity coinciding with high stock-market valuations and low credit spreads. The waves are not driven by industrial-organization shocks (which would be more uniform across time) but by sentiment cycles that periodically inflate stock-market valuations and lower the perceived cost of stock-financed deals.

# Intuition

The classical theory of mergers — synergies, market power, managerial discipline — treats acquisitions as fundamental decisions about combining real assets. The behavioral perspective adds a financial-market layer: even when the underlying-asset combination is value-neutral or value-destroying in fundamental terms, the deal can still create value for the bidder's shareholders if it converts overvalued paper into less-overvalued paper. The deal is then a special form of equity issuance — the target's shares are the medium of exchange — and the same market-timing logic that explains equity issuance also explains acquisition activity.

The two-regime structure of Shleifer-Vishny is the cleanest version of this argument. Either the bidder is overvalued and uses stock to buy a less-overvalued target, or the bidder has cash (or an accurate stock price) and buys a deeply undervalued target. Both patterns appear in the data; the relative frequency depends on the aggregate sentiment environment. In high-sentiment periods, the stock-financed-overvalued-bidder pattern dominates; in low-sentiment periods, the cash-financed-undervalued-target pattern dominates. The merger waves of the late $1990$s and the leveraged-buyout boom of the late $1980$s fit the two regimes respectively.

The $52$-week-high reference-point pattern is conceptually independent. It does not require any party to be misvalued; it requires only that target shareholders anchor on a salient past price level. The result is a kink in the bid-acceptance probability around the $52$-week high — bids just above the high are much more likely to succeed than bids just below — that has no rational explanation but is exactly what an anchoring story predicts.

# Properties

#### *(Shleifer-Vishny 2003 Two Regimes)* Cash-for-undervalued and stock-for-overvalued.

The Shleifer-Vishny ($2003$) framework partitions sentiment-driven mergers into two distinct types. Type $1$: a bidder with cash or accurate-pricing buys a deeply undervalued target. The bidder captures the discount; this resembles a leveraged buyout or activist acquisition in low-sentiment periods. Type $2$: an overvalued bidder uses its stock to buy a less-overvalued target. The bidder captures the gap between its own overvaluation and the target's lesser overvaluation. The two types appear at different points in the sentiment cycle and produce qualitatively different merger waves.

#### *(Dong-Hirshleifer-Richardson-Teoh 2006)* Bidder misvaluation predicts equity-financed deals.

Dong, Hirshleifer, Richardson, and Teoh ($2006$) test the Shleifer-Vishny predictions on $4{,}000$+ U.S. mergers from $1978$ to $2000$. They construct firm-level misvaluation proxies based on residual-income models. The empirical relationships:
- Bidder market-to-book ratios are systematically higher than target market-to-book ratios.
- The probability of an equity-financed (rather than cash-financed) deal rises with bidder misvaluation, as the Shleifer-Vishny stock-financed-overvalued-bidder regime predicts.
- Equity financing is also more likely when the *target* is misvalued upward, consistent with target shareholders preferring to receive what they perceive as good-future-prospects equity rather than cash.

The pattern survives controls for size, industry, and conventional merger-motive variables, supporting the Shleifer-Vishny interpretation against rational alternatives.

#### *(Baker-Pan-Wurgler 2011)* $52$-week-high reference points shape bid premia.

Baker, Pan, and Wurgler ($2011$) document a sharp non-monotonicity in bid-completion probabilities around the target's $52$-week-high price. Bids priced just above the $52$-week high have much higher deal-success rates than bids priced just below. The discontinuity does not appear at any other price reference point (such as the $200$-day moving average or the all-time high). The interpretation is that the $52$-week high is a salient reference point for target shareholders, who have anchored on it as the floor for accepting an acquisition.

#### *(Anchoring Mechanism)* Target shareholders refuse to accept "losses" relative to the $52$-week high.

The behavioral microfoundation for the Baker-Pan-Wurgler pattern is the prospect-theory loss-aversion logic: target shareholders who held the stock at higher prices feel they have already taken a loss when prices fell from the $52$-week high; accepting a bid below that high "locks in" the loss. They prefer to refuse the bid and hold out for prices to recover, even when the bid exceeds the prevailing market price by a substantial premium. The mechanism is purely psychological and is not a feature of any rational benchmark.

#### *(Bid-Premium Implications)* Acquirers strategically pay over the $52$-week high to ensure success.

Acquirers know about the $52$-week-high anchor and price their bids accordingly. The empirical result is a clustering of bid prices just above the $52$-week high — a pattern visible as a spike in the histogram of bid premia. Acquirers are willing to pay an additional premium (sometimes substantial) to secure the deal, treating the anchor-driven success-probability bonus as worth the extra cost. The pattern increases observed bid premia in periods when stock prices have run up, with the $52$-week-high reference setting the floor.

#### *(Merger Wave Pattern)* Mergers cluster in high-valuation, low-credit-spread periods.

Both the Shleifer-Vishny mechanism and the Baker-Pan-Wurgler reference-point pattern predict that merger activity should cluster when valuations are high. The empirical record confirms this: the $1969$–$1973$ wave, the $1985$–$1989$ leveraged-buyout wave, the $1997$–$2000$ tech wave, the $2005$–$2007$ private-equity wave, and the $2014$–$2017$ wave all coincided with high aggregate stock valuations and low credit spreads. The waves do not align with industrial-organization shocks (which would be staggered across industries) but with broad-market sentiment cycles.

#### *(Limits)* Sentiment is one merger driver among several.

The behavioral perspective does not claim that all mergers are sentiment-driven; rather, that sentiment is one of several drivers and that ignoring it leaves an empirical residual that the rational benchmarks cannot explain. Synergy-driven, market-power-driven, and discipline-driven mergers continue to occur and account for a meaningful fraction of activity. The behavioral story is most useful for explaining the *timing* and *form* (cash vs. stock financing) of mergers and for understanding bid-premium patterns that the rational benchmarks miss.

# Examples

>[!example] AOL-Time Warner ($2000$)
>AOL acquired Time Warner in January $2000$ for approximately $\$165$ billion in AOL stock — at the time the largest merger in U.S. history. AOL's stock at the announcement was at one of the highest valuations in the history of any major company; its market-to-book ratio was vastly higher than Time Warner's. The Shleifer-Vishny stock-financed-overvalued-bidder regime predicts exactly this pattern: an extremely overvalued bidder uses its inflated stock to buy a less-overvalued target. The post-merger collapse — AOL's stock fell over $90\%$ from the announcement peak — confirmed that AOL had been profoundly overvalued; the deal converted overpriced AOL paper into Time Warner's real (less-overpriced) assets, capturing value for AOL's pre-merger shareholders relative to a counterfactual in which the deal did not happen.

>[!example]- A bid just above the $52$-week high
>A target firm's stock has traded between $\$30$ and $\$45$ over the past year, with a $52$-week high of $\$45$ reached six months ago. The current price is $\$32$. An acquirer considering a bid faces a choice: offer $\$42$ (representing a $31\%$ premium to current price but $7\%$ below the $52$-week high) or offer $\$46$ (representing a $44\%$ premium to current price and $2\%$ above the $52$-week high). Baker-Pan-Wurgler ($2011$) predict that the $\$46$ bid is much more likely to be accepted than the $\$42$ bid, because the $\$42$ bid asks target shareholders to accept a "loss" relative to the salient anchor of the $52$-week high. The acquirer who knows about the anchoring effect will choose to bid above the high even though it costs more; the success-probability bonus is worth the extra cost.

>[!example]- The 2005-2007 LBO wave
>Private-equity-led leveraged buyouts of public companies surged from $2005$ through $2007$, peaking with several deals over $\$25$ billion (e.g., Hilton Worldwide, TXU). The wave coincided with very high public-equity valuations and historically low credit spreads — exactly the conditions in which the Shleifer-Vishny framework predicts both stock-for-overvalued and cash-for-undervalued patterns. Many of the LBOs of this period were stock-financed by the acquirers (private-equity funds using their fund equity), which fits the Shleifer-Vishny logic for that regime. The post-$2008$ collapse and several large bankruptcies of LBO targets confirmed that valuations at the peak of the wave had been overstretched.
