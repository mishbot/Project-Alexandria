>[!note] Entropy
>**Entropy** $S$ is a state function that measures the disorder of a system, in joules per kelvin. Its true definition, $S = k \ln(\text{number of accessible microscopic states})$, is not used in this course. What is used is the rule for how it changes. When a system absorbs a small amount of heat $dQ$ at absolute temperature $T$ in a [[2.7.3 Reversible Process|reversible]] process, its entropy changes by
>$$dS = \frac{dQ}{T}, \qquad \Delta S = \int_1^2 \frac{dQ}{T} \quad (\text{reversible path}).$$
>Heat in raises the entropy and heat out lowers it, and the same heat changes the entropy more at low temperature than at high. Because $S$ is a state function, $\Delta S$ between two states is the same for every path, reversible or not. To find it for an irreversible process, integrate $dQ/T$ along any reversible path with the same endpoints. Three cases cover most problems. A reservoir at fixed $T$ that absorbs heat $Q$ changes by $\Delta S = Q/T$. A body of mass $m$ and specific heat $c$ warmed from $T_1$ to $T_2$ changes by $\Delta S = mc \ln(T_2/T_1)$. A mass $m$ that melts or boils at its transition temperature $T$ absorbs $mL$ at that fixed $T$. Its entropy changes by $\Delta S = mL/T$, negative when it freezes or condenses.
>
>For an ideal gas with $d$ accessible degrees of freedom the integral can be done in general, and the result is
>$$\Delta S = \tfrac{d}{2}\, Nk \ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} + Nk \ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} = \tfrac{d}{2}\, Nk \ln\left[ \frac{T_2}{T_1} \left( \frac{V_2}{V_1} \right)^{\gamma - 1} \right],$$
>using $2/d = \gamma - 1$. In terms of pressure and volume, with $T = PV/Nk$,
>$$\Delta S = \tfrac{d}{2}\, Nk \ln\frac{P_2}{P_1} + \left( \tfrac{d}{2} + 1 \right) Nk \ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} = \tfrac{d}{2}\, Nk \ln\left[ \frac{P_2}{P_1} \left( \frac{V_2}{V_1} \right)^{\gamma} \right].$$
>On an [[2.5.3d Adiabatic Process|adiabat]] $TV^{\gamma - 1}$ is constant, the bracket is $1$, and $\Delta S = 0$: a reversible adiabatic process is isentropic. On an [[2.5.3c Isothermal Process|isotherm]] $\Delta S = Nk \ln(V_2/V_1)$. At constant pressure $\Delta S = \left( \tfrac{d}{2} + 1 \right) Nk \ln(V_2/V_1)$. At constant volume $\Delta S = \tfrac{d}{2} Nk \ln(T_2/T_1) = \tfrac{d}{2} Nk \ln(P_2/P_1)$. The [[2.7.7 Entropy Statement of the Second Law|second law]] says the total entropy of a system and its surroundings never decreases.

# Intuition

Put heat into a gas and its molecules move faster, spread over more speeds, and reach more arrangements. The gas is more disordered, and $dQ$ in the numerator says so. The $T$ in the denominator is less obvious. It says that the same joule of heat disorders a cold system more than a hot one. A cold gas has its molecules in a narrow band of low speeds, and a joule spread among them opens up many new possibilities. A hot gas already has its molecules spread widely, and one more joule barely changes the picture. The factor $1/T$ is exactly what makes $\int dQ/T$ come out the same along every reversible path between two states, which is what a state function needs. The diagram shows why the ideal gas formula has two terms: heating raises the entropy, and so does expanding, and the two effects add.

The microscopic definition counts states, and a state lists the position and velocity of every molecule. Entropy measures how much of that list stays unknown once $P$, $V$, $T$, and $N$ are fixed. The two terms of the ideal gas formula match the two halves of a state. The $Nk \ln(V_2/V_1)$ term counts positions. A larger volume gives each molecule more places to be. The $\frac{d}{2} Nk \ln(T_2/T_1)$ term counts velocities. A hotter gas has a wider [[2.3.3 Maxwell Speed Distribution|Maxwell distribution]], so each molecule's speed is harder to predict. The factor $d/2$ counts the ways a molecule stores energy, as in [[2.3.4 Equipartition of Energy|equipartition]]. A diatomic molecule also stores energy in rotation, so its $T$ term is larger. The direction of travel never contributes to a change in entropy. In equilibrium every direction is equally likely at any temperature and volume. That part of the uncertainty is always at its maximum.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture defined a state as the position and velocity of every particle. It did not match the two terms of the formula to positions and velocities. The paragraph above is standard statistical-mechanics reasoning.

# Properties

#### *(A State Function)* The entropy change between two states is the same for every process that connects them.

The ideal gas formula proves it for one system: the result contains only the endpoint values of $T$ and $V$ and nothing about the path. The property holds in general and is the reason entropy is useful. Heat and work are process quantities that need a path to be defined. Entropy, like [[2.4.1 Internal Energy|internal energy]], is a property of the state. A gas returned to its starting point has $\Delta S = 0$, whatever it went through on the way. Around a cycle the changes on the legs add to zero,
$$\oint dS = \sum_{\text{legs}} \Delta S_i = 0.$$
In the [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot cycle]] the adiabats add nothing. The isotherms add $Nk \ln(V_C/V_B)$ and $Nk \ln(V_A/V_D)$, which cancel because $V_C/V_B = V_D/V_A$.

#### *(Irreversible Paths Use a Reversible Stand-In)* $\int dQ/T$ along an irreversible path does not give $\Delta S$, but any reversible path between the same states does.

Pouring hot water into cold water in an insulated container is irreversible. The mixture never separates again into a hot part and a cold part. Its entropy change can still be found, because entropy depends only on the initial and final states. Replace the mixing by reversible steps with the same endpoints. Cool the hot water slowly to the final temperature $T_f$, for example on ice. Warm the cold water slowly to $T_f$, for example over a burner. Then pour the two together. Water poured into water at the same temperature can be separated again, so this last step is reversible too. Each step can be run backward, so $dS = dQ/T$ holds on each. Their sum is the $\Delta S$ of the real mixing.

The diagram shows both routes. The top arrow is the real process. The lower route is the reversible stand-in, and it has the same endpoints, so it has the same $\Delta S$. The stand-in is reversible, yet the water's entropy still changes. Its heat comes from and goes to the surroundings, and the [[2.7.7 Entropy Statement of the Second Law|entropy statement]] counts their entropy too. The rule is to identify the initial and final states, forget the actual process, and integrate along a convenient reversible route.

#### *(Temperature Sets the Exchange Rate)* Heat leaving a hot body lowers its entropy by less than the same heat raises the entropy of a cold body.

When $Q$ flows from $T_H$ to $T_L$, the hot body loses $Q/T_H$ and the cold body gains $Q/T_L$, and since $T_L < T_H$ the gain is larger. The total entropy rises by $Q(1/T_L - 1/T_H) > 0$. This is the Clausius statement of the second law in entropy form. Heat flow from hot to cold raises total entropy, and the reverse would lower it.

#### *(Units and Size)* Entropy is measured in $\text{J/K}$, and everyday changes are of order $1$ to $10^{3}\ \text{J/K}$.

Melting a kilogram of ice adds $1.2\ \text{kJ/K}$. Doubling the volume of a mole of gas adds $R \ln 2 = 5.8\ \text{J/K}$. Passing $1\ \text{kJ}$ from $500\ \text{K}$ to $300\ \text{K}$ creates $1.3\ \text{J/K}$. In the microscopic definition $S = k \ln \Omega$, a change of $1\ \text{J/K}$ corresponds to the number of accessible states growing by a factor of $e^{7 \times 10^{22}}$. The macroscopic numbers are modest because $k$ is tiny, and the microscopic numbers are astronomical for the same reason.

# Derivation

We want the entropy change of an ideal gas between $(T_1, V_1)$ and $(T_2, V_2)$. Choose a reversible path and write the first law for a small step, $dQ = dE_{\text{int}} + P\,dV$. Then
$$dS = \frac{dQ}{T} = \frac{dE_{\text{int}}}{T} + \frac{P\,dV}{T}.$$
Multiplied by $T$, this reads
$$dE_{\text{int}} = T\,dS - P\,dV.$$
Every term is a state function or its change. So the relation holds for any small change between equilibrium states.
For an ideal gas $dE_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2} Nk\, dT$ and $P/T = Nk/V$ from the gas law, so
$$dS = \tfrac{d}{2}\, Nk\, \frac{dT}{T} + Nk\, \frac{dV}{V}.$$
Each term is a logarithmic derivative, and each depends on one variable only, so the integral separates,
$$\Delta S = \tfrac{d}{2}\, Nk \int_{T_1}^{T_2} \frac{dT}{T} + Nk \int_{V_1}^{V_2} \frac{dV}{V} = \tfrac{d}{2}\, Nk \ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} + Nk \ln\frac{V_2}{V_1},$$
the headline result. Factoring out $\tfrac{d}{2} Nk$ and using $2/d = \gamma - 1$ turns the sum of logarithms into the logarithm of $(T_2/T_1)(V_2/V_1)^{\gamma - 1}$. The path never had to be specified beyond being reversible, which shows that the result depends only on the endpoints. Replacing $T$ by $PV/Nk$ gives the pressure-volume form.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Melting Ice
>One kilogram of ice at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ melts into water at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the entropy change of the ice.
>>The temperature stays at $273\ \text{K}$ throughout, so $T$ comes out of the integral and the heat is the [[2.4.4 Latent Heat|latent heat]],
>>$$\Delta S = \frac{Q}{T} = \frac{mL_F}{T} = \frac{(1.00)(333 \times 10^{3})}{273} = 1.22 \times 10^{3}\ \text{J/K}.$$
>>The water is more disordered than the ice, and $\Delta S$ is positive. If the heat came from a room at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$, the room lost $333 \times 10^{3} / 293 = 1.14 \times 10^{3}\ \text{J/K}$, and the total rose by $83\ \text{J/K}$.

The answer for the gas does not depend on $T$: doubling the volume adds $R \ln 2$ per mole whatever the temperature.

>>[!example]- Find the final temperature, the entropy change of each part, and the total.
>>No heat leaves the container, so [[2.4.5 Calorimetry|calorimetry]] gives the final temperature,
>>$$m_H c\,(T_f - T_H) + m_C c\,(T_f - T_C) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad T_f = \frac{m_H T_H + m_C T_C}{m_H + m_C} = 40\ ^\circ\text{C} = 313\ \text{K}.$$
>>The mixing is irreversible, so use the reversible stand-in from the properties. First cool the hot water slowly from $T_H = 353\ \text{K}$ to $T_f$. Each small step has $dQ = m_H c\, d\tilde{T}$, where $\tilde{T}$ is the integration variable and $T_f$ is a fixed limit,
>>$$\Delta S_H = \int_{T_H}^{T_f} \frac{m_H c\, d\tilde{T}}{\tilde{T}} = m_H c \ln\frac{T_f}{T_H} = (1.00)(4186)\ln\frac{313}{353} = -503.4\ \text{J/K}.$$
>>Warming the cold water slowly from $T_C = 293\ \text{K}$ to $T_f$ gives
>>$$\Delta S_C = m_C c \ln\frac{T_f}{T_C} = (2.00)(4186)\ln\frac{313}{293} = +552.8\ \text{J/K}.$$
>>The hot water cooled, and its molecules became less disordered, so its entropy fell. The cold water warmed and gained more entropy than the hot water lost. Pouring the two together at one temperature adds nothing, so
>>$$\Delta S = \Delta S_H + \Delta S_C = +49.4\ \text{J/K}.$$
>>The container is isolated, so this is the whole entropy change, and it is positive because the mixing is irreversible. The same method works for two bodies with different specific heats, such as a hot iron block dropped into water,
>>$$T_f = \frac{m_1 c_1 T_1 + m_2 c_2 T_2}{m_1 c_1 + m_2 c_2}, \qquad \Delta S = m_1 c_1 \ln\frac{T_f}{T_1} + m_2 c_2 \ln\frac{T_f}{T_2}.$$
>>Mixing two different liquids, such as water and alcohol, adds a further entropy of mixing that this calculation leaves out.
