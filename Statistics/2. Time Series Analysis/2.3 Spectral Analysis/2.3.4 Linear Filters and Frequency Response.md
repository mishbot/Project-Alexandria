>[!note] Linear Filter and Frequency Response
>A **linear time-invariant filter** transforms an input series $\{x_t\}$ into an output series $\{y_t\}$ by a fixed weighted combination of past, present, and possibly future values:
>$$y_t \;=\; \sum_{j = -\infty}^{\infty} a_j\, x_{t-j} \;=\; (a * x)_t, \qquad \sum_j |a_j| < \infty.$$
>The coefficients $\{a_j\}$ are the **filter weights** (also called the *impulse response*: they are exactly what comes out when the input is a unit impulse $x_t = \mathbb{1}\{t = 0\}$). The filter is *causal* if $a_j = 0$ for all $j < 0$, meaning the output depends only on present and past inputs and can be implemented online; non-causal filters require the entire input record before producing output and so are restricted to offline analysis.
>
>The **frequency response** is the discrete-time Fourier transform of the weights:
>$$A(\omega) \;=\; \sum_{j = -\infty}^{\infty} a_j\, e^{-2\pi i j \omega}, \qquad \omega \in [-\tfrac12, \tfrac12].$$
>Its magnitude $|A(\omega)|$ is the **gain** at frequency $\omega$ (how much each frequency is amplified or attenuated by the filter), and its argument $\arg A(\omega)$ is the **phase shift** (how much that frequency component is delayed in time).
>
>The central spectral identity is that filtering a stationary input scales its spectrum by the squared gain:
>$$f_y(\omega) \;=\; |A(\omega)|^2\, f_x(\omega).$$
>This converts the time-domain operation $(a * x)_t$ into a frequency-domain product, which is far easier to work with both analytically and computationally.
>
>>[!warning] Convolution-Multiplication Duality
>>The whole reason linear filters are tractable is that **convolution in the time domain becomes multiplication in the frequency domain**. The DTFT of $(a * x)$ equals $A(\omega)\,\hat x(\omega)$, and applying expectations to a stationary process gives the spectral identity $f_y = |A|^2 f_x$. Designing a filter to amplify or suppress a specific frequency band is therefore done in the frequency domain (decide what $|A(\omega)|^2$ should look like) and inverse-transformed to recover the weights $a_j$ that implement it.

# Intuition

A linear filter is a recipe for re-weighting a time series: smoothing, sharpening, delaying, or differencing. Once the recipe is described in the frequency domain ("keep frequencies below $0.1$, kill frequencies above $0.4$"), its action on any input is just point-wise multiplication of spectra. Convolution is unwieldy because every output value depends on a window of input values; multiplication is trivial because each frequency is handled independently. This duality is what makes spectral analysis the natural language for any system whose response is linear and time-invariant: amplifiers, electrical circuits, audio processing, the cochlea, and most signal-processing pipelines.

The spectral identity $f_y(\omega) = |A(\omega)|^2 f_x(\omega)$ has the same shape as the variance rule for scalar random variables, $\mathrm{Var}(aX) = a^2 \mathrm{Var}(X)$. The filter's gain plays the role of $a$, and squaring is the price of dropping phase information when going from amplitudes to power. Once the spectrum is recognized as a frequency-by-frequency variance, the two are visibly the same identity.

# Properties

#### *(Convolution-Multiplication)* The DTFT of a convolution is the product of the DTFTs.

The identity $\widehat{a * x}(\omega) = A(\omega)\,\hat x(\omega)$, applied to expectations over a stationary process, yields the spectral identity $f_y = |A|^2 f_x$. It also explains why FFT-based convolution beats direct convolution for long filters: an $O(n^2)$ time-domain operation becomes an $O(n\log n)$ frequency-domain product plus two FFTs, with the crossover happening at filter lengths around $n \approx 64$ in typical libraries.

#### *(Cascade)* Two filters in series have combined frequency response equal to the product of the individual responses.

If filter $A$ is followed by filter $B$, the cascade has frequency response $B(\omega)A(\omega)$ and the output spectrum is $f_z(\omega) = |B(\omega)|^2|A(\omega)|^2 f_x(\omega)$. Cascading is how complex filters are built from simple primitives: pre-emphasis, equalization, decimation pipelines all use this composition rule.

#### *(Causality and Stability)* Stability requires the weights to be absolutely summable; causality requires no negative-index weights.

A filter with $\sum_j |a_j| < \infty$ is **stable** (bounded inputs produce bounded outputs); a filter with $a_j = 0$ for $j < 0$ is **causal**. Stability is required for the frequency response to even be defined as a convergent sum. Causality is required for online application. Many useful filters (zero-phase, ideal low-pass) are non-causal in their natural form and must be approximated causally for real-time use.

#### *(Differencing as a Filter)* The difference operator is a high-pass filter with zero gain at zero frequency.

The difference operator $\nabla x_t = x_t - x_{t-1}$ has weights $a_0 = 1, a_1 = -1$ and frequency response $A(\omega) = 1 - e^{-2\pi i\omega}$, giving
$$|A(\omega)|^2 \;=\; 4\sin^2(\pi\omega).$$
The gain is zero at $\omega = 0$ (kills constant trends) and reaches its maximum of $4$ at $\omega = 1/2$ (amplifies the highest frequencies). This is why differencing a signal with a linear trend produces a stationary residual, but also why over-differencing inflates high-frequency noise, a fact that matters for [[2.5.6 ARIMA(p,d,q) Process|ARIMA]] modeling.

#### *(Moving Average as Low-Pass)* The simple moving average smooths by averaging over a window.

For weights $a_j = 1/L$ for $j = 0, \ldots, L-1$, the frequency response works out to
$$|A(\omega)| \;=\; \frac{1}{L}\,\Bigl|\frac{\sin(\pi L\omega)}{\sin(\pi\omega)}\Bigr|,$$
peaking at $|A(0)| = 1$ and zero at $\omega = k/L$ for $k = 1, \ldots, L-1$. Increasing $L$ narrows the pass-band around $\omega = 0$ (more aggressive smoothing) but also slows the filter's response to changes in the underlying signal. The phase factor $e^{-i\pi(L-1)\omega}$ encodes a delay of $(L-1)/2$ samples, the cost of asymmetric averaging over only past observations.

#### *(Zero-Phase Filtering)* Applying a filter forward and then backward eliminates phase distortion.

Running a filter $A$ once forward and once in reverse (`filtfilt` in scipy) yields a zero-phase response with squared gain $|A(\omega)|^4$. This eliminates the phase distortion of asymmetric filters at the cost of doubling the gain shape and making the operation strictly non-causal. Standard practice for offline cleaning (EEG, audio post-production, climate data) where exact temporal alignment matters more than real-time operation.

# Examples

>[!example] First-difference of an AR(1) process
>For $X_t = 0.9 X_{t-1} + W_t$ with $W_t \sim \mathrm{WN}(0, 1)$, what is the spectrum of $Y_t = \nabla X_t$?
>>[!example]- Solution
>>The AR(1) spectrum is $f_X(\omega) = 1 / (1 - 1.8\cos 2\pi\omega + 0.81)$. The differencing filter has $|A(\omega)|^2 = 4\sin^2(\pi\omega)$. Multiplying these gives
>>$$f_Y(\omega) \;=\; \frac{4\sin^2(\pi\omega)}{1 - 1.8\cos 2\pi\omega + 0.81}.$$
>>At $\omega = 0$, $f_Y \to 0$, since differencing kills the constant component that the AR(1) builds up at low frequency. At $\omega = 1/2$, $f_Y = 4/(1 + 1.8 + 0.81) \approx 1.10$, larger than $f_X(1/2) = 1/3.61 \approx 0.28$, so high-frequency noise is amplified four-fold. The output spectrum is bowl-shaped: low frequencies suppressed, high frequencies enhanced.

>[!example] Filtering away a 60Hz line-noise component
>An EEG signal $x_t$ sampled at $f_s = 500$ Hz contains a strong $60$ Hz line-noise contamination. Sketch a notch filter that passes everything else and predict the output spectrum.
>>[!example]- Solution
>>In normalized units the offending frequency is $\omega_0 = 60/500 = 0.12$. A *notch filter* with frequency response approximately
>>$$|A(\omega)|^2 \;=\; \begin{cases} 0 & |\omega - \omega_0| < \delta \\ 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
>>for small bandwidth $\delta$ kills the spike. The output spectrum is $f_y(\omega) = |A(\omega)|^2 f_x(\omega)$, identical to $f_x$ except for a thin trough at $\pm 0.12$ that removes the line noise. The corresponding filter weights $a_j$ come from the inverse DFT of $A(\omega)$ and have many non-zero entries (sharp frequency responses require long filters), but the *design* lives entirely in the frequency domain.
