>[!note] Altruism and Charitable Donations
>The **altruism model** shows that a utility maximizer need not be self-interested. Two people consume one good with price $1$. Wendy has income $M_W$ and consumes $c_W$. Mark has income $M_M$ and consumes $c_M$. Each gets utility $u(c)$ from own consumption, with $u' > 0$ and $u'' < 0$. Mark maximizes $u(c_M)$, so he spends all he has, $c_M^* = M_M + D$. Wendy is **altruistic**: she maximizes $u(c_W) + \alpha\,u(c_M)$, where $\alpha > 0$ is her weight on Mark's utility. She chooses a **donation** $D$ to Mark and solves
>$$\max_{D}\ u(M_W - D) + \alpha\,u(M_M + D) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad D \ge 0 .$$
>- *Interior optimum:* $u'(M_W - D^*) = \alpha\,u'(M_M + D^*)$. The utility Wendy loses on the last dollar equals the utility she gains through Mark.
>- *Second-order condition:* $u''(M_W - D) + \alpha\,u''(M_M + D) < 0$ holds at every $D$ because $u'' < 0$. The objective is strictly concave in $D$, so the optimum is unique.
>- *Corner:* without the constraint $D \ge 0$, a richer Mark would get a negative donation, which means Wendy would take his income. For general $\alpha$ (not taught), $D^* = 0$ exactly when $u'(M_W) \ge \alpha\,u'(M_M)$.
>- *Equal weights* ($\alpha = 1$): Wendy equalizes incomes when she is the richer one,
>$$D^* = \begin{cases} (M_W - M_M)/2 & \text{if } M_W - M_M > 0, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$
>- *Log utility* ($u = \ln c$, not taught): $D^* = \max\{0,\ (\alpha M_W - M_M)/(1+\alpha)\}$.
>- *Comparative statics* at an interior optimum, with $H = u''(M_W - D^*) + \alpha\,u''(M_M + D^*) < 0$:
>$$\frac{\partial D^*}{\partial \alpha} = -\frac{u'(M_M + D^*)}{H} > 0, \qquad \frac{\partial D^*}{\partial M_W} = -\frac{-u''(M_W - D^*)}{H} > 0, \qquad \frac{\partial D^*}{\partial M_M} = -\frac{\alpha\,u''(M_M + D^*)}{H} < 0 .$$
>- Wendy gives more when she cares more or earns more, and less when Mark earns more. At a corner with $u'(M_W) > \alpha\,u'(M_M)$ all three derivatives are $0$.

# Intuition

Wendy splits her money between two uses, her own consumption and Mark's. Each dollar she gives costs her $u'(c_W)$ of her own utility. The same dollar raises Mark's utility by $u'(c_M)$, which Wendy values at $\alpha\,u'(c_M)$. Because $u$ is concave, each further dollar is less attractive than the last. Wendy gets poorer, so her marginal utility rises. Mark gets richer, so his marginal utility falls. She stops where the two meet. If the first dollar already costs her more than it brings, she gives nothing. She would like to take money from Mark, but a donation cannot be negative.

# Properties

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture solved the problem with $D \ge 0$ only for $\alpha = 1$ and derived the three comparative statics at an interior optimum. The properties below apply the same first-order condition to any $\alpha$. The consumption ranking, the general corner condition, crowd-out, the role of total income, and the log case were not covered.

#### *(Who Ends Up Richer)* At an interior optimum Wendy consumes more than Mark if $\alpha < 1$ and less if $\alpha > 1$.

The first-order condition reads $u'(c_W^*) = \alpha\,u'(c_M^*)$, and $u'$ strictly falls as consumption rises. If $\alpha < 1$, then $u'(c_W^*) < u'(c_M^*)$, so $c_W^* > c_M^*$. Wendy helps Mark but stops before he catches up. If $\alpha = 1$, the marginal utilities are equal, so consumption is equal and the gift equals half the income gap. This equal split needs no fairness motive. With a concave $u$, moving a dollar from the richer person to the poorer one raises $u(c_W) + u(c_M)$. If $\alpha > 1$, Wendy weights Mark's utility above her own and leaves him richer than herself.

#### *(When Wendy Gives)* Wendy gives a positive amount exactly when the first dollar brings more utility than it costs.

The derivative of Wendy's objective is $V'(D) = -u'(M_W - D) + \alpha\,u'(M_M + D)$. It strictly falls in $D$, since its own derivative $V''(D) = u''(M_W - D) + \alpha\,u''(M_M + D)$ is negative at every $D$. If $V'(0) > 0$, Wendy keeps giving until $V'(D^*) = 0$. If $V'(0) \le 0$, then $V'(D) < 0$ for every $D > 0$, so each dollar given lowers her utility and $D^* = 0$. In both cases $D^* = \max\{0, \tilde D\}$, where $\tilde D$ solves the first-order condition without the constraint. This is the case split of [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions]]. For $\alpha = 1$ the condition $V'(0) > 0$ becomes $M_W > M_M$, and for log utility it becomes $\alpha > M_M/M_W$. The figure plots $D^*$ for $\alpha = 1$ against both incomes. It is zero on the flat region where $M_M \ge M_W$. Past the fold at $M_W = M_M$ it rises with slope $1/2$ in $M_W$ and falls with slope $1/2$ in $M_M$.

#### *(Partial Crowd-Out)* An extra dollar for Mark lowers the gift by less than a dollar, so both people gain.

Both terms of $H$ are negative, so the ratio $\alpha\,u''(M_M + D^*)/H$ lies strictly between $0$ and $1$, and $-1 < \partial D^*/\partial M_M < 0$. Mark's consumption $M_M + D^*$ rises by $1 + \partial D^*/\partial M_M$, a share between $0$ and $1$ of the extra dollar. Wendy's consumption $M_W - D^*$ rises by the rest. The same argument gives $0 < \partial D^*/\partial M_W < 1$, so Wendy passes part of a raise to Mark and keeps part. With $\alpha = 1$ each share is $1/2$. The fall in private giving when the recipient gets other income is called **crowd-out**.

#### *(Only Total Income Matters)* At an interior optimum each person's consumption depends only on $M_W + M_M$.

The conditions $c_W + c_M = M_W + M_M$ and $u'(c_W) = \alpha\,u'(c_M)$ pin down both consumptions, and only the sum of incomes enters them. The formulas agree: $\partial D^*/\partial M_W - \partial D^*/\partial M_M = \big(u''(M_W - D^*) + \alpha\,u''(M_M + D^*)\big)/H = 1$. A government that taxes Wendy $T$ and gives $T$ to Mark lowers $D^*$ by exactly $T$ and leaves both consumptions unchanged. This holds only while $T \le D^*$, because the gift cannot fall below zero. Only the part of a transfer beyond Wendy's gift changes who consumes what.

#### *(Log Utility)* With $u = \ln c$, Wendy splits total income in fixed shares.

The first-order condition $1/(M_W - D) = \alpha/(M_M + D)$ gives $\tilde D = (\alpha M_W - M_M)/(1+\alpha)$. At an interior optimum $c_W^* = (M_W + M_M)/(1+\alpha)$ and $c_M^* = \alpha(M_W + M_M)/(1+\alpha)$. The objective $\ln c_W + \alpha\ln c_M$ is an increasing transformation of $c_W^{1/(1+\alpha)}c_M^{\alpha/(1+\alpha)}$, so these are the fixed shares of [[6.4a Cobb-Douglas]] demand with total income as the budget. The derivatives are constant: $\partial D^*/\partial M_W = \alpha/(1+\alpha)$, $\partial D^*/\partial M_M = -1/(1+\alpha)$, and $\partial D^*/\partial \alpha = (M_W + M_M)/(1+\alpha)^2$.

# Derivation

We want the first-order condition, the solution for $\alpha = 1$, and the three comparative statics. Mark's problem gives $c_M^* = M_M + D$. Wendy's budget $c_W \le M_W - D$ binds because $u' > 0$. Substituting both leaves one choice variable, $V(D) = u(M_W - D) + \alpha\,u(M_M + D)$. By the chain rule,
$$V'(D) = -u'(M_W - D) + \alpha\,u'(M_M + D), \qquad V''(D) = u''(M_W - D) + \alpha\,u''(M_M + D) < 0 .$$
By [[7.1 Unconstrained Optimality Conditions]], an interior optimum has $V'(D^*) = 0$, and $V'' < 0$ makes it a maximum. With $\alpha = 1$ the condition is $u'(M_W - D^*) = u'(M_M + D^*)$. Since $u'$ is strictly decreasing, equal values need equal arguments, $M_W - D^* = M_M + D^*$, so $D^* = (M_W - M_M)/2$. This is negative when $M_M > M_W$. In that case $V'(0) = -u'(M_W) + u'(M_M) < 0$, and the constrained optimum is $D^* = 0$.

For the comparative statics, let $F(D; \alpha, M_W, M_M) = V'(D)$. At an interior optimum $F = 0$, and the [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] gives $\partial D^*/\partial \theta = -F'_\theta/F'_D$ for each parameter $\theta$, as in [[6.3.1 Comparative Statics]]. The denominator is $F'_D = H < 0$. The numerators are
$$F'_\alpha = u'(M_M + D^*) > 0, \qquad F'_{M_W} = -u''(M_W - D^*) > 0, \qquad F'_{M_M} = \alpha\,u''(M_M + D^*) < 0 .$$
A positive numerator over a negative denominator, with the minus sign in front, gives a positive derivative. This yields the three signs in the callout. At a corner with $V'(0) < 0$, small parameter changes keep $V'(0) < 0$, so $D^*$ stays at $0$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Equalizing Incomes
>Wendy earns $80$ and her brother Mark earns $20$, in thousands of dollars per year. Wendy weights Mark's utility as much as her own, $\alpha = 1$. Both have the same concave $u$.
>
>>[!example]- How much does Wendy give? What changes if Mark takes a job that pays $90$?
>>With $\alpha = 1$ and $M_W > M_M$, the gift is $D^* = (80 - 20)/2 = 30$, and both consume $50$. The answer does not depend on the shape of $u$, only on $u'' < 0$.
>>
>>If Mark earns $90$, the formula without the constraint gives $(80 - 90)/2 = -5$. Wendy would like to take $5$ from Mark, which a donation cannot do. Her first dollar of giving costs $u'(80)$ and brings $u'(90) < u'(80)$, so $D^* = 0$. Each person consumes his or her own income.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture solved only the case $\alpha = 1$. The two examples below use $\alpha = 0.5$, log utility, and the crowd-out properties, which the lecture did not cover.

>[!example] (Medium) A Donor Who Cares Half as Much
>Wendy earns $M_W = 100$ and Mark earns $M_M = 20$. Both have $u = \ln c$, and Wendy's weight on Mark is $\alpha = 0.5$.
>
>>[!example]- Find $D^*$ and check the first-order condition. Compute $\partial D^*/\partial M_M$ and $\partial D^*/\partial \alpha$ from the general formulas. Below which $\alpha$ does Wendy give nothing?
>>The log formula gives $D^* = (0.5 \times 100 - 20)/1.5 = 20$, so $c_W^* = 80$ and $c_M^* = 40$. Check: $u'(80) = 1/80 = 0.0125$ and $\alpha\,u'(40) = 0.5/40 = 0.0125$. Wendy stays twice as rich as Mark, as expected for $\alpha < 1$.
>>
>>With $u''(c) = -1/c^2$, the denominator is $H = -1/6400 - 0.5/1600 = -0.00046875$. Then
>>$$\frac{\partial D^*}{\partial M_M} = -\frac{0.5 \times (-1/1600)}{-0.00046875} = -0.667, \qquad \frac{\partial D^*}{\partial \alpha} = -\frac{1/40}{-0.00046875} = 53.3 .$$
>>These match the log formulas $-1/(1+\alpha) = -2/3$ and $(M_W + M_M)/(1+\alpha)^2 = 120/2.25 = 53.3$. If Mark earns one more unit, Wendy gives $0.667$ less, and Mark ends up $0.333$ richer. Wendy gives a positive amount only if $\alpha > M_M/M_W = 0.2$.

>[!example] (Hard) A Government Transfer
>Keep the setting of the Medium example. The government taxes Wendy $T$ and gives $T$ to Mark.
>
>>[!example]- Find each person's consumption for $T = 15$ and for $T = 30$. Which transfers change consumption?
>>With $T = 15$, incomes become $85$ and $35$. The gift falls to $D^* = (0.5 \times 85 - 35)/1.5 = 5$, which is $15$ less than before. Consumption stays at $c_W^* = 80$ and $c_M^* = 40$. Total income is still $120$, and with an interior gift only the total matters. The transfer replaces $15$ of Wendy's giving one for one.
>>
>>With $T = 30$, incomes become $70$ and $50$. Now $\alpha M_W - M_M = 35 - 50 < 0$, so $D^* = 0$, and consumption is $c_W^* = 70$ and $c_M^* = 50$. The first $20$ of the transfer replaces Wendy's whole gift. Only the remaining $10$ moves consumption from Wendy to Mark. Every transfer $T \le 20$ leaves consumption at $(80, 40)$. For $T > 20$, Mark consumes $20 + T$.
