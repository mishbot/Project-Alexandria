>[!note] Binomial Distribution
>A discrete random variable $X \geq 0$ follows the **Binomial distribution** $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$ if it represents the number of **successes** in (i.e., the sum of) $n$ independent [[2.2b.2 Bernoulli Distribution|Bernoulli trials]], where each trial has a probability $p$ of success. Formally, the Binomial distribution of a random variable is given by the PMF,
>$$p_{X}(x)=\binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{n-x},$$
>where $\binom{n}{x}=\frac{n!}{x!(n-x)!}$ is the binomial coefficient, $p^{k}$ is the probability of exactly $k$ successes, and $(1-p)^{n-x}$ is the probability of the remaining trials being failures.


# Properties
#### (*Mean*) $\mathbb{E}[X \sim \text{Bin}(n,p)]=np.$
#### (*Variance*) $\text{Var}[X \sim \text{Bin}(n,p)]=np(1-p)$.
$$\begin{align}
\operatorname{Var}(X) &= \mathbb{E}(X^2) - \mathbb{E}(X)^2 \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{E}(I_{i}) + \sum_{i \ne j}^{n}\mathbb{E}(I_{i}I_{j}) - n^2p^2 \\
&= np + n(n-1)p^2 - n^2p^2 \\
&= np + n^2p^2-np^2-n^2p^2 \\
&= np-np^2 \\
&= np(1-p).
\end{align}$$
Since there are exactly $n(n-1)$ ordered distinct pairs of $i, j$ and $I_{i} \sim_{\text{iid}} \text{Ber}(p)$, so $\mathbb{E}(I_{i}I_{j})=\mathbb{E}(I_{i})\mathbb{E}(I_{j})$ for $i \neq j$.

#### (*MGF*) $\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]=(1-p+pe^t)^n$.
#### (Sum) Let $X=\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}$, where $X_{i} \sim_{\text{iid}} \text{Bin}(n_{i}, p)$. Then, $X \sim \text{Bin}\left( \sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}n_{i}, \; p \right)$.

# Examples

>Consider a system with $n$ inputs and $n$ outputs. At each input, a packet appears independently with probability $p$. If a packet appears, it is destined for one of the $n$ outputs uniformly randomly, independently of the other packets.
>
>

There are $n$ independent Bernoulli trials, and each trial has the same probability of success $\frac{p}{n}$.
