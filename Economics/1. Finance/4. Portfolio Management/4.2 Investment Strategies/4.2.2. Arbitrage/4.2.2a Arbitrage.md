>[!note] Arbitrage
>An **arbitrage** is a trading strategy that costs zero or less today, can never lose money, and has a positive payoff in at least one state. Let $V_0$ be the cost of the strategy today and $V_T$ its value at a later date $T$. The strategy is an arbitrage if
>$$V_0 \le 0, \qquad V_T \ge 0 \text{ in every state}, \qquad V_0 < 0 \text{ or } V_T > 0 \text{ in some state}.$$
>It creates a positive payoff from a zero initial position, or cash today with no later obligation. It is a risk-free profit that needs no capital. A market with no such strategy is **arbitrage-free**.
>
>The **Law of One Price (LOP)** states that two assets or portfolios with the same payoff in every state must have the same price today. It applies across markets and across forms: the same asset in two places, or the same payoff built in two ways. A violation of the LOP gives an arbitrage. Buy the cheap side, sell the expensive side, and keep the difference. The same rule prices assets by **replication**. If a portfolio of traded assets reproduces a payoff in every state, the payoff must cost what the portfolio costs. [[2.2.2b Forward Price]] and [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity]] both follow from this argument.
>
>Exact no-arbitrage prices assume a frictionless market. Traders can borrow and lend any amount at the risk-free rate and take any long or short position. There are no transaction costs, commissions, bid-ask spreads, market impact, or taxes, and every party honors its contracts (no counterparty risk). Real frictions widen each exact price into a band, and arbitrage exists only outside the band. A strict arbitrage built on a LOP violation is also called Type 1 arbitrage. A **convergence trade**, also called Type 2 arbitrage, profits only if the prices of two related assets come together over time. It can lose money first, so it is not an arbitrage in the strict sense.

# Intuition

Arbitrage arguments price an asset without any view on where prices will go. They assume only that traders take free money when they see it. An arbitrage needs no capital and carries no risk, so a trader can scale it up without limit. Arbitrageurs buy the cheap asset and sell the expensive one in large size. Their buying pushes the cheap price up and their selling pushes the expensive price down, until the gap closes. Arbitrageurs keep prices efficient in this sense, and in equilibrium every no-arbitrage relation holds.

The definition is strict. A trade that makes money on average, or almost always, is not an arbitrage. One state with a loss, however unlikely, disqualifies it. This strictness is the reason a no-arbitrage price holds for every investor, whatever their beliefs about the future or their attitude to risk.

# Properties

#### *(LOP Versus No Arbitrage)* A violation of the Law of One Price always gives an arbitrage, but an arbitrage can exist with no violation.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules use the Law of One Price in their replication arguments. They do not compare it with the no-arbitrage condition.

Suppose portfolios $A$ and $B$ pay the same in every state and $P_A > P_B$. Selling $A$ and buying $B$ brings in $P_A - P_B > 0$ today, and the payoffs cancel at $T$. The converse fails. The LOP says that assets that pay the same must cost the same. It says nothing about assets that cost the same and pay different amounts. If $A$ pays at least as much as $B$ in every state and more in some state, no-arbitrage requires $P_A > P_B$. Two such assets at the same price give an arbitrage with no LOP violation, as in the (Easy) example. No-arbitrage is therefore the stronger condition, and every arbitrage-free market satisfies the LOP.

#### *(Replication)* A payoff that can be built from traded assets costs exactly what it costs to build.

A [[2.1.1a Bonds|coupon bond]] is a bundle of zero-coupon bonds. Let $P(0, t_i)$ be the price today of a zero-coupon bond that pays $\$1$ at time $t_i$. Let the coupon bond pay $\text{CF}_i$ at $t_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$. Holding $\text{CF}_i$ units of each zero-coupon bond reproduces every cash flow, so the no-arbitrage bond price is
$$B = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \text{CF}_i\,P(0, t_i).$$
Each $P(0, t_i)$ equals $e^{-r_i t_i}$, where $r_i$ is the continuously compounded zero rate for maturity $t_i$ (the yield on a zero-coupon bond that matures at $t_i$). If the bond trades above $B$, sell the bond and buy the zero-coupon bonds, which together form a **synthetic bond**. If it trades below $B$, do the reverse. The cash flows cancel on every date, and the price gap is the profit today. Forwards and swaps are priced by the same steps: build the payoff, add up the cost, and trade against any other price. The (Medium) example works one case.

#### *(Frictions Create Bounds)* With trading costs, each no-arbitrage price becomes a range, and arbitrage exists only outside it.

Each direction of an arbitrage trade has its own costs. Take a forward on a stock with no dividends. Let the stock trade at bid $S_{\text{bid}}$ and ask $S_{\text{ask}}$. Each stock trade pays a commission $h_C$, a fraction of the trade value. Traders borrow at $r_b$ and lend at $r_l$, usually with $r_l < r_b$, and a stock lender charges a borrow fee $b$ per year. A forward quoted at bid $F_{\text{bid}}$ and ask $F_{\text{ask}}$ allows no arbitrage when
$$F_{\text{bid}} \le S_{\text{ask}}(1 + h_C)\,e^{r_b T}, \qquad F_{\text{ask}} \ge S_{\text{bid}}(1 - h_C)\,e^{(r_l - b)T}.$$
The first right side is the cost of buying the stock at the ask, paying the commission, and carrying it to $T$ with money borrowed at $r_b$. A short forward sold above it would lock in a profit. The second right side is what a short sale at the bid yields at $T$, after the commission, interest at $r_l$, and the borrow fee. A long forward bought below it would lock in a profit. With no frictions both bounds equal $S_0e^{rT}$. The band widens with the bid-ask spread, the commission, the gap $r_b - r_l$, and the fee $b$. If the stock cannot be borrowed, the short-sale trade is impossible, and only an investor who already owns the stock can sell it to enforce the lower bound. Taxes widen the band further. The (Hard) example applies these bounds.

#### *(Convergence Trades)* A convergence trade bets that two related prices will meet, and it can lose money before they do.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules do not cover convergence trades, noise-trader risk, or the Type 1 and Type 2 labels. This material comes from behavioral finance.

A convergence trade takes a long position in the relatively cheap asset and a short position in the relatively expensive one. The two assets may even be claims on the same cash flows, as with the two share lines of a dual-listed company. No fixed date forces their prices to meet, so the profit arrives only if and when the gap closes. The trade removes **fundamental risk**, the risk from news about the shared cash flows, because the long and the short react to that news in the same way. It keeps **noise-trader risk**, the risk that uninformed or irrational traders push the gap wider first. A wider gap brings margin calls and recalls of borrowed shares, and can force the trader to close at a loss. Funding limits, shorting costs, and illiquidity add to this risk. Long-Term Capital Management ran large convergence trades and collapsed in 1998, after the Russian default widened the spreads it had bet on. These limits to arbitrage explain why some mispricings last for years ([[8.1. Principles of Behavioral Finance]]). A Type 1 arbitrage has none of this interim risk, because its two positions pay the same in every state on the same date.

#### *(State Prices)* Strictly positive state prices rule out arbitrage.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules do not cover state prices or their link to arbitrage.

A state price $q_s$ is the price today of a claim that pays $\$1$ if state $s$ occurs and nothing otherwise ([[4.2.2b Arrow-Debreu Securities]]). Suppose every asset's price equals $\sum_s q_s X_s$, where $X_s$ is its payoff in state $s$, and every $q_s > 0$. Then any payoff that is never negative and is positive in some state has a positive price, so no strategy can obtain it for zero or less. With finitely many states the converse also holds: an arbitrage-free market always has strictly positive state prices that fit every asset price. In the (Easy) example, solving for the two state prices that fit both stock prices gives a negative price for the up state. The negative state price signals the arbitrage.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Two Stocks at the Same Price
>Two stocks, $X$ and $Y$, both trade at $\$40$ today and pay no dividends. Tomorrow one of two states occurs. In the down state $X$ is worth $\$34$ and $Y$ is worth $\$31$. In the up state $X$ is worth $\$52$ and $Y$ is worth $\$47$.
>
>>[!example]- Find an arbitrage, and say whether the prices break the Law of One Price.
>>$X$ pays more than $Y$ in both states, yet the two cost the same. Buy one share of $X$ and sell one share of $Y$ short. The cash flow today is $-40 + 40 = 0$. Tomorrow the position is worth $34 - 31 = \$3$ in the down state and $52 - 47 = \$5$ in the up state. It costs nothing and never loses, so it is an arbitrage. With $1{,}000$ pairs the trader makes $\$3{,}000$ or $\$5{,}000$ for no outlay. The probabilities of the two states play no role.
>>
>>The prices do not break the LOP, because $X$ and $Y$ have different payoffs. The LOP only says that assets that pay the same must cost the same. It does not say that assets that cost the same must pay the same. No-arbitrage makes the stronger demand that $P_X > P_Y$, since $X$ pays more in every state.

>[!example] (Medium) A Synthetic Coupon Bond
>Zero-coupon bonds with face value $\$100$ trade at $\$96.10$ for one year, $\$92.04$ for two years, and $\$87.90$ for three years. A three-year bond with face value $\$1{,}000$ pays an annual coupon of $5\%$ and trades at $\$1{,}021.00$.
>
>>[!example]- Build a synthetic bond from the zero-coupon bonds, find its price, and describe the arbitrage.
>>The coupon bond pays $\$50$ at years $1$ and $2$, and $\$1{,}050$ at year $3$. Each zero-coupon bond pays $\$100$, so the synthetic bond holds $0.5$ one-year bonds, $0.5$ two-year bonds, and $10.5$ three-year bonds. Its cost is
>>$$0.5 \times 96.10 + 0.5 \times 92.04 + 10.5 \times 87.90 = 48.05 + 46.02 + 922.95 = \$1{,}017.02.$$
>>The three terms are the costs of the year-1, year-2, and year-3 cash flows. The bond trades $\$3.98$ above its synthetic copy.
>>
>>Sell the coupon bond for $\$1{,}021.00$ and buy the synthetic bond for $\$1{,}017.02$. The trader keeps $\$3.98$ today. On each payment date the zero-coupon bonds pay exactly what the short coupon bond owes, so nothing is left to pay. For $1{,}000$ bonds the profit is $\$3{,}980$.

>[!example] (Hard) A Forward Inside the Band
>A stock pays no dividends. It trades at bid $\$99.90$ and ask $\$100.10$. Each stock trade pays a commission of $0.25\%$ of its value. A trader borrows at $6\%$ and lends at $4\%$ per year, both with continuous compounding. Borrowing the stock costs a fee of $0.5\%$ per year. A dealer quotes a six-month forward at bid $\$103.00$ and ask $\$103.30$.
>
>>[!example]- Show that the forward looks too expensive in a frictionless market, then check whether any trade makes a sure profit once the frictions are included.
>>With the mid stock price of $\$100$ and a single rate of $5\%$, the frictionless forward price is $100e^{0.05 \times 0.5} = \$102.53$. The dealer's bid is $\$0.47$ above it. In a frictionless market, selling the forward and buying the stock with borrowed money would lock in $\$0.47$ per share.
>>
>>Now include the frictions. Buying one share costs $100.10 \times 1.0025 = \$100.35$ today. The trader borrows this at $6\%$ for six months and owes
>>$$S_{\text{ask}}(1 + h_C)e^{r_bT} = 100.35\,e^{0.03} = \$103.41$$
>>at expiry. Here $h_C = 0.0025$, $r_b = 0.06$, and $T = 0.5$. The forward bid pays only $\$103.00$ for the share, so this trade loses $\$0.41$.
>>
>>The reverse trade shorts the stock at the bid and receives $99.90 \times 0.9975 = \$99.65$. The proceeds earn $4\%$ less the $0.5\%$ borrow fee, and grow to
>>$$S_{\text{bid}}(1 - h_C)e^{(r_l - b)T} = 99.65\,e^{0.0175} = \$101.41.$$
>>Buying the share back through the forward at the ask costs $\$103.30$, so this trade loses $\$1.89$.
>>
>>The band is $\$101.41$ to $\$103.41$. The bid $\$103.00$ is below the upper bound and the ask $\$103.30$ is above the lower bound, so no trade makes a sure profit. The upper bound sits $\$0.88$ above the frictionless price. Paying the ask adds $\$0.10$, the commission adds $\$0.26$, and the higher borrowing rate adds $\$0.52$. A cash-and-carry arbitrage would need a forward bid above $\$103.41$.
