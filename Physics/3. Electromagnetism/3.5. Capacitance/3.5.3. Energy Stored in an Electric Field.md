>[!note] Energy Stored in an Electric Field
>Charging a capacitor takes work. Each new bit of charge must cross a potential difference that grows as the charge builds up. The work is stored as electric potential energy. A capacitor $C$ with charge $Q$ and potential difference $V$ stores
>$$U = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{1}{2}QV.$$
>The three forms are equal, since $Q = CV$. The best form is the one whose other variable stays fixed:
>- An **isolated** capacitor, with its supply disconnected, keeps its charge. Use $U = Q^2/(2C)$, in which only $C$ can change.
>- A capacitor **connected to a supply** keeps its potential difference. Use $U = \tfrac12 CV^2$, in which only $C$ can change.
>
>The energy is stored in the field between the plates. The **energy density**, the energy per unit volume, is
>$$u = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2,$$
>with $E$ the size of the field at that point. For a parallel-plate capacitor, $U = u\,Ad$, the energy density times the volume between the plates.

# Intuition

The first bit of charge moves while both plates are neutral, so it takes no work. The last bit crosses the full potential difference $V$. In between, the potential difference rises in proportion to the charge already moved. On average, each bit crosses $V/2$. That average is the source of the factor $\tfrac12$ in $U = \tfrac12 QV$.

The energy can be counted in two ways. One count adds up the work done to separate the charges. The other assigns an energy to each small volume of field. Both counts give the same total. The field picture explains why a stronger field between the same plates holds more energy.

A capacitor is charged one small bit at a time. Each green strip is the work for one bit, $v\,dq$. The strips fill the triangle under $v = q/C$, whose area is $\tfrac12 QV$.

# Derivation

Charge the capacitor in small steps. Suppose the plates already carry $+q$ and $-q$, so their potential difference is $v = q/C$. Move a small charge $dq$ from the negative plate to the positive plate. Its potential energy rises by $dq$ times the potential difference, as in [[3.4.2. Electric Potential]]. The work needed is
$$dW = v\,dq = \frac{q}{C}\,dq.$$
In a circuit the charge travels through the wires and the supply. The electric force is conservative, so the work depends only on the starting and ending plates. Any path gives the same answer, including a straight line across the gap. For parallel plates, that straight path gives $dW = (q/\varepsilon_0 A)\,d\,dq$, the force on $dq$ times the gap $d$.

Add the steps from $q = 0$ to $q = Q$:
$$U = \int_0^Q \frac{q}{C}\,dq = \frac{Q^2}{2C}.$$
Substituting $Q = CV$ gives $\tfrac12 CV^2$. Replacing one factor of $Q$ by $CV$ gives $\tfrac12 QV$.

For the energy density, use a parallel-plate capacitor with $C = \varepsilon_0 A/d$ and $V = Ed$:
$$U = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}\,(Ed)^2 = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2\,(Ad).$$
The product $Ad$ is the volume between the plates. The field is uniform there, so dividing by the volume gives $u = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ at every point.

# Properties

#### *(Opposite Trends)* Raising $C$ lowers the energy of an isolated capacitor and raises the energy of one connected to a supply.

At fixed $Q$, $U = Q^2/(2C)$ falls as $C$ rises. At fixed $V$, $U = \tfrac12 CV^2$ rises as $C$ rises. Both statements are correct, because a different quantity is held fixed in each. Pulling the plates of an isolated capacitor twice as far apart halves $C$ and doubles $U$. The hand that pulls supplies the extra energy. The same move with a supply connected halves $U$. Using $\tfrac12 CV^2$ for an isolated capacitor, with $V$ treated as constant, gives the wrong trend. [[3.5.4b Force on a Dielectric]] depends on the same choice.

#### *(Energy Density in Any Field)* The formula $u = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ gives the energy per unit volume of any electric field in vacuum.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture derived $u$ only for the parallel-plate capacitor. Giancoli section 24.4 states that it holds for any field in vacuum.

The formula contains no plate area and no gap. It depends only on the field at a point. Integrating $u$ over all space gives the total energy of any spread-out charge distribution. A field of $2.0 \times 10^{6}\ \text{V/m}$ holds $u = \tfrac12(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(2.0 \times 10^{6})^2 = 17.7\ \text{J/m}^3$. A liter of such a field holds less than $0.02\ \text{J}$. Air-filled capacitors therefore store little energy for their size.

#### *(Fast Release)* A capacitor can give up its whole energy in a very short time, which produces a large power.

The separated charges sit on the plates. A low-resistance path between the plates lets them recombine almost at once. In a classroom demonstration, a large capacitor was charged to about $1900\ \text{V}$. A metal sphere was then touched across its terminals. The energy came out at once as a loud, bright bluish-white flash. The flash was a spark through the air, described in [[3.5.4a Dielectric Breakdown]].

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Camera Flash
>A flash unit charges a $120\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor to $330\ \text{V}$. It then empties the capacitor through the lamp in $1.0\ \text{ms}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the stored energy and the average power during the flash.
>>The stored energy is
>>$$U = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{1}{2}(120 \times 10^{-6})(330)^2 = 6.53\ \text{J}.$$
>>The average power is the energy divided by the time:
>>$$P = \frac{U}{t} = \frac{6.53\ \text{J}}{1.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{s}} = 6.5 \times 10^{3}\ \text{W}.$$

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not treat the energy lost when charge moves between capacitors. The next example combines the formulas of Giancoli section 24.4 with conservation of charge.

>[!example] (Medium) Sharing Charge Loses Energy
>A $3.0\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor is charged to $20\ \text{V}$ and removed from its supply. It is then wired in parallel with an uncharged $6.0\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor.
>
>>[!example]- Find the final potential difference and the energy before and after. Where did the difference go?
>>The charge is $Q = (3.0\ \mu\text{F})(20\ \text{V}) = 60\ \mu\text{C}$, and it stays $60\ \mu\text{C}$ on the joined plates. The initial energy is
>>$$U_i = \frac{1}{2}C_1V^2 = \frac{1}{2}(3.0 \times 10^{-6})(20)^2 = 6.00 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J}.$$
>>The two capacitors in parallel act as $9.0\ \mu\text{F}$. The final potential difference is $V_f = 60\ \mu\text{C}/9.0\ \mu\text{F} = 6.67\ \text{V}$. Only $Q$ is known for certain here, so the charge form is the natural one:
>>$$U_f = \frac{Q^2}{2C_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{(60 \times 10^{-6})^2}{2(9.0 \times 10^{-6})} = 2.00 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J}.$$
>>Two thirds of the energy, $4.00 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J}$, is gone. It leaves as heat in the wires and as light and sound from the small spark when they touch. The fraction lost is $C_2/(C_1 + C_2)$, whatever the wires are made of.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The last step of the next example uses the single-sheet field from [[3.3.2c Planar Symmetry]]. Lecture did not cover it, and Giancoli section 22.3 derives it.

>[!example] (Hard) Pulling the Plates Apart
>An isolated parallel-plate capacitor has plates of area $0.040\ \text{m}^2$ and charges $\pm 0.20\ \mu\text{C}$. A hand pulls the plates slowly from $1.0\ \text{mm}$ apart to $3.0\ \text{mm}$ apart.
>
>>[!example]- Find the energy before and after, the work done by the hand, and the force between the plates.
>>The charge is fixed, so use $U = Q^2/(2C)$ with $C = \varepsilon_0 A/x$ at gap $x$:
>>$$U(x) = \frac{Q^2 x}{2\varepsilon_0 A}.$$
>>At $x = 1.0\ \text{mm}$,
>>$$U = \frac{(0.20 \times 10^{-6})^2(1.0 \times 10^{-3})}{2(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(0.040)} = 5.65 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{J}.$$
>>At $3.0\ \text{mm}$ the energy is three times larger, $1.69 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J}$. The hand does the difference, $1.13 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J}$, as work.
>>
>>The energy grows in proportion to $x$, so the force is constant. From [[1.3d.1 Potential Energy]], $F_x = -dU/dx$:
>>$$F_x = -\frac{Q^2}{2\varepsilon_0 A} = -\frac{(0.20 \times 10^{-6})^2}{2(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(0.040)} = -0.0565\ \text{N}.$$
>>The minus sign means the force on the moving plate points toward the other plate, so the plates attract. The hand pulls outward with $0.0565\ \text{N}$ over $2.0\ \text{mm}$, which gives the same $1.13 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J}$.
>>
>>The field stays $E = Q/(\varepsilon_0 A) = 5.65 \times 10^{5}\ \text{V/m}$, so $V$ rises from $565\ \text{V}$ to $1.69\ \text{kV}$. The force equals $\tfrac12 QE$, half of $QE$. Each plate feels only the field of the other plate, which is $E/2$ by the single-sheet result.
