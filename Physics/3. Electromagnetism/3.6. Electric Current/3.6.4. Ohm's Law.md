>[!note] Ohm's Law
>A material obeys **Ohm's law** when its resistivity $\rho$ does not depend on the field. The field and the current density are then proportional at every point:
>$$\vec E = \rho\vec J.$$
>Here $\vec E$ is the field, $\vec J$ is the current density, and $\rho$ is a constant for the material at fixed temperature. Physicists usually call this form Ohm's law.
>
>For a whole object made of such a material, the potential difference is proportional to the current. Let a current $I$ enter the object at end $a$ and leave at end $b$. Then
>$$V_{ab} = IR.$$
>Here $V_{ab} = V_a - V_b$ is the potential at $a$ minus the potential at $b$, and $R$ is the resistance of the object. The resistance depends on the material and the shape. It does not depend on $V_{ab}$ or $I$. This form is the one usually taught as Ohm's law.
>
>A **resistor** is a circuit element made to have a chosen resistance. Its main use is to limit the current in part of a circuit. Its symbol is a zigzag line between its two ends. The EE note [[2.1f.1 Resistor]] treats the resistor as a lumped element with the same law. A lumped element is a part defined only by the relation between its current and its potential difference.
>
>Two methods find $R$ for an object of any shape:
>- Slice and dice. Cut the object into thin slices that the current crosses one after another. The current must cross each slice at right angles and spread evenly over its area. A slice of thickness $ds$ and area $A$ has resistance $dR = \rho\,ds/A$. Add the slices, $R = \int \rho\,ds/A$.
>- From the field. Use symmetry and conservation of charge to write $\vec J$ in terms of $I$. Then find $\vec E = \rho\vec J$. Integrate $\vec E$ from $a$ to $b$ to get $V_{ab}$, and divide by $I$.

# Intuition

The law is named after Georg Ohm. In the 1820s he found that the current in a metal wire is proportional to the potential difference across it. Ohm's law describes how many materials behave. Some materials and devices do not follow it.

In an ohmic material the drift velocity is proportional to the field. Doubling the field doubles the current density at every point. Over a whole object, doubling the potential difference doubles the current. The ratio $V_{ab}/I$ is then a fixed number for that object, its resistance. Once $R$ is known, a circuit needs nothing else about the resistor. A thin wire, a cylinder, and a spherical shell with equal $R$ behave the same way in a circuit.

A thin shell sweeps outward through the spherical resistor of the Hard example. The green area under $dR/dr = \rho/(4\pi r^2)$ adds up to $R = 265\ \Omega$.

# Derivation

The proportionality of $V_{ab}$ and $I$ holds for any shape. Suppose a current $I$ flows through an object with constant $\rho$. Doubling the current doubles $\vec J$ at every point, with the same pattern of flow. Since $\vec E = \rho\vec J$, the field doubles at every point too. The potential difference is the integral of the field along a path through the material:
$$V_{ab} = V_a - V_b = \int_a^b \vec E\cdot d\vec s.$$
Here the path runs from $a$ to $b$, and $d\vec s$ is a small step along it. This is the rule of [[3.4.3. Electric Potential from Electric Field]], with the endpoints swapped to remove its minus sign. The integral doubles when the field doubles. So $V_{ab}/I$ stays the same, and it depends only on the shape and on $\rho$. This ratio is the resistance $R$.

Slice and dice uses two facts. First, every slice carries the whole current $I$, because no charge piles up in a steady state, as in [[3.6.1. Electric Current]]. Second, a thin slice acts like a short uniform wire. Its potential difference is $I\,dR$, with $dR = \rho\,ds/A$ from [[3.6.3. Resistivity]]. The potential differences of successive slices add along the path:
$$V_{ab} = \int I\,dR = I\int \frac{\rho\,ds}{A}.$$
Here $A$ is the area of the slice at position $s$ along the path. Dividing by $I$ gives $R = \int \rho\,ds/A$. The slices must be cut so that the current crosses each one at right angles and spreads evenly over its area. In a symmetric shape, such as the spherical shell, the current does both.

# Properties

#### *(Two Forms of One Law)* The form $\vec E = \rho\vec J$ holds at each point, and $V_{ab} = IR$ holds for a whole object.

The point form uses only material quantities. It contains no length or area. The object form uses a potential difference and a current, which are easy to measure. The step from one form to the other brings in the shape, through $R$. For a uniform wire this step gives $R = \rho L/A$. Other shapes give other formulas, as the examples show. Both forms fail when $\rho$ depends on the field.

#### *(Current Flows Toward Lower Potential)* Through a resistor, the current flows from the higher-potential end to the lower-potential end.

The resistance is positive. So $V_{ab} = IR$ is positive when the current flows from $a$ to $b$. The end where the current enters is therefore at the higher potential. The sign rule for crossing a resistor in [[3.7.5. Kirchhoff's Loop Rule]] comes from this fact.

#### *(Constant Only at Fixed Temperature)* A resistor's $R$ stays constant only while its temperature stays fixed.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not discuss lamp filaments or graphs of current against potential difference. Giancoli section 25.3 covers nonohmic devices.

The resistivity of every material depends a little on the field, but this effect is usually negligible. Resistivity depends strongly on temperature, as in [[3.6.3. Resistivity]]. Each collision passes energy to the atoms, so a current heats a resistor. [[3.6.5. Electric Power]] finds the rate. A small current heats it little, and $R$ stays nearly constant. A lamp filament runs very hot. Its resistance at full brightness is many times its value when cold. A graph of its current against its potential difference is therefore a curve. For a resistor at fixed temperature, the same graph is a straight line through the origin.

#### *(Choosing a Method)* Slice and dice shows each step, and the field method is quicker but needs the current's pattern first.

Slice and dice builds $R$ from pieces that each act like a short uniform wire. Each step has a clear meaning. The field method ends in one routine integral. Before that integral, it needs $\vec J$ at every point. Symmetry and conservation of charge supply $\vec J$, in the same way that symmetry supplied $\vec E$ for [[3.3.2. Gauss's Law|Gauss's law]]. Both methods give the same $R$, as the Hard example shows.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Current Through a Resistor
>A $2.2\ \text{k}\Omega$ resistor has $9.0\ \text{V}$ across it.
>
>>[!example]- Find the current, the number of electrons that pass through each second, and which end is at the higher potential.
>>The current is
>>$$I = \frac{V_{ab}}{R} = \frac{9.0\ \text{V}}{2200\ \Omega} = 4.09 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{A} = 4.09\ \text{mA}.$$
>>Here $V_{ab}$ is the potential difference and $R$ the resistance. Dividing by the electron charge gives $4.09 \times 10^{-3}/(1.602 \times 10^{-19}) = 2.55 \times 10^{16}$ electrons each second. The current enters at the higher-potential end. The electrons move the other way, so they enter at the lower-potential end.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture worked only the spherical shell resistor. This example applies the same slice and dice to a cylindrical shell.

>[!example] (Medium) Leakage Through a Cable's Insulation
>A coaxial cable is $50\ \text{m}$ long. Its inner wire has radius $a = 0.50\ \text{mm}$. A metal tube of inner radius $b = 2.5\ \text{mm}$ surrounds it. Plastic with $\rho = 1.0 \times 10^{13}\ \Omega\cdot\text{m}$ fills the space between them. The inner wire is held $120\ \text{V}$ above the tube.
>
>>[!example]- Find the resistance of the plastic between the wire and the tube, and the current that leaks through it.
>>The leakage current flows outward from the wire to the tube, across the plastic. Slice the plastic into thin cylindrical shells of radius $r$, thickness $dr$, and length $L = 50\ \text{m}$. The current crosses each shell at right angles, through the area $2\pi rL$. Each shell has
>>$$dR = \frac{\rho\,dr}{2\pi rL}.$$
>>Here $dr$ is the distance the current travels through the shell, and $2\pi rL$ is the area it crosses. Adding the shells from $a$ to $b$ gives
>>$$R = \frac{\rho}{2\pi L}\int_a^b \frac{dr}{r} = \frac{\rho}{2\pi L}\ln\frac{b}{a} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{13}}{2\pi(50)}\ln 5 = 5.1 \times 10^{10}\ \Omega.$$
>>Here $\ln(b/a)$ is the natural logarithm of the ratio of the radii. The leakage current is $I = V_{ab}/R = 120/(5.1 \times 10^{10}) = 2.3 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{A}$. A longer cable has a smaller leakage resistance. The current crosses the plastic sideways, so a longer cable gives it more area to cross.

>[!example] (Hard) The Spherical Shell Resistor
>A metal sphere of radius $a$ sits at the center of a metal shell of inner radius $b$. Material with resistivity $\rho$ fills the space between them. A wire through a small hole in the shell connects the inner sphere to a supply. A current $I$ then flows outward from the sphere to the shell. This is a classroom example, worked both ways.
>
>>[!example]- Find the resistance by slice and dice and again from the field. Then evaluate it for $a = 1.0\ \text{cm}$, $b = 3.0\ \text{cm}$, $\rho = 50\ \Omega\cdot\text{m}$, and $V_{ab} = 12\ \text{V}$, and find the field at each surface.
>>The setup is spherically symmetric, so the current flows straight outward and spreads evenly in all directions.
>>
>>For slice and dice, cut the material into thin spherical shells. The current crosses a shell of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$ at right angles, through the area $4\pi r^2$. The shell's resistance is
>>$$dR = \frac{\rho\,dr}{4\pi r^2}.$$
>>Here $dr$ is the path length through the shell, and $4\pi r^2$ is its area. Adding the shells from $a$ to $b$ gives
>>$$R = \frac{\rho}{4\pi}\int_a^b \frac{dr}{r^2} = \frac{\rho}{4\pi}\left(\frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{b}\right).$$
>>Here the integral of $1/r^2$ is $-1/r$, evaluated from $a$ to $b$.
>>
>>For the field method, draw a sphere of radius $r$ between $a$ and $b$. No charge piles up inside the material, so the whole current $I$ crosses this sphere. It spreads evenly over the area $4\pi r^2$, and it points outward. So
>>$$\vec J = \frac{I}{4\pi r^2}\,\hat r, \qquad \vec E = \rho\vec J = \frac{\rho I}{4\pi r^2}\,\hat r.$$
>>Here $\hat r$ is the outward unit vector. Integrate from the inner sphere $a$ to the outer shell $b$ along a radius, where $\hat r\cdot d\vec s = dr$:
>>$$V_{ab} = \int_a^b \frac{\rho I}{4\pi r^2}\,dr = \frac{\rho I}{4\pi}\left(\frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{b}\right).$$
>>Here $V_{ab} = V_a - V_b$ is the potential of the inner sphere minus that of the shell. The result is positive, so the inner sphere is at the higher potential, as the outward current requires. Dividing by $I$ gives the same $R$ as slice and dice.
>>
>>With the numbers,
>>$$R = \frac{50}{4\pi}\left(\frac{1}{0.010} - \frac{1}{0.030}\right) = (3.98)(66.7) = 265\ \Omega.$$
>>Here the radii are in meters, so $R$ is in ohms. The current is $I = 12/265 = 0.0452\ \text{A}$. At the inner surface the field is $E = \rho I/(4\pi a^2) = 1.80 \times 10^{3}\ \text{V/m}$. At the outer surface it is nine times smaller, $200\ \text{V/m}$. The shells between $1.0\ \text{cm}$ and $2.0\ \text{cm}$ give $(1/0.010 - 1/0.020)/66.7 = 75\%$ of $R$. Most of the resistance sits near the inner sphere, where the current crosses the least area.
