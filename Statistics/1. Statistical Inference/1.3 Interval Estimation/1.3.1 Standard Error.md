>[!note] Standard Error
>The **standard error** of an [[1.1 Estimators|estimator]] $\hat\Theta_n = g(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ is the standard deviation of its sampling distribution:
>$$\text{SE}(\hat\Theta_n) = \sqrt{\text{Var}(\hat\Theta_n)}.$$
>For the sample mean $\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ of i.i.d. observations with population standard deviation $\sigma$, this simplifies to
>$$\text{SE}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}.$$
>When $\sigma$ is unknown (the typical case), it is replaced by the sample standard deviation $s$, giving the **estimated standard error** $\widehat{\text{SE}}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$.
>
>When sampling without replacement from a finite population of size $N$, the standard error includes the **finite population correction**:
>$$\text{SE}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\frac{N - n}{N - 1}}.$$
>This factor is negligible when $n \ll N$ but becomes significant when the sampling fraction $n/N$ is large.
>
>>[!warning] Standard Error vs. Standard Deviation
>>The standard deviation $\sigma$ measures the spread of **individual observations** in the population. The standard error measures the spread of a **statistic** (like $\bar{X}$) across repeated samples. As $n$ grows, $\sigma$ stays fixed but $\text{SE} \to 0$, reflecting that larger samples yield more precise estimates.

# Intuition
The standard error quantifies **how much an estimate would vary if we repeated the entire experiment**. Imagine drawing a sample of size $n$, computing $\bar{X}$, then drawing a new sample and computing $\bar{X}$ again, and so on. The standard error is the standard deviation of all these $\bar{X}$ values.

The $1/\sqrt{n}$ dependence is the key insight: doubling precision requires quadrupling the sample size. This follows directly from the variance of a sum of independent random variables—each observation contributes $\sigma^2$ to the variance of the total, so $\text{Var}(\bar{X}) = \sigma^2/n$.

# Properties
#### (*Rate of Decay*) $\text{SE}(\bar{X}_n) = O(1/\sqrt{n})$, so the standard error halves when the sample size is quadrupled.
#### (*CLT Connection*) By the [[3.3 Central Limit Theorem|central limit theorem]], $\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\text{SE}(\bar{X}_n)} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ as $n \to \infty$, which justifies using normal-based [[1.3.2 Confidence Intervals|confidence intervals]].
#### (*General Estimators*) For estimators other than the sample mean—proportions, regression coefficients, medians—the standard error takes different forms but always measures sampling variability. When no closed-form expression exists, the [[1.5.1 Classical Bootstrap|bootstrap]] provides a nonparametric estimate of SE.

# Examples
>[!example] Poll Margin of Error
>A poll of $n = 1000$ voters finds $\hat{p} = 0.52$ support for a candidate. The estimated standard error of the proportion is $\widehat{\text{SE}}(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$.
>>[!example]- Calculation
>>$$\widehat{\text{SE}}(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\frac{0.52 \times 0.48}{1000}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.2496}{1000}} \approx 0.0158.$$
>>The "margin of error" reported by polls at the $95\%$ level is $1.96 \times 0.0158 \approx 0.031$, or about $\pm 3.1$ percentage points. This means the true proportion plausibly lies in $[0.489, 0.551]$—the race is too close to call.
