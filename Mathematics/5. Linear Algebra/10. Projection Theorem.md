>[!note] Projection Theorem
>For any vector $\vec{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and a subspace $\mathcal{S} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$, the projection of $\vec{x}$ onto $\mathcal{S}$ is the unique linear operator in $\mathcal{S}$ that minimizes the distance between $\vec{x}$ and $\mathcal{S}$, defined as
>$$\Pi_\mathcal{S}(\vec{x})=\arg\min\limits_{\vec{y} \in \mathcal{S}} ||\vec{x}-\vec{y}||,$$
>such that $\Pi_\mathcal{S}(\vec{x}) \in \mathcal{S}$ and the residual vector $\vec{x}-\Pi_S(\vec{x}) \perp \mathcal{S}$.
>
>Then, the general solution of $\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}(\vec{x})$, given a matrix $S \in \mathcal{S}^n$, such that $\operatorname{rank}(S)=n$ (i.e., $S$ is full-rank),
>$$\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}(\vec{x}) = S(S^TS)^{-1}S^T\vec{x},$$
>where $P = S(S^TS)^{-1}S^T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is called the **projection matrix**.
>
>If $S$ is not full rank (i.e., $\operatorname{rank}(S)<n$), given an orthonormal basis matrix $B_{S}$ of $\mathcal{S}$, the orthonormal projection of $\vec{x}$ onto $\mathcal{S}$ can be expressed as
>$$\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}(\vec{x}) = B_{S}^TB_{S}\vec{x} = \sum^{n}_{i=1}\langle \vec{(B_{S})}_{i}, \vec{x} \rangle \vec{(B_{S})}_{i},$$
>which can be equivalently written as $\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}(\vec{x}) = \sum^{n}_{i=1}\frac{\langle \vec{s}_{i}, \vec{x} \rangle}{\|\vec{s}_{i}\|^2} \vec{s}_{i}$ if the basis vectors $s_{i}$ are orthogonal rather than orthonormal.

# Examples
>[!example] Projection Onto a Hyperplane
>**Conditions:** Let $y = \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$, and let $\mathcal{H} = \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : c^Tx = 0 \}$ be a hyperplane<sup>[[3.4f Hyperplanes|3.4c]]</sup> in $\mathbb{R^2}$, such that $c=\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$. 
>
>**Task:** Compute the minimum distance from $y$ to $\mathcal{H}$.
>
>**Solution:** Since $c$ is the normal direction of the hyperplane, it follows that $y - p^* \parallel c$ (since the error term of a projection is always perpendicular to the space of the projection). This, in turn, implies that the error term is some scaling of $c$, such that 
>$$y-p^* = \alpha c.$$
>
>Since $p^* \in \mathcal{H} \perp c$, it also follows that $c^Tp^*=0$.  As such, 
>$$c^T(y-p^*) = \langle c, y \rangle - 0 = \alpha \|c\|^2_{2} \iff \alpha = \frac{\langle c, y \rangle}{\|c\|^2_{2}}.$$
>
>Therefore, $\alpha = \frac{2}{2} = 1$, meaning that the distance $\|y-p^*\|_{2}=\|c\|_{2}=\sqrt{ 2 }$.
