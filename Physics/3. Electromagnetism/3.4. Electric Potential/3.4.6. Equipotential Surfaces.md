>[!note] Equipotential Surfaces
>A conductor in [[3.3.3. Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium|electrostatic equilibrium]] has the same potential at every point inside it and on its surface. The field inside is zero. So $V_B - V_A = -\int_A^B \vec E\cdot d\vec s = 0$ for any two points A and B in the metal. A connected piece of metal therefore has one potential. Circuit analysis uses this fact. Every point of a connecting wire sits at one potential, and the whole wire counts as a single node.
>
>An **equipotential surface** is a surface on which $V$ has the same value at every point. Its properties:
>- The field is perpendicular to every equipotential surface, at every point.
>- The field points from higher-valued equipotentials toward lower-valued ones.
>- The field does no work on a test charge $q_0$ moving along an equipotential, since $W = -q_0\,\Delta V = 0$.
>- Two equipotentials with different values never meet, since each point has one potential.
>- The surface of a conductor is an equipotential.
>
>Standard shapes:
>- A point charge has spheres centered on the charge.
>- A uniform field has planes perpendicular to the field.
>- An infinite line has cylinders around the line.
>- A dipole has closed surfaces around each charge, and its bisecting plane is the surface $V = 0$.
>
>An **equipotential map** draws the surfaces at equal steps of $V$, such as every $10\ \text{V}$. Close spacing means a strong field. The field size is about
>$$E \approx \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta s},$$
>where $\Delta V$ is the step and $\Delta s$ is the distance between neighboring surfaces.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture stated only that a conductor has one potential throughout, and that circuit analysis uses this. Equipotential surfaces in general, their right angle with the field, and equipotential maps follow Giancoli section 23.5.

# Intuition

In a gravitational field, a level floor has the same height everywhere. A ball on it feels no push along the floor, because gravity points straight down. An equipotential surface is the electric counterpart of the level floor. A charge can move along it with no work from the field, because the field has no component along it.

The equipotentials and the field lines cross at right angles everywhere. Where the field is strong, $V$ changes quickly with distance. Surfaces drawn at equal steps of $V$ then sit close together. Where the field is weak, they sit far apart.

Equipotentials of a point charge and of a dipole are drawn every 100 V. Field lines cross them at right angles. The field does no work on a test charge that moves along an equipotential. It does positive work on a positive test charge that moves along $\vec E$.

# Derivation

To show that the field is perpendicular to an equipotential, take a small step $d\vec s$ within the surface. Along the step, $V$ does not change:
$$dV = -\vec E\cdot d\vec s = 0.$$
The dot product is zero for every direction within the surface. So $\vec E$ has no component along the surface and crosses it at a right angle. A step along $\vec E$ gives $dV = -E\,ds < 0$, so the field points toward lower $V$.

For a conductor, $\vec E = 0$ inside the metal, so the integral between any two interior points is zero. All interior points therefore share one potential. The potential has no jumps, as shown in [[3.4.3. Electric Potential from Electric Field]]. So each point on the surface takes the value of the interior points next to it. The surface and the interior share one value of $V$.

# Properties

#### *(Two Arguments for the Right Angle)* A conductor's surface field is perpendicular to the surface for two independent reasons.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture gave only the force argument. The potential argument uses equipotentials, from Giancoli section 23.5.

The force argument in [[3.3.3. Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium]] says a field component along the surface would push the free charges. Equilibrium therefore allows none. The potential argument starts from the interior. The surface takes the interior's potential, so it is an equipotential. Every field crosses an equipotential at a right angle. Neither argument uses the other's result, and each alone is enough.

#### *(Drawing Equipotentials)* Sketch the field lines first, then draw the equipotentials crossing them at right angles.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not draw equipotentials. This method follows Giancoli section 23.5.

Field lines are usually easier to sketch from the charges. Each equipotential then follows a path that meets every field line at $90^\circ$. Near a point charge the curves close into circles around it. Between two equal and opposite charges, the curve through the midpoint is the straight bisector, at $V = 0$. A sketch where two equipotentials with different values meet has an error. So does a sketch where a field line meets an equipotential at a slant.

# Examples

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The next two examples use equipotential surfaces, from Giancoli section 23.5.

>[!example] (Easy) Equipotentials Between Plates
>Two large parallel plates sit $5.0\ \text{mm}$ apart. The potential difference between them is $150\ \text{V}$.
>
>>[!example]- Describe the equipotential surfaces at $0$, $50$, $100$, and $150\ \text{V}$, and find their spacing.
>>The field between the plates is uniform, with size $E = 150/0.0050 = 3.0 \times 10^{4}\ \text{V/m}$. The equipotentials are planes parallel to the plates. The two plates are the $0$ and $150\ \text{V}$ surfaces. Each step of $50\ \text{V}$ takes the distance
>>$$\Delta s = \frac{\Delta V}{E} = \frac{50}{3.0 \times 10^{4}} = 1.7 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}.$$
>>The four planes are evenly spaced, $1.7\ \text{mm}$ apart, because the field is uniform.

>[!example] (Medium) Equipotentials Around a Point Charge
>A small sphere carries $+2.0\ \text{nC}$. Take $V = 0$ at infinity.
>
>>[!example]- Find the radii of the $300$, $200$, and $100\ \text{V}$ equipotentials. Use their spacing to estimate the field between them.
>>The equipotentials are spheres of radius $r = q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 V)$, with $q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 17.98\ \text{V}\cdot\text{m}$:
>>$$r_{300} = \frac{17.98}{300} = 0.060\ \text{m}, \qquad r_{200} = 0.090\ \text{m}, \qquad r_{100} = 0.180\ \text{m}.$$
>>The first gap is $3.0\ \text{cm}$, and the second is $9.0\ \text{cm}$. The estimates are $E \approx 100/0.030 = 3.3 \times 10^{3}\ \text{V/m}$ and $E \approx 100/0.090 = 1.1 \times 10^{3}\ \text{V/m}$. The exact fields at the middle radii, $7.5$ and $13.5\ \text{cm}$, are $3.2 \times 10^{3}$ and $0.99 \times 10^{3}\ \text{V/m}$. Equal steps of $V$ sit closer together near the charge, where the field is stronger.

>[!example] (Hard) Two Spheres Joined by a Wire
>Two metal spheres of radii $2.0\ \text{cm}$ and $6.0\ \text{cm}$ sit far apart. A long thin wire joins them, and it holds a negligible charge. Together the spheres carry $16\ \text{nC}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the charge on each sphere, their common potential, and the field at each surface.
>>The spheres and the wire form one conductor, so they share one potential. The spheres are far apart, so each one's charge spreads evenly over its surface. Outside a uniform spherical shell, the potential is that of a point charge at its center. Equal potentials need
>>$$\frac{Q_1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R_1} = \frac{Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R_2}, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \frac{Q_1}{Q_2} = \frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{1}{3}.$$
>>With $Q_1 + Q_2 = 16\ \text{nC}$, the charges are $Q_1 = 4.0\ \text{nC}$ and $Q_2 = 12\ \text{nC}$. The common potential is
>>$$V = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(4.0 \times 10^{-9})}{0.020} = 1.8 \times 10^{3}\ \text{V}.$$
>>The field at each surface is $E = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2) = V/R$:
>>$$E_1 = \frac{1798}{0.020} = 9.0 \times 10^{4}\ \text{V/m}, \qquad E_2 = \frac{1798}{0.060} = 3.0 \times 10^{4}\ \text{V/m}.$$
>>The smaller sphere holds less charge. Its surface field is three times as strong as the larger sphere's.
