>[!note] Specific Heat
>The heat needed to change the temperature of an object is proportional to its mass and to the temperature change,
>$$Q = m\, c\, \Delta T,$$
>where $m$ is the mass, $\Delta T = T_f - T_i$ is the temperature change, and $c$ is the **specific heat** of the material. The SI unit of $c$ is $\text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K})$, which equals $\text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C})$ because $\Delta T$ is the same on both scales. The sign follows $\Delta T$: heat flows in when the object warms and out when it cools. The relation holds as long as the material stays in one phase. A phase change absorbs [[2.4.4 Latent Heat|latent heat]] with no temperature change at all.
>
>The specific heat of water is
>$$c_{\text{water}} = 4186\ \text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}) = 1.00\ \text{kcal}/(\text{kg}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}),$$
>and the second form is the definition of the [[2.4.2 Heat|calorie]]. The table lists values near $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and atmospheric pressure. Specific heats vary slowly with temperature, so the values serve for ordinary ranges.
>
>| Substance | $c$ $(\text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}))$ |
>|---|---|
>| Water (liquid) | $4186$ |
>| Ice ($-5\ ^\circ\text{C}$) | $2100$ |
>| Steam ($110\ ^\circ\text{C}$) | $2010$ |
>| Ethyl alcohol | $2400$ |
>| Human body (average) | $3470$ |
>| Wood | $1700$ |
>| Aluminum | $900$ |
>| Glass | $840$ |
>| Iron or steel | $450$ |
>| Copper | $390$ |
>| Zinc | $390$ |
>| Brass | $380$ |
>| Silver | $230$ |
>| Mercury | $140$ |
>| Lead | $130$ |
>
>The **heat capacity** of a particular object is $C = mc$, in $\text{J/K}$, so that $Q = C\, \Delta T$. For a gas the heat capacity depends on the process. At constant volume a sample of $n$ moles has $C = nC_V = \tfrac{d}{2} nR = \tfrac{d}{2} Nk$, as [[2.5.4 Molar Specific Heats]] shows. The **molar specific heat** is the heat per mole per kelvin, $c_{\text{mol}} = Mc$ with $M$ the molar mass in kilograms per mole. For gases the specific heat depends on whether the volume or the pressure is held fixed while the gas is heated. The two values, $c_V$ and $c_P$, and the reason they differ are the subject of [[2.5.4 Molar Specific Heats]].

# Intuition

The specific heat says how much energy a kilogram of the material must absorb to warm by one degree. Temperature tracks the average kinetic energy per molecule, so the question behind $c$ is how many places the energy has to go. A kilogram of a substance with light molecules contains more molecules than a kilogram of a heavy one. Each molecule takes its share of the energy. That is why metals with heavy atoms, such as lead and mercury, have small specific heats. Molecules that can rotate, vibrate, or pull on their neighbors have further places to store energy, which raises $c$ again. Water is light per molecule and its molecules cling to one another, so it sits at the top of the table. The diagram shows the same heat delivered to equal masses of water and copper.

The high specific heat of water shapes daily life. A pot of water takes a long time to boil and a long time to cool. Coastal cities have mild climates because the sea absorbs summer heat with little warming and returns it in winter. Car engines are cooled with water because each kilogram carries away more energy per degree than any other common liquid. The same property makes water a poor choice when quick heating is the goal and a good choice when steady temperature is.

# Properties

#### *(Heat Capacity of an Object)* An object's heat capacity $C = mc$ combines mass and material into one number.

A large object of low-$c$ material can hold more heat per degree than a small object of high-$c$ material. A $10\ \text{kg}$ iron pan has $C = 4500\ \text{J/K}$, more than a $1\ \text{kg}$ kettle of water at $4186\ \text{J/K}$. A sheet of aluminum foil straight from a hot oven can be held in bare fingers. Its mass, and so its heat capacity, is tiny, even though aluminum has a respectable specific heat. In a classroom demonstration, equal-sized spheres of brass, iron, zinc, lead, and glass are heated to $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and set on a wax sheet. The brass, iron, and zinc melt through and the lead and glass do not. Lead has a low specific heat, glass has a low density, and both therefore have a small heat capacity for the same volume. For equal volumes the heat capacity is $C = \rho V c$, so the product $\rho c$ decides. Per cubic centimeter, iron holds $3.5\ \text{J/K}$, brass $3.2$, zinc $2.8$, glass $2.1$, and lead $1.5$. Brass and zinc have nearly the same $c$, and brass wins by being denser. Heat capacity is the number that [[2.4.5 Calorimetry|calorimetry]] uses for each part of a system. A thermometer must have a small heat capacity compared with the object it measures.

#### *(Metals Are Alike per Mole)* Most solid elements have a molar specific heat near $25\ \text{J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K})$ at room temperature.

Multiplying $c$ by the molar mass gives $24.8$ for copper, $24.3$ for aluminum, $25.1$ for iron, and $26.9$ for lead, all close to $3R = 24.9\ \text{J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K})$. This is the **Dulong-Petit rule**, found in 1819. Each atom in a solid vibrates in three directions, and each direction stores kinetic and potential energy, for six shares of $\tfrac{1}{2} kT$ per atom. The large differences in the table's $c$ values come almost entirely from differences in atomic mass, not from differences in how atoms store energy. The rule fails at low temperature, where quantum effects freeze out the vibrations.

#### *(Temperature Dependence)* Specific heat changes with temperature and with phase, so a table value applies to a range.

Ice, liquid water, and steam have specific heats of $2100$, $4186$, and $2010\ \text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K})$. A problem that takes water from $-10\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to $110\ ^\circ\text{C}$ must use the three values in turn, with latent heat at each phase change. Within one phase the variation is a few percent over a hundred degrees for most substances, and problems at this level treat $c$ as constant.

#### *(Solids and Liquids Only)* $Q = mc\,\Delta T$ is a formula for solids and liquids, and it fails for gases.

A solid or a liquid barely changes volume when heated. Almost all of the heat goes into internal energy, and one number $c$ describes the material. A gas is different, because its pressure and volume are easy to change. Heating a gas in a rigid container raises its internal energy and nothing else. Heating it under a free piston also expands it, and the gas does work on the piston, so more heat is needed for the same $\Delta T$. Heating it at constant temperature, by letting it expand in a bath, gives $\Delta T = 0$ with $Q \neq 0$, which no value of $c$ can describe. The heat absorbed by a gas depends on the process, and it must be computed from the [[2.5.2 First Law of Thermodynamics|first law]] rather than looked up. The constant-volume and constant-pressure cases do reduce to the form $Q = n C\, \Delta T$, with $C_V = \tfrac{d}{2} R$ and $C_P = \left(\tfrac{d}{2} + 1\right) R$, and [[2.5.4 Molar Specific Heats]] derives both.

# Derivation

We want to see why the heat needed to warm an object is proportional to its mass and to $\Delta T$. By the [[2.3.1 Molecular Interpretation of Temperature|molecular interpretation of temperature]], raising the temperature by $\Delta T$ raises the average energy per particle by an amount proportional to $k\,\Delta T$. For a solid with $d = 6$ energy-storing modes per atom (three for motion and three for the spring-like bonds), [[2.3.4 Equipartition of Energy|equipartition]] makes the rise $\tfrac{d}{2} k\,\Delta T$. The object holds $N$ particles, so the total energy added is
$$Q = \tfrac{d}{2}\, N k\, \Delta T.$$
Counting particles is inconvenient, but weighing is easy. If each particle has mass $m_p$, then $N = m / m_p$, and
$$Q = \left( \frac{d\, k}{2\, m_p} \right) m\, \Delta T = m\, c\, \Delta T, \qquad c = \frac{d\, k}{2\, m_p}.$$
Everything in $c$ is a property of the material: how many modes each particle has and how much each particle weighs. Nothing in $c$ depends on the size of the sample, which is why $c$ is a material constant and $C = mc$ is not. With $d = 6$ the molar value is $c\, N_A m_p = 3 N_A k = 3R$, the Dulong-Petit value.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Boiling a Kettle
>An electric kettle rated at $1500\ \text{W}$ holds $0.250\ \text{kg}$ of water at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- How much heat brings the water to $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$, and how long does it take if all the power goes into the water?
>>The heat required is
>>$$Q = mc\,\Delta T = (0.250\ \text{kg})(4186\ \text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}))(80\ \text{K}) = 8.37 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}.$$
>>At $1500\ \text{J/s}$ this takes $8.37 \times 10^{4} / 1500 = 56\ \text{s}$. A real kettle takes longer, because some heat warms the kettle itself and some escapes to the air. Bringing the water to a boil does not yet turn any of it to steam. That needs the much larger latent heat.

>[!example] (Medium) Same Heat, Different Metal
>The $8.37 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}$ from the previous example is delivered instead to a block of copper.
>
>>[!example]- What mass of copper warms by the same $80\ \text{K}$, and by how much would a $0.250\ \text{kg}$ block warm?
>>Solving $Q = mc\,\Delta T$ for the mass,
>>$$m = \frac{Q}{c\,\Delta T} = \frac{8.37 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}}{(390\ \text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}))(80\ \text{K})} = 2.7\ \text{kg},$$
>>nearly eleven times the mass of water. A $0.250\ \text{kg}$ block would warm by $\Delta T = Q / mc = 8.37 \times 10^{4} / (0.250 \times 390) = 860\ \text{K}$, which would take it well past the point where copper glows. Copper's specific heat is $10.7$ times smaller than water's, and every result scales by that factor.

>[!example] (Hard) Hot Brakes
>A $1200\ \text{kg}$ car traveling at $25.0\ \text{m/s}$ stops. Its kinetic energy goes into four iron brake discs with a total mass of $12.0\ \text{kg}$, and none escapes during the stop.
>
>>[!example]- By how much does the temperature of the discs rise?
>>The kinetic energy is
>>$$K = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(1200\ \text{kg})(25.0\ \text{m/s})^2 = 3.75 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}.$$
>>All of it becomes internal energy of the discs, so
>>$$\Delta T = \frac{K}{m_{\text{disc}}\, c_{\text{iron}}} = \frac{3.75 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}}{(12.0\ \text{kg})(450\ \text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K}))} = 69\ \text{K}.$$
>>Repeated hard stops on a mountain road stack these rises. Air cooling between stops is what keeps the discs from overheating. The energy entered as work against friction, not as heat, but $Q = mc\,\Delta T$ applies to any process that raises the internal energy by that amount.
