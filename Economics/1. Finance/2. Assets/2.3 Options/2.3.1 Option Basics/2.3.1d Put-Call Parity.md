>[!note] Put-Call Parity
>**Put-call parity** links the prices of a European call and a European put on the same stock, with the same strike $K$ and the same expiry $T$. Let $c$ and $p$ be their prices today, $S$ the stock price today, and $r$ the continuously compounded risk-free rate. For a stock that pays no dividends before $T$,
>$$c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S.$$
>The left side is a call plus a zero-coupon bond that pays $K$ at $T$. The right side is a put plus one share. Both portfolios pay $\max(S_T, K)$ at expiry, so in a market without arbitrage they cost the same today. Written as $c - p = S - Ke^{-rT}$, the parity says that a long call plus a short put is a long [[2.2.2c Value of a Forward Contract|forward contract]] with delivery price $K$.
>
>Dividends change only the stock side. If the stock pays known cash dividends with present value $\text{PV}(D)$ before $T$, or a continuous dividend yield $q$, the parity becomes
>$$c - p = S - \text{PV}(D) - Ke^{-rT}, \qquad c - p = Se^{-qT} - Ke^{-rT}.$$
>The parity holds for European options only. Let $C$ and $P$ be the prices of the American call and put. On a stock with no dividends they satisfy two inequalities instead,
>$$S - K \le C - P \le S - Ke^{-rT}.$$
>The parity assumes a frictionless market: no transaction costs or taxes, free short selling, and borrowing and lending at the same rate $r$. It does not depend on any model of how the stock price moves.

# Intuition

A long call and a short put together pay the same amount whichever way the stock moves. Above $K$, the call pays $S_T - K$ and the short put costs nothing. Below $K$, the call pays nothing and the short put loses $K - S_T$. In both cases the position pays $S_T - K$. That is the payoff of a long forward contract with delivery price $K$. Such a forward is worth $S - Ke^{-rT}$ today, so the call minus the put must be worth the same.

# Properties

#### *(Forward Decomposition)* The dividend versions of the parity follow from the forward, with no new option argument.

The identity $\max(S_T - K, 0) - \max(K - S_T, 0) = S_T - K$ holds in every state, whatever the stock pays before $T$. So the option pair is always worth exactly what a forward with delivery price $K$ is worth. A forward on a stock with cash dividends is worth $S - \text{PV}(D) - Ke^{-rT}$. A forward on a stock with dividend yield $q$ is worth $Se^{-qT} - Ke^{-rT}$. Each value gives the matching line of the callout.

#### *(Lower Bounds as Slack)* The gap between an option price and its lower bound equals the price of the other option.

The [[2.3.1b Option Price Bounds|lower bounds]] for European options on a stock with no dividends are $c \ge S - Ke^{-rT}$ and $p \ge Ke^{-rT} - S$. Rearranging the parity gives the size of each gap,
$$c - (S - Ke^{-rT}) = p, \qquad p - (Ke^{-rT} - S) = c.$$
Option prices are never negative, so the parity implies both bounds. It also shows when a bound is nearly tight. A deep in-the-money call trades close to $S - Ke^{-rT}$, because the matching put is deep out of the money and almost worthless.

#### *(Synthetic Positions)* Any one of the four instruments can be built from the other three.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules state the parity and the forward decomposition. They do not name synthetic positions, conversions, or reversals.

Rearranging $c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S$ gives four replicating portfolios. A **synthetic stock** is a long call, a short put, and a loan of $Ke^{-rT}$ made today, since $S = c - p + Ke^{-rT}$. A synthetic call is a long put, a long share, and a borrowing of $Ke^{-rT}$. A synthetic put is a long call, a short share, and a loan of $Ke^{-rT}$. A synthetic bond is a long put, a long share, and a short call. Traders call that last position a **conversion**. It pays exactly $K$ at expiry. Its mirror image, short stock with a long call and a short put, is a **reversal**. It owes exactly $K$ at expiry.

#### *(American Options)* Early exercise turns the parity into a band of width $K(1 - e^{-rT})$.

An American put on a stock with no dividends may be worth exercising early, so $P \ge p$. An American call on the same stock is never exercised early, so $C = c$ ([[2.3.1c Early Exercise]]). The upper bound $C - P \le S - Ke^{-rT}$ follows from these two facts and the European parity. The lower bound $S - K \le C - P$ needs its own argument, given below. The gap between the two bounds is the interest earned on $K$ over the life of the options. It shrinks to zero as $r \to 0$ or $T \to 0$.

>[!example]- Proof of the lower bound $S - K \le C - P$
>Form a portfolio $H$ that holds one American call, writes one American put, sells one share short, and deposits $K$ in cash at rate $r$. Its value today is $H = C - P - S + K$. We show that $H$ can never be negative.
>
>Suppose the put holder exercises at a time $t < T$. We pay $K$ and receive the share, which closes the short sale. We still hold the call and the cash left over, $Ke^{rt} - K$. The portfolio is worth $C_t + K(e^{rt} - 1) \ge 0$.
>
>Suppose instead the put is not exercised before $T$. If $S_T \le K$, the put is exercised and the call expires. The portfolio is worth $-(K - S_T) - S_T + Ke^{rT} = K(e^{rT} - 1) \ge 0$. If $S_T > K$, the put expires and the call pays $S_T - K$. The portfolio is worth $(S_T - K) - S_T + Ke^{rT} = K(e^{rT} - 1) \ge 0$.
>
>The portfolio is worth at least zero in every case. With no arbitrage, it cannot cost less than zero today. So $C - P - S + K \ge 0$.

#### *(Model-Free)* Every arbitrage-free pricing model satisfies the parity automatically.

The parity rests on payoffs alone, so a pricing model cannot break it without allowing arbitrage. In a [[2.3.3c Binomial Tree|binomial tree]], the price is a discounted expectation, which is linear. The expected payoff of the call minus the put is the expected payoff of the forward. In the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]], the normal CDF satisfies $N(x) + N(-x) = 1$. Subtracting the put formula from the call formula gives
$$c - p = S\big[N(d_1) + N(-d_1)\big] - Ke^{-rT}\big[N(d_2) + N(-d_2)\big] = S - Ke^{-rT}.$$
A model price that fails the parity points to a bug in the pricing code.

# Derivation

We want to show $c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S$ for a stock with no dividends.

Form two portfolios today. Portfolio A holds one European call and a zero-coupon bond that pays $K$ at $T$. The bond costs $Ke^{-rT}$, so A costs $c + Ke^{-rT}$. Portfolio B holds one European put and one share, and costs $p + S$. The table lists each position's payoff at expiry.

| Position | $S_T > K$ | $S_T \le K$ |
|---|---|---|
| Call | $S_T - K$ | $0$ |
| Bond | $K$ | $K$ |
| Portfolio A | $S_T$ | $K$ |
| Put | $0$ | $K - S_T$ |
| Share | $S_T$ | $S_T$ |
| Portfolio B | $S_T$ | $K$ |

Both portfolios pay $\max(S_T, K)$ in every state. European options cannot be exercised before $T$, so neither portfolio has any other cash flow. By the [[4.2.2a Arbitrage|law of one price]], two portfolios with the same payoff in every state have the same price today. Hence $c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S$.

Dividends change Portfolio B only. With known cash dividends, B holds the share and borrows $\text{PV}(D)$, and the dividends repay the loan as they arrive. With a dividend yield $q$, B holds $e^{-qT}$ shares and reinvests every dividend in more shares. Either way B ends with one share at $T$. Its cost today is $p + S - \text{PV}(D)$ or $p + Se^{-qT}$, which gives the two dividend formulas in the callout.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Pricing a Put From a Call
>A stock trades at $\$52$ and pays no dividends in the next three months. A three-month European call with strike $\$50$ costs $\$4.20$. The risk-free rate is $5\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Find the price of the three-month European put with strike $\$50$.
>>Solve the parity for $p$:
>>$$p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S = 4.20 + 50e^{-0.05 \times 0.25} - 52.$$
>>The discounted strike is $50e^{-0.0125} = 49.38$. So $p = 4.20 + 49.38 - 52 = \$1.58$. The call costs more than the put because the stock is already $\$2$ above the strike.

>[!example] (Medium) Dividends Raise the Put
>A stock trades at $\$64$. It will pay a dividend of $\$0.80$ in one month and another $\$0.80$ in four months. A six-month European call with strike $\$65$ costs $\$3.10$. The risk-free rate is $6\%$ per year with continuous compounding for all maturities.
>
>>[!example]- Find the price of the six-month European put with strike $\$65$, and explain why the dividends raise it.
>>The present value of the dividends is
>>$$\text{PV}(D) = 0.80e^{-0.06 \times 1/12} + 0.80e^{-0.06 \times 4/12} = 0.796 + 0.784 = \$1.580.$$
>>The discounted strike is $65e^{-0.06 \times 0.5} = \$63.079$. Solve the dividend version of the parity for $p$:
>>$$p = c - S + \text{PV}(D) + Ke^{-rT} = 3.10 - 64 + 1.580 + 63.079 = \$3.76.$$
>>Without the dividends the same formula would give $\$2.18$. The stock falls by the dividend amount on each ex-dividend date. That drop helps the put holder, who gains when the stock is low. The put price rises by exactly $\text{PV}(D)$ when the call price is held fixed.

>[!example] (Hard) Trading a Parity Violation
>A stock trades at $\$40$ and pays no dividends. One-year options with strike $\$40$ are quoted at $\$4.50$ for the call and $\$2.00$ for the put. The risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Show that the quotes break put-call parity, build a trade that profits from it, and check whether the trade still works if both options are American.
>>The parity requires $c - p = S - Ke^{-rT} = 40 - 40e^{-0.04} = 40 - 38.43 = \$1.57$. The quotes give $c - p = \$2.50$. The call is too expensive relative to the put.
>>
>>Sell the expensive side and buy the cheap side. Write the call for $\$4.50$, buy the put for $\$2.00$, buy the share for $\$40$, and borrow $\$38.43$ for one year. The cash flow today is
>>$$4.50 - 2.00 - 40 + 38.43 = +\$0.93.$$
>>At expiry the put and the share together pay $\max(S_T, 40)$. The short call costs $\max(S_T - 40, 0)$, and the loan costs $\$40$. The total is $\max(S_T, 40) - \max(S_T - 40, 0) - 40 = 0$ in every state. The trade keeps $\$0.93$ today and owes nothing later. This position is a conversion.
>>
>>With American options, the upper bound of the band is $C - P \le S - Ke^{-rT} = \$1.57$. The quotes give $\$2.50$, so they break the American band too. The same trade still works. We own the put, so we choose when to exercise it. If the call holder exercises early at time $t$, we deliver our share and receive $\$40$. The loan balance is then $38.43e^{0.04t} \le \$40$, so we can repay it and still hold the put.
