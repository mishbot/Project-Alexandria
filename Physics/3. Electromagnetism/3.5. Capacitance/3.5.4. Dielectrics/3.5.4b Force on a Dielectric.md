>[!note] Force on a Dielectric
>A dielectric slab partly inside a charged capacitor is pulled into the gap. The force follows from the stored energy, with no need to know where the induced charges sit. The setup:
>- Square plates of side $L$ sit a distance $d$ apart. The capacitor is charged to $\pm Q$ and then disconnected from its supply, so $Q$ stays fixed.
>- A slab with dielectric constant $K$, thickness $d$, and width $L$ slides in from one edge.
>- The length $x$ is the part of the gap still empty. The slab fills the other $L - x$. Many texts measure the inserted length instead, which is $L - x$ here.
>
>Both parts sit between the same two plates, so they have the same potential difference. They act as two capacitors in [[3.5.2. Equivalent Capacitance|parallel]]. With $C_0 = \varepsilon_0 L^2/d$ the capacitance of the empty gap,
>$$C(x) = \frac{\varepsilon_0 L x}{d} + \frac{K\varepsilon_0 L(L - x)}{d} = C_0\left[K - (K-1)\frac{x}{L}\right].$$
>The stored energy at fixed charge is $U(x) = Q^2/(2C(x))$. The force along $x$ is $F_x = -dU/dx$:
>$$F_x = -\frac{Q^2}{2C_0 L}\,\frac{K-1}{\left[K - (K-1)x/L\right]^2}.$$
>For $K > 1$ the force is negative. It points toward smaller $x$, so the slab is pulled in. Its size is largest as the slab starts to enter, at $x = L$. It is smallest when the slab fills the gap, at $x = 0$, smaller by the factor $K^2$.
>
>The form $Q^2/(2C)$ is the one to differentiate, because its only changing quantity is $C$. In $\tfrac12 CV^2$ and $\tfrac12 QV$, the potential difference $V$ also changes with $x$.

# Intuition

The plates' field polarizes the slab. Near the edge of the plates the field bulges outward and reaches the part of the slab still outside. The face of the slab near the positive plate carries negative induced charge, close to the plate's positive charge. The same holds with signs reversed at the other plate. These attractions have a component that points into the gap. As in [[3.2.6. Electric Dipole]], the slab's dipoles sit in a nonuniform field and move toward the stronger field inside.

A direct calculation would need the exact field at the edge, which is hard to find. The energy method avoids it. Moving the slab a small distance changes $C$, which changes $U$. The change in $U$ per unit distance gives the force. At fixed charge, the slab moves in the direction that lowers $U$, which is the direction that raises $C$.

The slab from the examples slides into an isolated capacitor, and $x$ is the empty length. The graph traces $U = Q^2/(2C(x))$, and the green tangent gives its slope. The force arrow shrinks as the slab goes in.

# Derivation

The top plate is one conductor at one potential, and the bottom plate is another. The empty part and the filled part therefore have the same potential difference. Capacitors with the same potential difference are in parallel, and their capacitances add. The bent field at the edges is ignored, as in [[3.5.1a Parallel-Plate Capacitor]].

The empty part has area $Lx$ and capacitance $\varepsilon_0 Lx/d$. The filled part has area $L(L - x)$ and capacitance $K\varepsilon_0 L(L-x)/d$, from [[3.5.4. Dielectrics]]. Adding them gives
$$C(x) = \frac{\varepsilon_0 L}{d}\big[x + K(L - x)\big] = C_0\left[K - (K - 1)\frac{x}{L}\right],$$
with $C_0 = \varepsilon_0 L^2/d$. The ends check out. At $x = L$ the gap is empty and $C = C_0$. At $x = 0$ it is full and $C = KC_0$.

The charge is fixed, so the stored energy is
$$U(x) = \frac{Q^2}{2C(x)} = \frac{Q^2}{2C_0}\,\frac{1}{K - (K-1)x/L}.$$
Only $C(x)$ depends on $x$. The derivative of $1/f$ is $-f'/f^2$, and here $f = K - (K-1)x/L$ has $f' = -(K-1)/L$. So
$$\frac{dU}{dx} = \frac{Q^2}{2C_0}\,\frac{(K-1)/L}{\left[K - (K-1)x/L\right]^2}.$$
The force is $F_x = -dU/dx$, as in [[1.3d.1 Potential Energy]], which gives the result in the callout. Since $V = Q/C$, the size of the force can also be written $|F| = \tfrac12 V^2\,|dC/dx|$. Here $V$ is the potential difference at that moment, and $|dC/dx| = (K-1)C_0/L$.

# Properties

#### *(Strongest at the Entrance)* The pull is largest when the slab first enters, because the potential difference is then largest.

From $|F| = \tfrac12 V^2\,|dC/dx|$, the change in $C$ per unit length is the same at every position. At fixed $Q$, $V = Q/C$ is largest when $C$ is smallest, with the gap empty. As the slab goes in, $C$ rises, $V$ falls, and the pull weakens. At full insertion $V$ is smaller by $K$, so the force is smaller by $K^2$. To find where the force is largest, compare sizes. The minus sign gives only the direction.

#### *(The Wrong Energy Form Flips the Sign)* Differentiating $\tfrac12 CV^2$ with $V$ held constant gives a force of the right size in the wrong direction.

With $V$ treated as constant, $-d(\tfrac12 CV^2)/dx = -\tfrac12 V^2\,dC/dx$. Here $dC/dx < 0$, so this expression is positive and would push the slab out. The error is treating $V$ as constant. At fixed $Q$, $V = Q/C$ changes with $x$. Written as $Q^2/(2C)$, the energy has only one changing factor, and its derivative involves only $dC/dx$. Treating $V$ as constant in $\tfrac12 QV$ gives zero force, which is also wrong.

#### *(Why Ignoring the Edge Works)* The bent field at the edge exerts the pull, yet $C(x)$ without edge effects still gives the correct force.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture used $C(x)$ without the edge field and did not explain why the force still comes out right. The argument below follows from the energy method.

In the middle of the gap, the field points straight across from plate to plate. It pushes the slab's induced charges toward the plates and has no component along $x$. The pull along $x$ therefore comes only from the bent field near the plates' edge. The energy method does not need that field. While the slab is partly inside, the region near the plates' edge looks the same at every position. Its stored energy is therefore a constant. At the slab's front face inside the gap, the field is $V/d$ on both sides. That boundary adds no extra energy. Moving the slab by $dx$ changes only a strip of length $dx$ in the uniform region, from empty to filled. The formula for $C(x)$ describes that region exactly, so $dU/dx$ is exact. Only $U$ itself is off, by the constant edge energy.

#### *(Oscillation About Full Insertion)* Without friction, the slab overshoots full insertion and oscillates, and the motion is not simple harmonic.

A slab of length $L$ released at the entrance has its largest speed at full insertion. It then starts to leave through the far edge. An empty length now opens on the near side, and the same pull acts back toward the center. With $|x|$ in place of $x$, the formula above still applies. Near full insertion the force has nearly constant size and flips direction at the center. A simple harmonic force grows in proportion to the displacement, as in [[1.6.1. Simple Harmonic Motion]]. The slab therefore oscillates with a period that depends on its amplitude.

#### *(Supply Still Connected)* With the supply connected, the slab is still pulled in, with a force of constant size $\tfrac12 V^2 (K-1)C_0/L$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture analyzed only the disconnected case. The result follows from energy conservation with the supply's work included.

Now $V$ stays fixed and $Q$ changes. Let the slab move inward so that $C$ rises by $dC$. The supply moves charge $dQ = V\,dC$ and does work $V\,dQ = V^2\,dC$. The capacitor's energy $\tfrac12 CV^2$ rises by $\tfrac12 V^2\,dC$. The remaining $\tfrac12 V^2\,dC$ is the work done on the slab. The inward force therefore has size $\tfrac12 V^2\,|dC/dx|$, the same formula as before. Its size no longer changes with position, since $V$ is fixed. The glycerin classroom demonstration in [[3.5.4. Dielectrics]] kept its supply connected. Using $F_x = -dU/dx$ with the capacitor's energy alone here would give the wrong direction, because it leaves out the supply's work.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Capacitance With the Slab Halfway In
>Square plates $10\ \text{cm}$ on a side sit $1.0\ \text{mm}$ apart. A slab with $K = 2.5$ and thickness $1.0\ \text{mm}$ fills half the gap.
>
>>[!example]- Find the capacitance.
>>The empty capacitance is
>>$$C_0 = \frac{\varepsilon_0 L^2}{d} = \frac{(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(0.10)^2}{1.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 88.5\ \text{pF}.$$
>>With $x = L/2$, the bracket is $K - (K-1)/2 = (K+1)/2 = 1.75$. So $C = (1.75)(88.5\ \text{pF}) = 155\ \text{pF}$. As a check, the empty half gives $44.3\ \text{pF}$ and the filled half gives $2.5 \times 44.3 = 111\ \text{pF}$.

>[!example] (Medium) Force at the Two Ends
>The capacitor from the Easy example carries $\pm 0.10\ \mu\text{C}$ and is disconnected from its supply.
>
>>[!example]- Find the size of the force on the slab as it starts to enter and when it fills the gap.
>>As the slab starts to enter, the bracket equals $1$, so
>>$$|F| = \frac{Q^2(K-1)}{2C_0 L} = \frac{(0.10 \times 10^{-6})^2(1.5)}{2(88.5 \times 10^{-12})(0.10)} = 8.47 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{N}.$$
>>When the slab fills the gap, the bracket equals $K$, so the force is smaller by $K^2 = 6.25$. It drops to $1.36 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{N}$. Both forces point into the gap. With the gap empty, $V = Q/C_0 = 1130\ \text{V}$ and $E = 1.13 \times 10^{6}\ \text{V/m}$. That field stays below the breakdown field of air from [[3.5.4a Dielectric Breakdown]].

>[!example] (Hard) Speed of the Slab
>The slab from the Medium example has mass $12\ \text{g}$ and slides without friction. It is released from rest at the entrance.
>
>>[!example]- Find its speed when it fills the gap.
>>Energy is conserved, so the slab's kinetic energy equals the drop in stored energy:
>>$$\tfrac12 mv^2 = U(L) - U(0) = \frac{Q^2}{2C_0} - \frac{Q^2}{2KC_0} = \frac{Q^2}{2C_0}\left(1 - \frac{1}{K}\right).$$
>>Here $Q^2/(2C_0) = (0.10 \times 10^{-6})^2/(2 \times 88.5 \times 10^{-12}) = 5.65 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{J}$. The factor $1 - 1/K$ is $0.60$, so the drop is $3.39 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{J}$. The speed is
>>$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2(3.39 \times 10^{-5})}{0.012}} = 0.075\ \text{m/s}.$$
>>Integrating the force from $x = L$ to $x = 0$ gives the same $3.39 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{J}$. The slab then passes full insertion and oscillates about it.
