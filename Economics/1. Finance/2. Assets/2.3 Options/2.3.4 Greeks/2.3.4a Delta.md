>[!note] Delta
>**Delta** is the rate at which an option's value changes with the price of the underlying stock, $\Delta = \partial f/\partial S$. Under the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|BSM model]], with dividend yield $q$ and time to expiry $\tau = T - t$, European options have closed forms.
>- A call has $\Delta_c = e^{-q\tau}N(d_1)$, which lies between $0$ and $e^{-q\tau}$.
>- A put has $\Delta_p = -e^{-q\tau}N(-d_1)$, which lies between $-e^{-q\tau}$ and $0$.
>
>Here $d_1 = [\ln(S/K) + (r - q + \sigma^2/2)\tau]/(\sigma\sqrt{\tau})$, $d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau}$, and $N$ is the standard normal CDF. The pricing formula splits each option into $\Delta$ shares and cash held in zero-coupon bonds that pay at $T$:
>$$c = \Delta_c S - B_c, \quad B_c = Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2), \qquad p = \Delta_p S + B_p, \quad B_p = Ke^{-r\tau}N(-d_2).$$
>The call's replicating portfolio borrows $B_c$, and the put's lends $B_p$. Each cash amount is the present value of the strike times the risk-neutral probability that the option ends in the money.
>
>The holder of an option $f$ hedges it by selling $m$ shares, which forms the portfolio $\Pi = f - mS$. The choice $m = \Delta$ makes $\partial\Pi/\partial S = 0$, and such a portfolio is **delta neutral**. Its value does not change to first order when the stock moves a small amount. The neutrality holds only at the current $S$ and $t$, so the hedge must be reset as they change. Delta is the continuous-time limit of the tree hedge ratio in [[2.3.3d Delta Hedging]]. Its rate of change in $S$ is gamma ([[2.3.4b Gamma and Theta]]).

>[!warning] Sign of the Cash Leg
>The lecture writes $c = \Delta S + B^c_t$ with $B^c_t = -Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2)$, a negative cash position. This note writes the loan as the positive amount $B_c$, so $c = \Delta_c S - B_c$. The put's cash $B_p$ is the same in both.

# Intuition

A dealer who sells a call loses money when the stock rises. To offset small moves, the dealer needs the call's price change per \$1 move in the stock. That rate is the slope of the price curve at today's stock price. Delta is that slope. Holding $\Delta$ shares per call cancels the first-order effect of any small move. This use gives delta its other name, the hedge ratio.

The tangent line at today's price is itself the replicating portfolio. A portfolio of $\Delta$ shares and a fixed bond position is worth $\Delta S - B$, a straight line in $S$. Its slope is the number of shares. Its height at $S = 0$ is the bond position, since the shares are worth nothing there.

# Properties

#### *(Dividend Cap)* A deep in-the-money call has a delta near $e^{-q\tau}$, which is below one.

Deep in the money, $N(d_1)$ is close to $1$ and the call behaves like a long forward with delivery price $K$. A forward on a stock with yield $q$ is worth $Se^{-q\tau} - Ke^{-r\tau}$. Its hedge is $e^{-q\tau}$ shares, because reinvesting the dividends in more shares grows the holding to exactly one share at $T$. A one-year call on a stock with $q = 4\%$ therefore starts with a delta of at most $0.96$. Differentiating [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity|put-call parity]], $c - p = Se^{-q\tau} - Ke^{-r\tau}$, in $S$ gives
$$\Delta_c - \Delta_p = e^{-q\tau}.$$
So a long call plus a short put with the same strike and expiry has the fixed delta $e^{-q\tau}$. Its hedge needs no trades beyond reinvesting the dividends.

#### *(Time to Expiry)* As expiry nears, the call's delta turns from a gentle S-curve into a step at the strike.

Write $d_1$ as the sum of two terms,
$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K)}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} + \frac{(r - q + \sigma^2/2)\sqrt{\tau}}{\sigma}.$$
The first term measures moneyness in units of $\sigma\sqrt{\tau}$, and the second is a small drift term. As $\tau \to 0$ the first term dominates. For $S > K$, $d_1 \to +\infty$ and $\Delta_c \to 1$. For $S < K$, $d_1 \to -\infty$ and $\Delta_c \to 0$. At $S = K$ only the second term remains, and it tends to $0$, so $\Delta_c \to \frac{1}{2}$. With more time left, the first term shrinks, so every call's delta sits nearer the middle. An in-the-money call's delta falls as $\tau$ grows, and an out-of-the-money call's delta rises. The animation shows the call's delta for $K = \$100$, $r = 5\%$, $q = 2\%$, and $\sigma = 30\%$ as $\tau$ shrinks from one year to two days. The gray curves are the earlier frames.

Near expiry a hedger must trade many shares for small moves near the strike. With two days left, the call's delta goes from $0.17$ at \$98 to $0.83$ at \$102. With a full year left, the same \$4 range moves the delta only from $0.56$ to $0.61$.

#### *(Not the Exercise Probability)* A call's delta exceeds the risk-neutral probability that the call ends in the money.

The cash leg contains $N(d_2)$, the [[2.3.3b Risk-Neutral Valuation|risk-neutral]] probability of exercise. Delta contains $N(d_1)$, and $d_1 = d_2 + \sigma\sqrt{\tau}$. So $N(d_1) > N(d_2)$, and the gap widens with $\sigma\sqrt{\tau}$. Take an at-the-money one-year call with $\sigma = 50\%$, $r = 4\%$, and $q = 1\%$. Then $N(d_1) = 0.6217$ and $\Delta_c = 0.6155$, while $N(d_2) = 0.4247$. Traders often read delta as a rough chance of exercise. That reading is close only when $\sigma\sqrt{\tau}$ is small, as for short-dated options. The hedge holds extra shares because the payoff $S_T - K$ grows with $S_T$ in the states where the call is exercised.

#### *(Rebalancing Triggers)* A delta-neutral hedge drifts when the stock moves, when time passes, and when volatility changes.

The condition $\partial\Pi/\partial S = 0$ holds at one stock price, one date, and one volatility. Each of the three inputs moves delta.
- A stock move $dS$ changes delta by about $\Gamma\,dS$, where $\Gamma = \partial\Delta/\partial S$. The fixed hedge then misses the option's change by about $\frac{1}{2}\Gamma(dS)^2$.
- Time passing moves delta even when $S$ stays the same. An out-of-the-money call loses delta overnight, and an in-the-money call gains it. This drift, $\partial\Delta/\partial t$, is called charm.
- A change in volatility changes $d_1$ and so delta. A rise in $\sigma$ raises a call's delta when $d_2 < 0$ and lowers it when $d_2 > 0$. This sensitivity, $\partial\Delta/\partial\sigma$, is called vanna.

A desk rebalances at discrete times, so it carries all three drifts between trades. [[2.3.3d Delta Hedging]] shows on a tree that each rebalancing is self-financing.

#### *(Tree Limit)* The hedge ratio of a binomial tree converges to the BSM delta as the steps shrink.

In a [[2.3.3c Binomial Tree|binomial tree]], the hedge at the root uses the difference quotient $(f_u - f_d)/(uS - dS)$ over the first step. The BSM delta is its limit as the step length goes to zero. For the call in the (Easy) example below, CRR trees with $2$, $10$, $50$, and $200$ steps give $0.6425$, $0.6524$, $0.6539$, and $0.6533$. The formula gives $0.6532$. The convergence is not monotone, as for the tree price itself. A desk that prices on a tree reads delta from these same node values at no extra cost.

# Derivation

We want $\Delta_c = e^{-q\tau}N(d_1)$, the put's delta, and the split into shares and cash. The call price is $c = Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1) - Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2)$.

First we need an identity for the normal density $n(x) = e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$. The ratio of the densities at $d_2$ and $d_1$ is $n(d_2)/n(d_1) = e^{(d_1^2 - d_2^2)/2}$. Since $d_1 - d_2 = \sigma\sqrt{\tau}$ and $d_1 + d_2 = 2d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau}$, the exponent equals $\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\,d_1 - \sigma^2\tau/2 = \ln(S/K) + (r - q)\tau$. Exponentiating and rearranging gives
$$Se^{-q\tau}n(d_1) = Ke^{-r\tau}n(d_2).$$
This identity makes the two density terms cancel in the next step.

Next, only $\ln S$ inside $d_1$ and $d_2$ depends on $S$, so $\partial d_1/\partial S = \partial d_2/\partial S = 1/(S\sigma\sqrt{\tau})$. The product rule applied to $c$ gives
$$\frac{\partial c}{\partial S} = e^{-q\tau}N(d_1) + \Big[Se^{-q\tau}n(d_1) - Ke^{-r\tau}n(d_2)\Big]\frac{1}{S\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}.$$
The bracket is zero by the identity, so $\Delta_c = e^{-q\tau}N(d_1)$.

For the put, differentiate parity $p = c - Se^{-q\tau} + Ke^{-r\tau}$ in $S$. This gives $\Delta_p = e^{-q\tau}N(d_1) - e^{-q\tau} = -e^{-q\tau}N(-d_1)$, using $1 - N(x) = N(-x)$.

Substituting $\Delta_c$ into the call formula gives $c = \Delta_c S - Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2) = \Delta_c S - B_c$. The put formula $p = -Se^{-q\tau}N(-d_1) + Ke^{-r\tau}N(-d_2)$ reads $p = \Delta_p S + B_p$ in the same way. By [[2.3.3e Black-Scholes-Merton Equation]], holding $\Delta$ shares and cash $f - \Delta S$, reset continuously, is self-financing and replicates the option. Finally, the hedged portfolio $\Pi = f - mS$ has $\partial\Pi/\partial S = \Delta - m$, which is zero for $m = \Delta$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Replicating a Call and a Put
>A stock trades at \$37 and has a dividend yield of $2\%$ per year. European options on it have strike \$35 and expire in three months. The volatility is $40\%$ and the risk-free rate is $4\%$, both with continuous compounding. A trader wants to copy one contract of each, $100$ options, with shares and a bond.
>
>>[!example]- Find each option's delta and bond position, and the trades that replicate 100 calls and 100 puts.
>>Here $\tau = 0.25$ and $\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = 0.20$. So $d_1 = [\ln(37/35) + (0.04 - 0.02 + 0.08)(0.25)]/0.20 = (0.05557 + 0.02500)/0.20 = 0.4028$, and $d_2 = 0.2028$. A normal table gives $N(d_1) = 0.6565$ and $N(d_2) = 0.5804$. The discount factor for dividends is $e^{-q\tau} = 0.99501$, and the discounted strike is $Ke^{-r\tau} = \$34.652$.
>>
>>For the call, $\Delta_c = 0.99501 \times 0.6565 = 0.6532$ and $B_c = 34.652 \times 0.5804 = \$20.11$. The call is worth $0.6532 \times 37 - 20.11 = 24.17 - 20.11 = \$4.06$. To replicate $100$ calls, the trader buys $65.32$ shares and borrows \$2,011.10.
>>
>>For the put, $\Delta_p = -0.99501 \times 0.3435 = -0.3418$ and $B_p = 34.652 \times 0.4196 = \$14.54$. The put is worth $-0.3418 \times 37 + 14.54 = \$1.89$. To replicate $100$ puts, the trader shorts $34.18$ shares and lends \$1,454.08.
>>
>>Two checks follow from parity. The deltas differ by $0.6532 + 0.3418 = 0.9950 = e^{-q\tau}$. The cash amounts add to $20.11 + 14.54 = 34.65$, the discounted strike, because $N(d_2) + N(-d_2) = 1$.

>[!example] (Medium) One Day of Hedging a Written Put
>A dealer writes $50$ put contracts, $5{,}000$ puts, on a stock at \$120 with a dividend yield of $1\%$. The puts have strike \$115 and expire in $0.3$ years. The volatility is $28\%$ and the risk-free rate is $4.5\%$, both with continuous compounding. The dealer delta hedges at the close. The next day the stock closes at \$117.50.
>
>>[!example]- Find the initial hedge, the hedged and unhedged results over the day, and the trade that restores delta neutrality.
>>At the start each put is worth \$4.4537 and has $\Delta_p = -0.335266$. The dealer is short the puts, so the position's delta is $-5{,}000 \times (-0.335266) = +1{,}676.33$ shares. The hedge is a short sale of $1{,}676.33$ shares. The premium and the short sale bring in $22{,}268.59 + 201{,}159.34 = \$223{,}427.92$ of cash.
>>
>>One day later, $\tau = 0.3 - 1/365$, the put is worth \$5.3287, and $\Delta_p = -0.386534$. Each put has risen by \$0.8750, so the dealer loses about \$4,375 on the puts. The short shares gain $1{,}676.33 \times 2.50 = \$4{,}190.82$. Interest on the cash adds $223{,}427.92\,(e^{0.045/365} - 1) = \$27.55$. The dividend owed on the borrowed shares costs about $0.01 \times 201{,}159.34/365 = \$5.51$. The hedged result is a loss of about \$162, against \$4,375 without the hedge.
>>
>>The remaining loss comes from the curvature of the put price. The put's gamma is $0.01977$, so the move costs about $\frac{1}{2} \times 0.01977 \times 2.5^2 \times 5{,}000 = \$309$. One day of time decay earns the dealer about \$131, which offsets part of it ([[2.3.4b Gamma and Theta]]).
>>
>>To restore neutrality, the dealer needs $5{,}000 \times 0.386534 = 1{,}932.67$ short shares. The dealer sells $256.34$ more shares short at \$117.50. A put writer sells after the stock falls, as on the tree in [[2.3.3d Delta Hedging]].

>[!example] (Hard) Delta Near Expiry
>A desk is short $1{,}000$ European calls with strike \$200 on a stock at \$200. The stock has a dividend yield of $1.5\%$ and a volatility of $25\%$. The risk-free rate is $4\%$, and all rates are continuously compounded. The desk compares two expiries, six months and one week ($\tau = 1/52$). During one day the stock rises to \$204 and then returns to \$200. Ignore the passage of time within the day.
>
>>[!example]- For each expiry, find the delta at \$200 and \$204, the shares the desk trades, and its hedging loss. Then explain the difference, and find the limit of delta at $S = K$ as $\tau \to 0$.
>>For six months, $\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = 0.17678$, $e^{-q\tau} = 0.99253$, and $(r - q + \sigma^2/2)\tau = 0.028125$. At \$200 the logarithm is zero, so $d_1 = 0.028125/0.17678 = 0.1591$ and $\Delta = 0.99253 \times 0.5632 = 0.5590$. At \$204, $\ln(204/200) = 0.019803$ and $d_1 = 0.047928/0.17678 = 0.2711$, so $\Delta = 0.99253 \times 0.6069 = 0.6023$. The desk buys $1{,}000 \times (0.6023 - 0.5590) = 43.32$ shares at \$204 and sells them at \$200. It loses $43.32 \times 4 = \$173.28$.
>>
>>For one week, $\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = 0.034669$, $e^{-q\tau} = 0.99971$, and $(r - q + \sigma^2/2)\tau = 0.001082$. At \$200, $d_1 = 0.0312$ and $\Delta = 0.5123$. At \$204, $d_1 = 0.020885/0.034669 = 0.6024$ and $\Delta = 0.7263$. The desk buys $214.04$ shares at \$204 and sells them at \$200, which loses \$856.15. That is about five times the loss at six months. The short calls end where they started, so these trades are the whole result.
>>
>>The moneyness term $\ln(S/K)/(\sigma\sqrt{\tau})$ explains the ratio. A \$4 move shifts $d_1$ by $0.019803/0.034669 = 0.57$ with one week left. It shifts $d_1$ by only $0.019803/0.17678 = 0.11$ with six months left. The ratio of the two shifts is $\sqrt{0.5 \times 52} = 5.1$. Near $d_1 = 0$ the normal CDF is at its steepest, so delta changes by about the same factor.
>>
>>At $S = K$, $d_1 = (r - q + \sigma^2/2)\sqrt{\tau}/\sigma$. It equals $0.1591$ at six months and $0.0312$ at one week, and it tends to $0$ as $\tau \to 0$. So the at-the-money delta tends to $\frac{1}{2}$, while the delta on each side tends to $0$ or $1$. A short-dated at-the-money option needs the most hedge trading per dollar of stock move. The loss on those trades is the cost of being short gamma.
