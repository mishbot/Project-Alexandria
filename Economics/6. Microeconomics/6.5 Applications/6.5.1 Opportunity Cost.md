>[!note] Opportunity Cost
>The **opportunity cost** of a choice is the value of the best alternative that the decision maker gives up by making it. It includes alternatives that involve no payment. Examples are the wages an hour of free time could have earned and the rent an owned building could have collected. The **accounting cost** of a choice counts only the payments it requires. Consumers and firms that maximize utility or profit compare options by their opportunity costs. An owned resource with a valuable alternative use is costly to keep, even though keeping it requires no payment.
>
>The central application in consumer theory is the price of time. A person who can work as many hours as she likes at the hourly wage $w$ gives up $w$ of earnings for each hour of leisure. Leisure is therefore a good with price $w$. In [[6.5.2 Labor Supply]] this gives the budget constraint
>$$pc + wl \le M + 24w,$$
>where $c$ is consumption at price $p$, $l$ is hours of leisure per day, and $M$ is income from sources other than work. The slope $-p_1/p_2$ of an ordinary [[6.2.1 Budget Constraint|budget line]] has the same reading. One more unit of good $1$ uses money that would have bought $p_1/p_2$ units of good $2$.
>
>Opportunity cost can rise while accounting cost stays fixed. A firm that owns its land pays no rent. When land prices rise, its accounting cost of staying does not change. Its opportunity cost of staying rises, because it could sell or lease the land for more and move to a cheaper site. The firm should move once the lease value of its land exceeds the profit advantage of its current site over the best cheaper site.

# Intuition

Every resource a person or firm controls has more than one use, and using it one way rules out the others. The money paid, if any, is only part of this loss. An evening of TV requires no payment, yet a student with a campus job gives up the wages of an evening shift to watch it. Economists count the value of the lost uses as a cost, whether or not money changes hands.

Accounting records can give a misleading picture of a choice. Accounting records show what was paid. A decision depends on what else could be done with the same resources. A firm that owns its site sees no rent in its accounts. It still faces the same choice as a firm that rents. It can keep using the site, or it can let someone else use it for a price.

# Properties

#### *(Only the Best Alternative)* The opportunity cost counts the single best option given up, and never adds up several options.

A resource can serve only one alternative use at a time. Suppose a free evening could go to a campus job paying $\$50$ or to tutoring paying $\$70$. Watching TV instead costs $\$70$, because the student could have done at most one of the two. Adding the two wages would count the same evening twice. The best alternative also depends on what is available. If the tutoring client cancels, the opportunity cost of the evening falls to $\$50$.

#### *(Every Price)* Every price in consumer theory is an opportunity cost.

In [[6.5.2 Labor Supply]], one more hour of leisure costs $w/p$ units of consumption, the slope of that budget line. In [[6.5.3 Intertemporal Choice]], one more unit of consumption when young costs $1 + r$ units when old, because the unit could have been saved at the interest rate $r$. In [[6.5.4 Taste-Based Discrimination]], a worker's reservation wage $\bar u$ is the opportunity cost of taking a job, the pay of her best outside option. At the optimum of [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]], the consumer's own rate of trade between two goods equals one of these ratios.

#### *(Price of Leisure Needs Free Hours)* The wage prices an hour of leisure only if the person can trade hours for pay one at a time.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Income taxes, overtime pay, and fixed-hours contracts were not part of the labor supply model in lecture. They show where the rule "leisure costs $w$" needs care.

The argument that leisure costs $w$ per hour assumes the person chooses her hours at the margin. A worker on a fixed 40-hour contract cannot turn an extra free hour into pay. For her, the wage does not price the marginal hour of leisure. The relevant wage is also the one the person keeps. If income tax takes a share $\tau$ of earnings, an hour of leisure costs $(1-\tau)w$. If the pay rate changes with hours, as with overtime, the price of leisure is the after-tax pay of the last hour worked.

#### *(Owning Versus Renting)* A firm that owns an asset and a firm that rents the same asset face the same decision.

A renting firm sees the market value of its site in its rent bill. When land prices triple, its rent rises and its accounts show the change. An owning firm pays no rent, but it can lease the site to another user at the same market rent. Keeping the site costs the owner that forgone rent every year. Both firms should stay only if staying earns more than moving once the market rent of the current site is counted as a cost. The owner reports a higher accounting profit than the renter. This difference does not change which choice is best.

#### *(Past Prices Do Not Matter)* The price paid for a resource in the past does not enter its opportunity cost.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Sunk costs are not covered in lecture. Textbooks usually state this property together with the opportunity-cost principle.

A **sunk cost** is a payment already made that no current choice can recover. A firm paid some price for its land decades ago. That payment is the same whether the firm stays or moves, so it cannot affect which option is better. Only the current alternatives count, namely the rent or sale price the land commands today and the cost of the best other site.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Night Out
>A student has a free Friday evening. She could work a four-hour shift at a campus café for $\$16$ per hour, or tutor a high school student for four hours for a flat $\$80$. She goes to a concert instead. The ticket costs $\$45$.
>
>>[!example]- What is the opportunity cost of the concert?
>>The concert uses two resources, money and time. The $\$45$ ticket could have bought other goods, so it counts in full. The evening has two alternative uses, but the student could do only one of them. The café shift pays $4 \times \$16 = \$64$ and tutoring pays $\$80$, so the best alternative use of the time is worth $\$80$. The opportunity cost of the concert is $\$45 + \$80 = \$125$. Adding the café wages as well would count the same four hours twice. The accounting cost is only the $\$45$ ticket. The student should go if the concert is worth more than $\$125$ to her.

>[!example] (Medium) The Warehouse
>A warehouse retailer owns its site in San Francisco's SoMa district outright, so it pays no rent. At this site the warehouse earns an operating profit of $\$10$ million per year. The best alternative site is in a cheaper area. Leasing it costs $\$0.5$ million per year, and operating profit there would be $\$8.5$ million per year because trucks travel farther to customers. Ten years ago another business would have leased the SoMa site for $\$1$ million per year. Since then land prices in SoMa have tripled, and the site now leases for $\$3$ million per year.
>
>>[!example]- Should the retailer have stayed in SoMa ten years ago, and should it stay today? What happened to the accounting cost and to the opportunity cost of staying?
>>All numbers are per year, so they can be compared directly. If the retailer stays, its profit is $\$10$ million in both years. It pays no rent, so the accounting cost of the site is zero in both years.
>>
>>If the retailer moves, it earns $\$8.5$ million at the new site, pays $\$0.5$ million in rent there, and leases out the SoMa site. Ten years ago this gave $8.5 - 0.5 + 1 = 9$ million dollars, less than the $\$10$ million from staying. Today it gives $8.5 - 0.5 + 3 = 11$ million dollars, more than staying. The retailer was right to stay then and should move now.
>>
>>The accounting cost of staying did not change. The opportunity cost of staying, the lease income the retailer gives up, rose from $\$1$ million to $\$3$ million. The SoMa site's advantage over the alternative is $10 - (8.5 - 0.5) = 2$ million dollars per year. The retailer should stay only while the SoMa lease value is below this $\$2$ million. The tripling of land prices pushed the lease value above it.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The example below uses overtime pay and an income tax, which were not in the lecture's labor supply model.

>[!example] (Hard) The Price of an Hour
>A student works at a job where she chooses her own hours. The job pays $\$20$ per hour for the first 6 hours of a day and $\$30$ per hour for each hour beyond 6. Income tax takes $20\%$ of all earnings. She spends her income on a consumption good with price $p$.
>
>>[!example]- What does one more hour of leisure cost her if she currently works 8 hours a day? What if she works 4 hours?
>>One more hour of leisure means one less hour of work, so its cost is the after-tax pay of the last hour worked. At 8 hours, the last hour is an overtime hour paid $\$30$. She keeps $80\%$ of it, so an hour of leisure costs $0.8 \times 30 = \$24$. At 4 hours, the last hour pays $\$20$, and an hour of leisure costs $0.8 \times 20 = \$16$.
>>
>>The price of leisure therefore depends on where she is. Her budget line in the $(l, c)$ plane of [[6.5.2 Labor Supply]] has two segments. Its slope is $-24/p$ when she has fewer than 18 hours of leisure and $-16/p$ between 18 and 24 hours. The pre-tax wages of $\$20$ and $\$30$ overstate the price of leisure, because $20\%$ of them never reaches her.
