> [!note] Chain Rule
> For a finite number of events $A_1, ..., A_N \subseteq \Omega$ in the same probability space, the chain rule states that the probability of all $N$ events occurring is equal to the product of the [[1.3 Conditional Probability|conditional probabilities]] of each event given the events that came before it, such that
> $$\mathbb{P}[\cap_{i = 1}^N A_i]=\mathbb{P}[A_1] \times \mathbb{P}[A_2 \mid A_1] \times \mathbb{P}[A_3 \mid A_1 \cap A_2] \times ... \times \mathbb{P}[A_N \mid \cap_{i = 1}^{N-1}A_i].$$
# Intuition. 
For two events, the chain rule is simply
$$\mathbb{P}[A \cap B]=\mathbb{P}[A] \times \mathbb{P}[B \mid A] = \mathbb{P}[B] \times \mathbb{P}[A \mid B],$$
which looks very similar to a reordering of the conditional probability theorem. We can say that the probability of both $A$ and $B$ happening is equal to the scaled probability of $B$ happening given that $A$ has already happened.  Let's expand our use case for 3 events using :
$$\begin{align}
\mathbb{P}[A \cap B \cap C] &= \mathbb{P}[(A \cap B) \cap C]  \\
&= \mathbb{P}[A \cap B] \times \mathbb{P}[C \mid A \cap B] \\
&= \mathbb{P}[A] \times \mathbb{P}[B \mid A] \times \mathbb{P}[C \mid A \cap B].
\end{align}$$
This comes from the fact that sets are associative (as seen in the first line of the equation). Therefore, we can implicitly substitute $D = A \cap B$, and first solve for the two-event scenario.
