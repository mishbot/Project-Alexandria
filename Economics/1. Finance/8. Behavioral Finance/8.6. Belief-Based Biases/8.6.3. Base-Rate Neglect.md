>[!note] Base-Rate Neglect
>**Base-rate neglect** is the systematic tendency to under-weight prior probabilities (base rates) when updating beliefs from new evidence. In the Rabin (1998) parametric template
>$$\frac{\pi(A \mid S)}{\pi(B \mid S)} = \left(\frac{p(S \mid A)}{p(S \mid B)}\right)^c \left(\frac{p(A)}{p(B)}\right)^d,$$
>base-rate neglect is the case $d < 1$ — the prior odds enter the posterior with less than full Bayesian weight. Bayes' rule is $c = d = 1$. Base-rate neglect is the *parametric expression* of the [[8.6.5. Representativeness Heuristic|representativeness heuristic]]: when an investor judges probabilities by how well the evidence resembles a stereotype, the base rate falls out of her calculation, which is exactly what $d < 1$ encodes.
>
>Base-rate neglect is the cognitive mirror image of [[8.6.1. Conservatism|conservatism]] ($c < 1$). The two biases pull in opposite directions, and both have empirical support. Griffin & Tversky (1992) reconcile them: conservatism dominates when the signal is weak but its generating process is well understood (urn experiments), while base-rate neglect dominates when the signal feels diagnostic but the base rate is abstract (the engineer-vs-lawyer problem). The two operate in different parts of the information environment and are not mutually exclusive.

# Intuition

Bayes' rule says posterior odds equal prior odds times the likelihood ratio. If your prior is that a disease is rare ($1$ in $10{,}000$) and a test with $99\%$ accuracy comes back positive, the posterior probability of having the disease is still less than $1\%$ — because the rarity of the disease swamps the test's diagnostic power. The Bayesian answer is counterintuitive precisely because it requires the prior to dominate the likelihood. Base-rate neglect is what happens when intuition fights this calculation and loses: the diagnostic-feeling test result feels more compelling than the abstract-feeling base rate, and the prior gets discounted.

The bias is robust across populations. Trained physicians, when asked the disease-test problem with realistic numbers, produce posteriors that are too high by an order of magnitude. Trained analysts, when asked which of two firms is more likely to be a "growth firm" given a description, ignore the actual fraction of growth firms in the universe. The cognitive habit of judging by resemblance — representativeness — is the underlying source.

# Properties

#### (*Parametric Form*) Base-rate neglect is $d < 1$ in the Rabin template.

In the Rabin (1998) deviation from Bayes' rule, $d$ governs the weight on the prior odds. Bayes is $d = 1$. Base-rate neglect is $d < 1$, with empirically estimated values typically between $0.5$ and $0.9$ depending on the domain. The parameter $c$ on the signal is left at $1$, meaning the base-rate-neglecting investor still uses the new evidence correctly — she just under-weights the *prior* relative to it.

#### (*Mirror Image of Conservatism*) The two biases pull in opposite directions.

Conservatism ($c < 1$) under-weights the new signal. Base-rate neglect ($d < 1$) under-weights the prior. A pure conservative agent moves her posterior less than Bayes prescribes; a pure base-rate-neglecting agent moves it more. The two biases were initially seen as contradictory — Edwards (1968) documented conservatism, and Kahneman & Tversky (1973) documented base-rate neglect within five years. Griffin & Tversky (1992) reconciled them by noting that they apply in different settings: conservatism when the signal is weak and the process is transparent, base-rate neglect when the signal is vivid and the base rate is abstract.

#### (*Cognitive Source*) Representativeness operationalizes base-rate neglect.

Why does $d$ shrink? Because when an investor judges probabilities by *resemblance* — does the description fit the engineer stereotype? — the base rate is invisible to the heuristic. Resemblance is a feature of the evidence and the hypothesis, not of the population. Base-rate neglect is therefore not a separate psychological mechanism from [[8.6.5. Representativeness Heuristic|representativeness]] — it is what representativeness looks like when you express it parametrically.

#### (*Empirical Magnitude*) The engineer-vs-lawyer experiment shows near-total prior neglect.

Kahneman & Tversky (1973) gave subjects a description of "Jack" and asked them to judge whether Jack was an engineer or a lawyer. One group was told the population was $70\%$ engineers; another was told $30\%$. Bayesian subjects should have produced posteriors differing by a factor of $5.4$. Real subjects produced posteriors differing by less than $0.1$ — implied $d$ values close to $0$. The bias is not subtle.

# Examples

>[!example] The disease-test problem
>A disease has prevalence $1/10{,}000$ in the population. A test for it has $99\%$ sensitivity (true-positive rate) and $99\%$ specificity (true-negative rate). A randomly selected person tests positive. What is the probability she has the disease?
>
>Bayesian calculation: $\Pr(\text{disease} \mid +) = \frac{0.99 \cdot 0.0001}{0.99 \cdot 0.0001 + 0.01 \cdot 0.9999} \approx 0.0098$, or about $1\%$. Most respondents — including trained physicians in survey studies — answer above $80\%$. The test's $99\%$ accuracy *feels* dispositive; the $1$-in-$10{,}000$ base rate feels abstract and gets discarded. The error is precisely $d \to 0$ in the Rabin template.

>[!example]- Engineer vs. lawyer (Kahneman-Tversky 1973)
>Group A is told: "We selected one description at random from a pool of $70$ engineers and $30$ lawyers." Group B is told: "We selected one description at random from a pool of $30$ engineers and $70$ lawyers." Both groups read the same description of "Jack" — a $45$-year-old conservative man with hobbies including carpentry and mathematical puzzles. Group A reports a posterior probability of $0.55$ that Jack is an engineer. Group B reports $0.50$. The Bayesian posteriors should be roughly $0.86$ and $0.43$ — a factor of $2$ apart. Subjects' answers differ by $0.05$. The base rate is being almost completely ignored.
