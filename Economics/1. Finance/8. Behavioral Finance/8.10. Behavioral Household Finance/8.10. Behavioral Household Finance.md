>[!note] Behavioral Household Finance
>**Behavioral household finance** studies the *portfolio choice and trading behavior of individual investors and households*, in contrast to the asset-pricing focus of the rest of behavioral finance. The field has grown rapidly in the past two decades because of new microdata sources — the Survey of Consumer Finances, brokerage data (Barber-Odean), Scandinavian administrative records, $401$(k) data, NYSE TAQ — that make individual decisions observable at scale. The empirical agenda is to document and explain a long list of household behaviors that depart systematically from the standard rational-investor benchmark. The patterns fall into five families:
>
>1. **Stock-market non-participation** — many households, including wealthy ones, hold zero equity. See [[8.10.1. Stock Market Non-Participation]].
>2. **Under-diversification** — households who do participate concentrate in domestic, local, familiar, and own-employer stocks. See [[8.10.2. Under-Diversification]].
>3. **The disposition effect** — investors sell winners too readily and hold losers too long, contrary to both the tax-optimal and the momentum-aware benchmark. See [[8.10.3. Disposition Effect]].
>4. **Under-annuitization** — retirees almost never annuitize their wealth, even though Yaari (1965) shows full annuitization is optimal. See [[8.10.4. Under-Annuitization]].
>5. **Liabilities puzzles** — households simultaneously hold high-interest credit-card debt and low-yield liquid assets, fail to refinance mortgages when it would save money, and use high-cost credit. See [[8.10.5. Household Liabilities Puzzles]].
6. **Hyperbolic discounting** — the $(\beta, \delta)$ model of present bias underlying under-saving, procrastination on $401$(k) enrollment, and the success of *Save More Tomorrow*. See [[8.10.6. Hyperbolic Discounting]].
7. **Active fund underperformance** — the average actively-managed equity fund delivers negative after-fee alpha, violating the zero-alpha Berk-Green benchmark; the underperformance is concentrated in broker-sold funds. See [[8.10.7. Active Fund Underperformance]].
>
>**Other documented patterns** that the lecture covers more briefly:
>- *Buying high and selling low.* Dollar-weighted average returns on mutual funds lag time-weighted returns by about $1.5\%$ per year, because investors increase exposure after high returns and decrease it after low returns — the household-side analogue of return [[8.6.7. Extrapolative Beliefs|extrapolation]].
>- *Life-cycle behavior.* The share of risky assets does decline modestly with age (consistent with the rational benchmark) but the *level* of risky-asset holdings is below the benchmark prediction at every age.
>- *Active fund preference.* Households continue to hold actively-managed funds despite their well-documented underperformance net of fees. See [[8.10.7. Active Fund Underperformance]] for the full treatment of the Berk-Green benchmark, the Gruber/French/Fama-French evidence, and the Del Guercio-Reuter broker-channel decomposition.
>- *Stock-picking performance.* Barber & Odean (2000) find that the average individual investor's net returns lag both the market and a [[4.3c Capital Asset Pricing Model|CAPM]] benchmark; high-turnover individuals do worst. Korniotis & Kumar (2013) find that individuals with higher cognitive ability do better at stock-picking but still only barely cover their costs.
>- *Institutional investors' selling failures.* Even professional asset managers, who buy well, exhibit V-shaped selling decisions on past returns (Imas et al. 2019) — the same pattern Hartzmark documented for individuals.
>
>**Common thread.** Most of these patterns can be traced to a small number of cognitive ingredients: [[8.5.1. Prospect Theory|reference dependence and loss aversion]], [[8.6.7. Extrapolative Beliefs|extrapolative expectations]], [[8.6.5. Representativeness Heuristic|familiarity bias and representativeness]], mental accounting, and present bias. Different patterns invoke different ingredients, but the underlying psychology is shared with the asset-pricing branch of behavioral finance — the household-finance literature is the same theory applied at the individual rather than the aggregate level.

# Intuition

The asset-pricing branch of behavioral finance asks how cognitive biases distort *prices*. The household-finance branch asks how the same biases distort *decisions* — the portfolio composition, trading frequency, and saving choices of individual investors who collectively determine those prices. The two branches are complementary: a bias that affects prices must affect decisions first, so household evidence is the empirical ground truth on which asset-pricing models stand.

The shift in focus brings methodological advantages. Individual investors leave a much richer paper trail than aggregate markets — every trade, every account balance, every $401$(k) allocation decision is observable in modern datasets. This lets researchers test cognitive theories at the individual level and rule out aggregation alternatives. It also brings a different normative lens: when an individual makes a costly mistake (failing to refinance, failing to annuitize), the welfare loss is directly hers, not diluted across millions of market participants. The policy implications are correspondingly sharper — auto-enrollment, default options, and intermediation rules are direct ways to fix biases at the individual level.
