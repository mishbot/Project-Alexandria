>[!note] Unconstrained Optimality Conditions
>**Unconstrained optimality conditions** are tests on the derivatives of a function $f$ that locate and classify its maxima and minima on an open set, such as $\mathbb{R}^n$, where no constraint binds. Throughout, $f$ is twice continuously differentiable.
>
>In one variable, the **first-order condition** (FOC) $f'(x^*) = 0$ is necessary for an interior local maximum or minimum at $x^*$. It is not sufficient, since $f(x) = x^3$ has $f'(0) = 0$ and no extremum at $0$. The **second-order condition** (SOC) checks the curvature at $x^*$. If $f'(x^*) = 0$ and $f''(x^*) < 0$, then $x^*$ is a strict local maximum. If $f'(x^*) = 0$ and $f''(x^*) > 0$, it is a strict local minimum.
>
>In $n$ variables, the FOC is $\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{0}$, that is, $\partial f(\mathbf{x}^*)/\partial x_i = 0$ for every $i$. The [[3.7. Hessians|Hessian]] $H$ is the $n \times n$ matrix with entries $f''_{x_i,x_j}$. Its **leading principal minors**, also called subdeterminants, are the numbers $|H|_i$. Each is the determinant of the submatrix formed by the first $i$ rows and the first $i$ columns. The **Sylvester criterion** classifies a point that satisfies the FOC:
>$$\text{max if } |H|_1 < 0,\ |H|_2 > 0,\ |H|_3 < 0, \dots \qquad\qquad \text{min if } |H|_1 > 0,\ |H|_2 > 0,\ |H|_3 > 0, \dots$$
>For a strict local maximum the signs alternate, starting with a negative one. For a strict local minimum all of them are positive. With $n = 2$ the conditions read
>$$f''_{x_1x_1} < 0 \text{ (max) or } f''_{x_1x_1} > 0 \text{ (min)}, \qquad f''_{x_1x_1}f''_{x_2x_2} - \left(f''_{x_1x_2}\right)^2 > 0.$$
>A point that fits neither pattern is not classified by the test. It may be a **saddle point**, where $f$ rises in some directions and falls in others.
>
>A function $f$ on a convex set $C \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is **concave** if $f(t\mathbf{x} + (1-t)\mathbf{y}) \geq t f(\mathbf{x}) + (1-t) f(\mathbf{y})$ for all $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in C$ and all $t \in [0, 1]$. It is **strictly concave** if the inequality is strict whenever $\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{y}$ and $t \in (0, 1)$. It is convex if $-f$ is concave, as in [[2.2 Convex Functions]]. A twice-differentiable $f$ on an open convex set is concave if and only if its Hessian minors satisfy $|H_1| \leq 0$, $|H_2| \geq 0$, $|H_3| \leq 0$, and so on, at every $\mathbf{x}$. The check must hold for every permutation of the rows and columns. With two variables this means $f''_{xx} \leq 0$, $f''_{yy} \leq 0$, and $f''_{xx}f''_{yy} - (f''_{xy})^2 \geq 0$ everywhere. The same pattern with strict inequalities implies strict concavity. If $f$ is concave (convex) on an open convex set and $\mathbf{x}_0$ satisfies the FOC, then $\mathbf{x}_0$ is a global maximum (minimum). No second-order check is needed in that case.

# Intuition

A maximum on an open set is a point where no small move helps. If the slope were positive, a move to the right would raise $f$. If it were negative, a move to the left would. So at an interior maximum the slope is zero in every direction, which is the FOC. A zero slope says the tangent is flat. It does not say which way the function bends. The diagram shows three points with a flat tangent: a local maximum, a local minimum, and an inflection point where $f$ keeps rising. The sign of $f''$ separates them.

With several variables, curvature depends on the direction of the move. The Hessian collects the second derivatives in all directions. The Sylvester criterion checks that $f$ bends down along every direction at once. A concave function bends down in every direction at every point of its domain. It therefore has no local maximum that is beaten somewhere else.

Economic agents are modeled as optimizers. A firm that chooses inputs sets the value of each input's marginal product equal to the input's price, which is the FOC for profit. The SOC or concavity confirms that the solution is a maximum. The FOCs are then the equations that the [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] differentiates in [[6.3.1 Comparative Statics]]. Consumer problems add a budget constraint and use [[7.2 Lagrange Multipliers]] instead.

# Properties

#### *(Candidate List)* The FOC finds interior candidates only, so a complete search also checks the boundary and the limits.

The FOC relies on moves in every direction, which are impossible at the edge of a closed domain. On $[0, 1]$ the function $f(x) = x$ has $f'(x) = 1$ everywhere, and its maximum is the **corner solution** $x = 1$. A maximum may also fail to exist. The function $e^x$ on $\mathbb{R}$ increases forever and has no critical point. On an exam, list the FOC points and the boundary points. Then check how $f$ behaves as $\|\mathbf{x}\| \to \infty$ or near an open edge, and compare the values of $f$.

#### *(Minimum as Negative Maximum)* Minimizing $f$ is the same as maximizing $-f$, and the switch flips the sign of every odd-order minor.

The Hessian of $-f$ is $-H$. Multiplying an $i \times i$ matrix by $-1$ multiplies its determinant by $(-1)^i$, so $|-H|_i = (-1)^i |H|_i$. The max pattern for $-f$ (negative, positive, negative, and so on) becomes the all-positive pattern for $f$. So the two criteria differ only by this sign flip. The FOC alone cannot tell the two cases apart. The function $f(x) = x^2$ satisfies $f'(0) = 0$, and $x = 0$ is its minimum.

#### *(Inconclusive Test)* When a minor is zero, or the signs fit neither pattern, the second-order test does not classify the point.

In one variable, $f''(x^*) = 0$ decides nothing. At $x = 0$ the functions $x^3$, $x^4$, and $-x^4$ all have $f' = f'' = 0$. The first has an inflection point, the second a minimum, and the third a maximum. In two variables, a negative $|H|_2$ fits neither pattern. The function $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 - x_2^2$ has $\nabla f(\mathbf{0}) = \mathbf{0}$ with minors $|H|_1 = 2$ and $|H|_2 = -4$. Along the $x_1$ axis $f = x_1^2$ rises, and along the $x_2$ axis $f = -x_2^2$ falls. The origin is a saddle point, as its level curves show.

#### *(Why Permutations)* The weak concavity test must check every principal minor, because zero leading minors can hide a positive diagonal entry.

Applying the same permutation to the rows and the columns of $H$ moves any principal minor into a leading position. A principal minor is the determinant of a submatrix that keeps the same set of rows and columns. So "all permutations" means checking all principal minors. The function $f(x, y) = 2x + y^2$ shows why this matters. Its Hessian is
$$H = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The leading minors are $|H_1| = 0 \leq 0$ and $|H_2| = 0 \geq 0$, so they pass. After swapping the two variables, $|H_1| = 2 > 0$, which fails. The function is convex in $y$, so it is not concave. With strict inequalities the leading minors alone are enough, which is why the SOC at a single point needs no permutations. The strict test is sufficient only. The function $-x^4$ is strictly concave even though its second derivative is $0$ at $x = 0$.

#### *(Local Versus Global)* The SOC certifies only a local maximum, and concavity on the whole domain gives a global one.

A point can pass the SOC and still be beaten far away. The function $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$ has a strict local maximum at $x = -1$, with $f(-1) = 2$ and $f''(-1) = -6 < 0$. Yet $f(3) = 18$, and $f$ grows without bound. A concave function cannot behave this way, because its curvature is restricted at every point. When concavity fails in some region, the only safe method is to compare the values of $f$ at all candidates.

#### *(Smooth Response)* A point that passes the Sylvester test has a nonsingular Hessian, so the optimal choice moves smoothly with parameters.

Let $\mathbf{x}^*(\mathbf{p})$ solve the FOCs $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{p}) = \mathbf{0}$. The Jacobian of this system with respect to $\mathbf{x}$ is the Hessian $H$. At a point that passes the Sylvester test, $|H|_n \neq 0$. The [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] then gives a unique, differentiable $\mathbf{x}^*(\mathbf{p})$ near that point, with $|H|_n$ as the common denominator. [[7.3 Envelope Theorem]] assumes exactly this differentiability. When $|H|_n = 0$, the optimal choice can jump or form a continuum, as in the last part of the (Hard) example below.

# Derivation

We want to show that $f'(x^*) = 0$ and $f''(x^*) < 0$ imply a strict local maximum, that is, $f(x^* + h) - f(x^*) < 0$ for every small $h \neq 0$. By Taylor's theorem,
$$f(x^* + h) - f(x^*) = f'(x^*)\,h + \frac{1}{2} f''(x^*)\,h^2 + \text{higher-order terms}.$$
The FOC removes the first term. The second term is negative for every $h \neq 0$ because $f''(x^*) < 0$. The higher-order terms shrink faster than $h^2$ as $h \to 0$. For $h$ small enough they cannot change the sign of the second term, so the difference is negative. In $n$ variables the same expansion reads
$$f(\mathbf{x}^* + \mathbf{h}) - f(\mathbf{x}^*) = \nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*) \cdot \mathbf{h} + \frac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{h}^\top H\,\mathbf{h} + \text{higher-order terms}.$$
The FOC removes the gradient term. The Sylvester pattern for a maximum is the condition that makes $\mathbf{h}^\top H\,\mathbf{h} < 0$ for every $\mathbf{h} \neq \mathbf{0}$, and the same argument applies.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture showed only that a critical point of a concave function passes the second-order conditions. That argument gives a local maximum. The global argument below uses the first-order condition for concavity instead.

For the global result, let $f$ be differentiable and concave on an open convex set $C$. Applying the first-order condition of [[2.2 Convex Functions]] to the convex function $-f$ shows that the tangent plane lies above the graph:
$$f(\mathbf{y}) \leq f(\mathbf{x}_0) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0) \cdot (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{x}_0) \qquad \text{for all } \mathbf{y} \in C.$$
If $\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0) = \mathbf{0}$, the inequality becomes $f(\mathbf{y}) \leq f(\mathbf{x}_0)$ for every $\mathbf{y} \in C$. So $\mathbf{x}_0$ is a global maximum. The convex case follows by applying this to $-f$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Fertilizer and a Storage Limit
>A farmer earns $60\sqrt{x}$ dollars of crop revenue from $x$ kilograms of fertilizer, which costs $3$ dollars per kilogram. Profit is $\pi(x) = 60\sqrt{x} - 3x$ for $x \geq 0$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the profit-maximizing amount of fertilizer. Then suppose the farmer can store at most $64$ kilograms.
>>The FOC is $\pi'(x) = 30/\sqrt{x} - 3 = 0$, so $\sqrt{x} = 10$ and $x^* = 100$. The second derivative is $\pi''(x) = -15x^{-3/2} < 0$ for every $x > 0$. So $\pi$ is concave on $(0, \infty)$, and $x^* = 100$ is the global maximum. Profit is $600 - 300 = 300$ dollars.
>>
>>With the storage limit the domain is $[0, 64]$, and the FOC point lies outside it. On this interval $\pi'$ is decreasing, and $\pi'(64) = 30/8 - 3 = 0.75 > 0$. So $\pi' > 0$ on the whole interval, and profit rises all the way to the edge. The maximum is the corner solution $x = 64$, with profit $60 \cdot 8 - 192 = 288$ dollars. The FOC does not hold at this corner. The positive derivative there shows that the farmer would gain from more fertilizer if storage allowed it.

>[!example] (Medium) A Maximum That Depends on Parameters
>Consider $f(x_1, x_2) = a x_1^2 + b x_2^2 + x_1 x_2 + 6x_1 + 4x_2$, where $a$ and $b$ are parameters.
>
>>[!example]- Find the critical point. For which $(a, b)$ is it a maximum, and for which a minimum? Solve the case $a = -1$, $b = -1/2$. Then take $a = b = -1/4$, where both own second derivatives are negative.
>>The FOCs are
>>$$2a x_1 + x_2 + 6 = 0, \qquad x_1 + 2b x_2 + 4 = 0.$$
>>When $4ab \neq 1$ they have the unique solution
>>$$x_1^* = \frac{4 - 12b}{4ab - 1}, \qquad x_2^* = \frac{6 - 8a}{4ab - 1}.$$
>>The Hessian does not depend on $\mathbf{x}$:
>>$$H = \begin{pmatrix} 2a & 1 \\ 1 & 2b \end{pmatrix}, \qquad |H|_1 = 2a, \qquad |H|_2 = 4ab - 1.$$
>>The point is a maximum when $a < 0$ and $ab > 1/4$, which forces $b < 0$ as well. It is a minimum when $a > 0$ and $ab > 1/4$. Because $H$ is constant, these sign patterns hold everywhere. The function is then strictly concave (convex), and the critical point is a global maximum (minimum). When $ab < 1/4$, $|H|_2 < 0$, so $H$ is indefinite and the critical point is a saddle. When $ab = 1/4$ the FOCs have no unique solution.
>>
>>For $a = -1$ and $b = -1/2$, $ab = 1/2 > 1/4$ and $|H|_1 = -2 < 0$. The critical point $(x_1^*, x_2^*) = (10, 14)$ is the global maximum, with $f = 58$.
>>
>>For $a = b = -1/4$, both $f''_{x_1x_1}$ and $f''_{x_2x_2}$ equal $-1/2$. Yet $|H|_2 = 1/4 - 1 = -3/4 < 0$, so the max pattern fails. The cross term $x_1x_2$ is too strong. Because $f$ is quadratic and $\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{0}$, a move by $t\mathbf{d}$ changes $f$ by exactly $\frac{1}{2}t^2\,\mathbf{d}^\top H\,\mathbf{d}$. For $\mathbf{d} = (1, 1)$ the change is $+\frac{1}{2}t^2$, and for $\mathbf{d} = (1, -1)$ it is $-\frac{3}{2}t^2$. The critical point is a saddle, and $f$ has no maximum.

>[!example] (Hard) A Firm Choosing Labor and Capital
>A firm sells its output at price $8$. It produces $q = L^{1/2}K^{1/4}$ from labor $L$ and capital $K$, each costing $1$ per unit. Profit is $\pi(L, K) = 8L^{1/2}K^{1/4} - L - K$ for $L, K > 0$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the profit-maximizing inputs and show they give a global maximum. Then explain why the method breaks down for the technology $q = L^{1/2}K^{1/2}$.
>>The FOCs set the value of each input's marginal product equal to its price:
>>$$4L^{-1/2}K^{1/4} = 1, \qquad 2L^{1/2}K^{-3/4} = 1.$$
>>The first gives $L^{1/2} = 4K^{1/4}$. Substituting into the second gives $8K^{-1/2} = 1$, so $K^* = 64$ and $L^* = 16 \cdot 8 = 128$. Output is $\sqrt{128} \cdot 64^{1/4} = 32$, and profit is $256 - 128 - 64 = 64$.
>>
>>The Hessian at any $(L, K)$ is
>>$$H = \begin{pmatrix} -2L^{-3/2}K^{1/4} & L^{-1/2}K^{-3/4} \\ L^{-1/2}K^{-3/4} & -\frac{3}{2}L^{1/2}K^{-7/4} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad |H|_2 = 2L^{-1}K^{-3/2}.$$
>>At every point $|H|_1 < 0$ and $|H|_2 > 0$, so $\pi$ is strictly concave on its whole domain. The FOC point is therefore the unique global maximum.
>>
>>Now take $q = L^{1/2}K^{1/2}$ with output price $p$ and input prices $w$ and $r$. The Hessian determinant of $pL^{1/2}K^{1/2}$ is $0$ everywhere. The function is concave but not strictly concave, and the SOC test gives no conclusion. Doubling both inputs doubles revenue and cost, so it doubles profit. Profit is therefore either unbounded or at most $0$. The FOCs have a solution only when $p^2 = 4wr$. In that case every input pair on one ray from the origin earns zero profit, and the firm has no unique optimal size.
