>[!note] Word2vec
>**Word2vec** (Mikolov et al., 2013) learns a vector for each word from unlabeled text. It rests on one idea. Words that appear in similar contexts have similar meanings. Each word $w$ in the vocabulary $\mathcal V$ gets two vectors in $\mathbb R^D$. These are a **center** vector $\mathbf v_w$ and a **context** vector $\mathbf u_w$. The **skip-gram** model predicts the words near a center word $c$. A word $o$ within a small window around $c$ gets the probability
>$$p(o \mid c) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf u_o^T\mathbf v_c)}{\sum_{w \in \mathcal V}\exp(\mathbf u_w^T\mathbf v_c)}.$$
>Training maximizes $\sum_{(c, o)}\log p(o \mid c)$ over all pairs of a center word and a nearby word in the corpus.
>
>The denominator sums over the whole vocabulary, so each pair costs $O(|\mathcal V|D)$. **Negative sampling** replaces it with binary questions. The true pair $(c, o)$ should get a high score. A few random words $w_1, \dots, w_K$ should get low scores:
>$$\max\ \log\sigma(\mathbf u_o^T\mathbf v_c) + \sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\sigma(-\mathbf u_{w_k}^T\mathbf v_c), \qquad \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}.$$
>The first term pulls $\mathbf v_c$ toward $\mathbf u_o$. The second pushes it away from the random words. After training, the embedding of a word is the average of its two vectors, $(\mathbf u_w + \mathbf v_w)/2$.

# Intuition

The [[7.3. Token Embeddings|embedding]] of a word should place it near words with similar meanings. No labels say which words are similar. Word2vec gets the signal from text itself, which makes it a [[8.1. Self-Supervised Learning|self-supervised]] method. "Cat" and "dog" both appear before "food", after "the" and near "pet". A model that predicts neighbors from the center word gives the two words similar vectors. Both must predict similar neighbors.

The Fall 2022 lecture 19 notes add a precise point. Two words get close vectors when they can replace each other in a sentence. Sitting next to each other is not enough. "Strong" and "coffee" appear together often, but they cannot swap places. "Strong" and "weak" can swap, so their vectors end up close, even though their meanings are opposite.

The same notes ask why the model predicts the neighbors from the center and not the reverse. With one input word and one output word, the prediction is simple. Predicting the center from several neighbors needs a way to combine the neighbors. The task is only a surrogate for learning vectors, so the simpler version comes first.

# Properties

#### *(Why Negatives Are Needed)* With only true pairs, every score can grow without bound, and all vectors line up.

Suppose training maximizes $\sum\log\sigma(\mathbf u_o^T\mathbf v_c)$ over true pairs alone. Making all vectors equal and long gives every pair a score near $\infty$ and a probability near $1$. Such vectors carry no information. The negative terms add a repulsive force. A random word $w$ is rarely a real neighbor of $c$, so $\mathbf v_c$ is pushed away from $\mathbf u_w$. Only words that keep appearing together stay aligned.

#### *(Why Few Negatives)* Using every wrong word as a negative would let the negatives dominate the loss, so training samples a few.

A sum over all $|\mathcal V| - 1$ wrong words brings back the cost of the softmax. It would also outweigh the single true pair. The Fall 2022 lecture 20 notes balance the two forces by sampling $K$ random words per true pair. With $|\mathcal V| = 100{,}000$, $D = 300$ and $K = 5$, a full softmax needs $3 \times 10^7$ multiply-adds per pair. Negative sampling needs $6 \cdot 300 = 1800$, about $17{,}000$ times fewer.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Mikolov et al. (2013) use $K$ from $5$ to $20$ for small corpora and $2$ to $5$ for large ones. They draw negatives with probability proportional to a word's count raised to the power $3/4$.

#### *(Gradient as Attraction and Repulsion)* The gradient with respect to $\mathbf v_c$ adds $\mathbf u_o$ with weight $1 - \sigma(\mathbf u_o^T\mathbf v_c)$ and subtracts each $\mathbf u_{w_k}$ with weight $\sigma(\mathbf u_{w_k}^T\mathbf v_c)$.

Use $\frac{d}{dx}\log\sigma(x) = 1 - \sigma(x)$ and $\frac{d}{dx}\log\sigma(-x) = -\sigma(x)$. The gradient of the objective $J$ is
$$\frac{\partial J}{\partial \mathbf v_c} = \big(1 - \sigma(\mathbf u_o^T\mathbf v_c)\big)\mathbf u_o - \sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma(\mathbf u_{w_k}^T\mathbf v_c)\,\mathbf u_{w_k}.$$
Each weight measures an error. If the true pair already scores high, its pull is weak. If a random word already scores low, its push is weak. The vectors $\mathbf u_o$ and $\mathbf u_{w_k}$ get gradients of the same form, with $\mathbf v_c$ in place of $\mathbf u$.

#### *(Analogies Emerge)* Differences between word vectors capture relations such as gender and plurality, although no part of training asks for this.

The Spring 2023 lecture 21 notes give the example $\mathrm{vec}(\text{woman}) - \mathrm{vec}(\text{man}) + \mathrm{vec}(\text{uncle}) \approx \mathrm{vec}(\text{aunt})$. The same difference also links "king" and "queen". Plural forms differ from singular forms by a similar vector. To solve "man is to woman as uncle is to ?", form $\mathrm{vec}(\text{woman}) - \mathrm{vec}(\text{man}) + \mathrm{vec}(\text{uncle})$. Then return the closest word by cosine similarity, leaving out the three input words. The Fall 2022 lecture 20 notes report that the answers mix good results with nonsense. The analogies are an emergent property, since training only predicts neighbors.

#### *(Embeddings Copy the Biases of the Text)* Word2vec learns every regularity of its corpus, including stereotypes.

The relations in the vectors come from how people write. If a corpus links some jobs mostly with one gender, the vectors do too. The Fall 2022 lecture 20 notes mention two responses. One removes harmful examples from the data. The other augments the data so that the vectors become invariant to such regularities. Neither fully solves the problem.

#### *(One Vector per Word)* Word2vec gives a word the same vector in every sentence, so it cannot separate the senses of a word.

"Bank" has one vector, which blends its meaning near "river" and near "loan". A language model fixes this. It reads the whole sentence and outputs a different vector for each use. These contextual vectors led to models like [[7.5. Encoder and Decoder Models|BERT]]. Word2vec vectors can still initialize the embedding table of such a model. Tokens that are fragments of words have no word2vec vector, though.

#### *(Choosing the Dimension)* The dimension $D$ is a hyperparameter, chosen on held-out data.

The Spring 2023 lecture 21 notes give two ways to pick it. One measures the loss of the skip-gram task on held-out text. The other measures performance on a downstream task that uses the vectors, with cross-validation. The same notes report that two vectors per word work better in practice than one.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Skip-Gram Probabilities
>A vocabulary has three context words with $\mathbf u_a = (2, 0)$, $\mathbf u_b = (0, 1)$ and $\mathbf u_c = (-1, 0)$. The center word has $\mathbf v = (1, 0)$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute $p(o \mid \text{center})$ for each of the three words.
>>The scores are $\mathbf u_a^T\mathbf v = 2$, $\mathbf u_b^T\mathbf v = 0$ and $\mathbf u_c^T\mathbf v = -1$. The exponentials are $7.389$, $1$ and $0.368$, with sum $8.757$. The probabilities are $0.844$, $0.114$ and $0.042$. Word $a$, whose context vector points along $\mathbf v$, is the most likely neighbor.

>[!example] (Medium) One Step of Negative Sampling
>A center word has $\mathbf v_c = (1, 1)$. Its true neighbor has $\mathbf u_o = (1, 0.5)$. Two sampled negatives have $\mathbf u_1 = (-1, 0)$ and $\mathbf u_2 = (0.5, -1)$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the objective and its gradient with respect to $\mathbf v_c$.
>>The scores are $\mathbf u_o^T\mathbf v_c = 1.5$, $\mathbf u_1^T\mathbf v_c = -1$ and $\mathbf u_2^T\mathbf v_c = -0.5$. The objective is
>>$$\log\sigma(1.5) + \log\sigma(1) + \log\sigma(0.5) = -0.201 - 0.313 - 0.474 = -0.989.$$
>>The weights in the gradient are $1 - \sigma(1.5) = 0.182$, $\sigma(-1) = 0.269$ and $\sigma(-0.5) = 0.378$. The gradient is
>>$$0.182\,(1, 0.5) - 0.269\,(-1, 0) - 0.378\,(0.5, -1) = (0.263,\ 0.469).$$
>>A step along this gradient raises the score of the true pair. It also lowers the scores of both negatives, since the gradient points away from each $\mathbf u_k$.

>[!example] (Hard) Solving an Analogy
>A toy embedding has $\mathrm{man} = (1, 0, 0)$, $\mathrm{woman} = (1, 1, 0)$ and $\mathrm{uncle} = (0, 0, 1)$. Other words are $\mathrm{aunt} = (0.1, 0.9, 1.0)$, $\mathrm{niece} = (0, 1, 0.4)$ and $\mathrm{king} = (1, 0.1, 0.2)$.
>
>>[!example]- Answer "man is to woman as uncle is to ?" with cosine similarity. Why must the input words be excluded?
>>The target is $\mathrm{woman} - \mathrm{man} + \mathrm{uncle} = (0, 1, 1)$, with norm $\sqrt 2$. The cosine similarities with the candidates are
>>$$\cos(\mathrm{aunt}) = \frac{1.9}{1.349 \cdot 1.414} = 0.996, \qquad \cos(\mathrm{niece}) = \frac{1.4}{1.077 \cdot 1.414} = 0.919, \qquad \cos(\mathrm{king}) = \frac{0.3}{1.025 \cdot 1.414} = 0.207.$$
>>The answer is "aunt". The input word "uncle" has cosine $0.707$, and "woman" has $0.5$. Here they rank below "aunt", but in real embeddings an input word is often the closest vector to the target. The difference vector $\mathrm{woman} - \mathrm{man}$ is short compared with $\mathrm{uncle}$, so the target stays near "uncle". Excluding the inputs makes the method return a new word.
