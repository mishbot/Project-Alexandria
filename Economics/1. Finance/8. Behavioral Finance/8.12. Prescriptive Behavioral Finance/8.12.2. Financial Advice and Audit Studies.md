>[!note] Financial Advice and Audit Studies
>**Financial-advice audit studies** are field experiments in which trained auditors pose as ordinary clients seeking financial advice and record what advisors recommend. They test the market-based hypothesis that competition among advisors should drive recommendations toward client welfare, even when individual clients are financially illiterate. The empirical record rejects this hypothesis. Two paradigm studies on different products and continents find the same pattern: advisors push high-fee, suboptimal products, often opposite to what the client should buy.
>
>**Mullainathan-Noth-Schoar ($2012$) — Boston advisor audit.** Trained auditors visited financial advisors with one of four pre-scripted portfolios: index-fund-heavy (the welfare-maximizing baseline), employer-stock-heavy, cash-heavy, and "bias-prone" (over-extrapolating recent winners). The auditors asked for advice on improving their existing portfolio. The dominant finding: advisors recommended that clients move *away* from index funds and *into* high-fee active funds, regardless of which baseline portfolio the auditor presented. Even auditors whose existing portfolio was already optimal (index-heavy) were steered toward higher-fee active funds.
>- Roughly $50\%$ of advisors recommended switching from index funds to active funds.
>- The fee differential averaged $1$ percentage point per year.
>- Advisors did *not* differentially flag the bias-prone portfolio for de-biasing — they generally accommodated whatever bias the client appeared to have, because doing so created an opening to sell new products.
>
>**Anagol-Cole-Santosh ($2013$) — India insurance audit.** Trained auditors visited insurance advisors in Delhi seeking life-insurance recommendations. Auditors expressed varying preferences and stated their financial circumstances. The welfare-improving recommendation in nearly every case was term life insurance — pure insurance with no savings component, low fees. The vast majority of advisors instead recommended *whole life* insurance — a more expensive, savings-loaded product that pays larger commissions to the advisor.
>- Whole-life recommended to roughly $80\%$ of auditor visits.
>- Term-life recommended to roughly $5\%$.
>- Advisors generally aligned recommendations with client-stated *preferences*, not with client-stated *circumstances* — they would not contradict a client's expressed leaning even when the client's circumstances pointed clearly toward term insurance.
>
>**The unifying lesson.** Both studies document a common failure: the financial-advice market does not reliably substitute for client financial literacy. Commission structures and conflicts of interest push advisors toward recommendations that maximize their own revenue rather than client welfare. The implication is that a regulatory regime relying on advisor competition to protect retail consumers is unlikely to succeed without structural reform of advisor incentives (fiduciary-duty rules, commission bans, disclosure mandates) — and even those reforms have a mixed empirical record.

# Intuition

Financial-advice markets are an information-asymmetry problem. The client does not know the welfare-maximizing portfolio; the advisor does (or could). In a competitive market with well-informed clients, advisors who recommend bad products lose business to advisors who recommend good ones. The audit-study finding is that this competitive force is weak in practice because the client is *not* well-informed enough to detect bad advice. The advisor can sell a high-fee active fund to a client who would have bought an index fund elsewhere, because the client cannot evaluate the recommendation against the alternative.

The key observation is that the advisor's incentive structure is misaligned with the client's interest. Most advisors are paid commissions or asset-based fees that scale with the fees the recommended product charges. A higher-fee product means more revenue to the advisor; the same fund-family relationships that pay the largest commissions also offer the highest-fee products. The advisor selecting a recommendation faces a direct trade-off between client welfare and own income. In settings where the client cannot detect the trade-off, the advisor predictably leans toward own income.

The two audit studies are particularly compelling because they are field experiments with random assignment of clients to advisors and pre-specified scripts. The pattern they document is not driven by a small subset of bad advisors; it appears across the majority of advisors visited. The implication is structural rather than personal: the advice market produces these recommendations because the incentive structure creates them, not because individual advisors are unusually unethical.

# Properties

#### *(Boston Audit — Active Over Index)* Advisors steered $50\%$ of clients toward active funds.

Mullainathan-Noth-Schoar ($2012$) find that approximately half of advisors visited recommended that the auditor move out of index funds and into actively-managed funds with higher fees. The finding is striking because the auditors with index-heavy portfolios were already in an optimal allocation by most academic standards (low fees, broad diversification). The recommendation moved them strictly worse on a fee basis without any improvement in expected return — exactly the kind of advice that would lose business in a competitive, well-informed market and that survives in a market with low client literacy.

#### *(Boston Audit — Bias Accommodation)* Advisors did not de-bias clients.

A key finding of Mullainathan-Noth-Schoar is that advisors did *not* exploit the audit as an opportunity to correct bias-prone portfolios. Auditors who walked in with bias-prone portfolios (extrapolating recent winners, holding employer stock, cash-heavy) typically left with new bias-prone portfolios — sometimes more bias-prone than before. The advisors did not push these clients toward the index-fund benchmark; instead, they accommodated the existing bias and added their own product recommendations on top. This is consistent with a commission-driven incentive: any reasonable change in the client's portfolio creates an opportunity to charge a transaction or advisory fee, and advisors choose whatever change is most palatable.

#### *(India Audit — Whole Life Over Term)* Advisors steered $80\%$ of clients toward whole-life insurance.

Anagol-Cole-Santosh ($2013$) find that whole-life insurance — typically the wrong product for the auditor's circumstances — was recommended to about $80\%$ of clients. Whole-life insurance combines insurance with a savings component and pays much larger commissions to the advisor than term insurance does. The combination is generally inferior to "buy term and invest the difference" for cost-aware investors, because the embedded savings vehicle inside the whole-life product earns lower returns than a comparably-allocated investment account would.

#### *(India Audit — Preference Confirmation)* Advisors aligned recommendations with stated preferences, not stated circumstances.

When the auditor expressed a preference for whole-life insurance even though her circumstances pointed toward term, the advisor confirmed the preference. When the auditor expressed a preference for term insurance even though her circumstances pointed toward whole-life, the advisor again often confirmed the preference. The pattern is consistent with an advisor who maximizes the probability of closing a sale: contradicting the client's stated preference risks losing the sale, so the advisor accommodates whatever the client wants regardless of welfare.

#### *(Pattern Robustness Across Continents)* Same failure in U.S. and India suggests structural cause.

The Boston and India studies cover different products (mutual funds versus life insurance), different financial-services regulatory regimes, and different client populations. The fact that both find the same pattern — advisors push higher-commission products against client welfare — supports a structural rather than country-specific interpretation. The unifying mechanism is the commission structure: when advisor compensation scales with the fee or premium of the product sold, advisors push high-fee products.

#### *(Policy Implication — Fiduciary Rules)* Regulatory reform addresses the structural incentive.

The audit-study results have motivated regulatory pushes for *fiduciary duty* — the requirement that advisors act in client interest rather than maximize own commissions. The U.S. Department of Labor's $2016$ fiduciary rule (later partially rescinded) and the SEC's $2019$ Regulation Best Interest are policy responses to exactly this problem. Empirical evidence on whether fiduciary rules succeed is mixed — Egan-Matvos-Seru ($2019$) find that fiduciary advisors do recommend less expensive products, but other channels (commission products sold under non-fiduciary capacity) sometimes substitute.

# Examples

>[!example] An auditor with an index-heavy portfolio visits a Boston advisor
>The auditor presents a portfolio: $\$200{,}000$ in low-fee index funds, $50/50$ stocks/bonds, no concentrated positions. By every academic standard, this is a defensible allocation requiring at most marginal adjustments. The advisor reviews the portfolio and recommends switching half of it into actively-managed equity and bond funds with average expense ratios of $1.0\%$ (versus the index funds' $0.1\%$). The recommended switch raises annual fees from approximately $\$200$ to approximately $\$1{,}100$, with no expected return advantage. Mullainathan-Noth-Schoar document this pattern in roughly $50\%$ of advisor visits with index-heavy auditors.

>[!example]- Why whole-life dominates term-life in India
>An auditor visits an insurance agent in Delhi seeking advice on life-insurance for a young family. The economically-correct recommendation is term life: a $\$1$ million, $20$-year level-premium term policy at $\$300$/year. Total premium over the policy life: $\$6{,}000$. A whole-life policy with the same death benefit might cost $\$5{,}000$/year for $20$ years and $\$3{,}000$/year for life thereafter. Total premium: easily $\$200{,}000+$. The whole-life policy includes a savings component, but the embedded return (typically $2$–$3\%$ per year, net of fees) is well below what the same client could earn investing the term-policy savings ($\$4{,}700$/year for $20$ years) in a low-cost equity index fund ($\sim 7\%$ historical real return). The agent's commission on whole-life is also much higher: typically $50$–$100\%$ of the first year's premium, versus $5$–$10\%$ for term. The structural conflict pushes the agent toward whole-life, and Anagol-Cole-Santosh document this pattern in roughly $80\%$ of audited advisory visits.

>[!example]- The fiduciary-rule reform
>The U.S. Department of Labor finalized a fiduciary rule in $2016$ requiring retirement-account advisors to act in client interest rather than under the looser "suitability" standard. Empirical work suggests the rule's announcement and partial implementation moved a meaningful share of advice away from high-commission annuity products toward lower-fee alternatives. The rule was rescinded after a court challenge in $2018$, providing a natural experiment in the reverse direction; some of the post-rescission patterns suggest a partial reversion to higher-commission product sales. The episode illustrates both that regulatory reform of advisor incentives can change behavior and that the political-economic foundation for such reform is fragile.
