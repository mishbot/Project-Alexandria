>[!note] Value of a Forward Contract
>The **value of a forward contract** at a date $t$ before maturity is the cost of taking over one side. A negative value means the new holder receives cash. A forward is worth zero on the day it is signed, and its value changes as prices move. Let $K$ be the delivery price in the contract and $T$ its maturity date. Let $F_t$ be the forward price at $t$ for delivery on the same date $T$. Let $r$ be the continuously compounded risk-free rate. The values of the long and the short positions are
>$$f_{\text{long}} = (F_t - K)e^{-r(T-t)}, \qquad f_{\text{short}} = (K - F_t)e^{-r(T-t)}.$$
>The long will buy at $K$ an asset that a new contract would buy at $F_t$. That difference arrives at $T$, so it is discounted over the time left, $T - t$. At signing $K = F_0$ and $f = 0$. At maturity $F_T = S_T$, so the value equals the payoff $S_T - K$.
>
>Substituting the [[2.2.2b Forward Price|forward price]] at $t$ gives three cases for the long. In each case the short's value is the negative of the long's value.
>- An asset with **no income** has $F_t = S_te^{r(T-t)}$, so the long is worth $S_t - Ke^{-r(T-t)}$.
>- An asset with **known cash income** has $F_t = \big(S_t - \text{PV}_t(D)\big)e^{r(T-t)}$, so the long is worth $S_t - \text{PV}_t(D) - Ke^{-r(T-t)}$.
>- An asset with a **dividend yield** $q$ has $F_t = S_te^{(r - q)(T-t)}$, so the long is worth $S_te^{-q(T-t)} - Ke^{-r(T-t)}$.
>
>Here $S_t$ is the asset price at $t$. The term $\text{PV}_t(D)$ is the value at $t$ of the cash dividends paid between $t$ and $T$.
>
>The proof uses an offsetting forward. The holder of the old long enters a new short at $F_t$, which costs nothing. The pair pays $F_t - K$ at $T$ for certain, so the old contract is worth the present value of that amount. If a dealer quotes any other value, a trader buys or sells the contract and enters the offsetting forward. The trader then locks in a risk-free profit. The argument assumes a frictionless market with no counterparty risk. The same argument values a currency forward ([[2.2.2d Covered Interest Parity]]) and an [[2.2.2e Forward Rate Agreement|FRA]].

>[!warning] Notation for the Value
>The lecture notes write $F_t$, and later $f$ or $f_A$, for the value of a forward contract. They write $F$ for the forward price at $t$. These notes write $f$ for the value and $F_t$ for the forward price. In the lecture notes, the line $F = S_t - \text{PV}_t(D) - Ke^{-r(T-t)}$ gives the value of the contract.

# Intuition

A forward signed today is worth nothing to either side. Then prices move, and the contract becomes an asset to one side and a debt to the other. A firm needs this number to report the contract in its accounts and to measure its counterparty risk. It also needs it to close the contract before $T$. The value is what a third party would pay to take over the firm's side of the contract.

The figure shows a long forward on an asset with no income. The dashed line is the payoff at $T$ against $S_T$. The solid line is the value at $t$ against $S_t$. Both lines have slope $1$. The value line lies to the left of the payoff line by $K(1 - e^{-r(T-t)})$. That gap is the interest that $Ke^{-r(T-t)}$ earns over the time left. So the long has a positive value whenever $S_t > Ke^{-r(T-t)}$, including some prices below $K$. As $t$ approaches $T$, the gap shrinks and the two lines meet.

# Properties

#### *(Closing a Forward Early)* A holder can exit a forward before $T$ in three ways, and each is worth $f$ today.

In an unwind, the two parties cancel the contract. The side with a negative value pays $|f|$ to the other side at $t$, and all risk ends. In a transfer, a third party takes over the contract and pays $f$, with the counterparty's consent. In an offset, the holder enters a new forward in the opposite direction at $F_t$. The offset fixes the amount $F_t - K$, but pays it only at $T$. The holder still has two open contracts, often with two different counterparties. It bears the default risk of both until $T$. A forward has no exchange where it can be resold, so each route needs a willing counterparty.

#### *(What Moves the Value)* The long's value rises with the asset price and with the interest rate, and it changes as time passes.

Take the yield case, $f = S_te^{-q(T-t)} - Ke^{-r(T-t)}$. A one-dollar rise in $S_t$ changes the value by
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial S_t} = e^{-q(T-t)}.$$
This number is the forward's delta, its change in value per unit change in the asset price. It equals $1$ with no income or with cash dividends. The delta does not depend on $S_t$. A dealer who is short a forward therefore hedges with $e^{-q(T-t)}$ units of the asset, whatever $S_t$ does. An option's delta changes with $S_t$, so its hedge must be adjusted. With $S_t$ held fixed, a higher $r$ lowers $Ke^{-r(T-t)}$ and raises the long's value. The holder of the long pays $K$ later, and a higher rate makes that later payment cheaper today. With no income and $S_t$ fixed, $Ke^{-r(T-t)}$ rises toward $K$ as $t$ approaches $T$, so the long's value falls.

#### *(Only Remaining Income Counts)* Dividends paid before $t$ do not enter the value, and the remaining dividends are discounted to $t$.

A dividend paid before $t$ went to whoever held the asset then. The price $S_t$ already reflects that payment, so subtracting it again counts it twice. Only dividends paid between $t$ and $T$ reduce what the long receives, so only they enter $\text{PV}_t(D)$. Each is discounted from its payment date back to $t$, not back to $0$. Two other errors are common. One uses the old forward price $F_0$ in place of $F_t$. The other discounts over $T$ instead of the time left, $T - t$. The (Medium) example below shows the size of the paid-dividend error.

#### *(Other Forward Contracts)* The offsetting argument values any contract that a new contract at today's market price can cancel.

A currency forward is the yield case with $q = r_f$, the foreign interest rate ([[2.2.2d Covered Interest Parity]]). An [[2.2.2e Forward Rate Agreement|FRA]] fixes the interest rate $R_K$ for a future period $[T_1, T_2]$. The parties compute payments on a notional $L$, a principal that never changes hands. Take the side that receives $R_K$ and pays the floating rate. A new FRA at today's forward rate $R_F$ cancels the floating payment. That leaves $L(R_K - R_F)\tau$ at $T_2$, with $\tau = T_2 - T_1$. Its value is that amount times $P(t, T_2)$, the price at $t$ of a zero-coupon bond paying $1$ at $T_2$. An [[2.2.5a Interest Rate Swap|interest rate swap]] is a portfolio of FRAs, so its value is the sum of their values. When the yield curve is not flat, $P(t, T)$ replaces $e^{-r(T-t)}$ in every formula above.

# Derivation

We want the value $f$ at $t$ of a long forward with delivery price $K$ and maturity $T$. At $t$, hold the old long and enter a new short forward at $F_t$. The new contract costs nothing, since $F_t$ is the delivery price that gives a new contract zero value. The table lists the cash flows.

| Position | At $t$ | At $T$ |
|---|---|---|
| Old long forward at $K$ | $0$ (already held) | $S_T - K$ |
| New short forward at $F_t$ | $0$ | $F_t - S_T$ |
| Total | $0$ | $F_t - K$ |

The total at $T$ does not depend on $S_T$. By the [[4.2.2a Arbitrage|law of one price]], a certain payoff is worth its present value. The new short is worth zero, so the old long is worth as much as the pair,
$$f = (F_t - K)e^{-r(T-t)}.$$
Here $e^{-r(T-t)}$ discounts from $T$ back to $t$. The short holds the mirror pair, so its value is $(K - F_t)e^{-r(T-t)}$. Substituting each forward price gives the three cases. With no income, $(S_te^{r(T-t)} - K)e^{-r(T-t)} = S_t - Ke^{-r(T-t)}$, and the other two follow the same way.

Now suppose a dealer quotes the long contract at $f_A \ne f$. If $f_A > f$, the trader takes the short side at $K$ and receives $f_A$ from the dealer, who takes the long. The trader also enters a new long at $F_t$ and invests $f_A$ at $r$.

| Position | At $t$ | At $T$ |
|---|---|---|
| Short at $K$ (the dealer takes the long) | $+f_A$ | $K - S_T$ |
| New long forward at $F_t$ | $0$ | $S_T - F_t$ |
| Invest $f_A$ at $r$ | $-f_A$ | $f_Ae^{r(T-t)}$ |
| Total | $0$ | $f_Ae^{r(T-t)} - (F_t - K)$ |

If $f_A < f$, the trader buys the long contract for $f_A$, enters a new short at $F_t$, and borrows $f_A$.

| Position | At $t$ | At $T$ |
|---|---|---|
| Long at $K$ (bought from the dealer) | $-f_A$ | $S_T - K$ |
| New short forward at $F_t$ | $0$ | $F_t - S_T$ |
| Borrow $f_A$ at $r$ | $+f_A$ | $-f_Ae^{r(T-t)}$ |
| Total | $0$ | $(F_t - K) - f_Ae^{r(T-t)}$ |

Each total is certain and positive exactly when the quote lies on that side of $f$. The sign of $f_A$ does not matter. A negative $f_A$ means the side that takes the long receives cash. The trader then borrows or invests a negative amount.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Stock Forward Five Months Later
>Five months ago, a trader entered a one-year long forward on 300 shares at $K = \$84.20$. The stock pays no dividends. Today the stock trades at $\$91.50$. The risk-free rate is $3.6\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Find today's forward price for the same delivery date and the value of the contract to each side.
>>The time left is $T - t = 7/12$ year, so $r(T - t) = 0.036 \times 7/12 = 0.021$. Today's forward price is
>>$$F_t = 91.50e^{0.021} = \$93.44.$$
>>The value of the long per share is $(93.44 - 84.20)e^{-0.021} = 9.24 \times 0.9792 = \$9.05$. The shortcut for no income gives the same number, $91.50 - 84.20e^{-0.021} = 91.50 - 82.45 = \$9.05$. For 300 shares, the unrounded value of $\$9.0498$ per share gives $\$2{,}714.93$ for the long and $-\$2{,}714.93$ for the short. The long has gained because the stock now trades above $Ke^{-r(T-t)} = \$82.45$.

>[!example] (Medium) A Dividend Already Paid
>Four months ago, a trader entered a ten-month long forward on 1,000 shares at $K = \$47.00$. The stock pays a dividend of $\$0.65$ three months and nine months after signing. Today the stock trades at $\$44.80$. The risk-free rate is $5\%$ per year with continuous compounding for all maturities.
>
>>[!example]- Find the value of the contract to the long, and the error from also subtracting the dividend already paid.
>>The first dividend was paid one month ago, so it does not count. The second comes in five months. The time left is $T - t = 0.5$ year. The present value of the remaining dividend is
>>$$\text{PV}_t(D) = 0.65e^{-0.05 \times 5/12} = \$0.6366.$$
>>Today's forward price is $F_t = (44.80 - 0.6366)e^{0.025} = 44.1634 \times 1.0253 = \$45.28$. The value of the long per share is
>>$$f = S_t - \text{PV}_t(D) - Ke^{-r(T-t)} = 44.80 - 0.6366 - 47e^{-0.025} = 44.1634 - 45.8396 = -\$1.68.$$
>>For 1,000 shares the long is worth $-\$1{,}676.16$ and the short $+\$1{,}676.16$. The long is worth less than zero because $S_t - \text{PV}_t(D)$ is below $Ke^{-r(T-t)}$. Subtracting the paid $\$0.65$ as well gives $-\$2.33$ per share. That error overstates the long's loss by $\$650$ on the contract.

>[!example] (Hard) A Dealer Offer on a Losing Forward
>A fund is long a forward on 25,000 shares of an index fund at $K = \$412.00$, with nine months left. The index fund trades at $\$398.40$ and pays a dividend yield of $1.4\%$ per year. The risk-free rate is $4.2\%$ per year. Both rates are continuously compounded. A dealer offers to take over the fund's long position if the fund pays it $\$95{,}000$.
>
>>[!example]- Value the fund's position, decide whether to accept, and build a third party's arbitrage at this quote.
>>Today's forward price is $F_t = 398.40e^{(0.042 - 0.014)(0.75)} = 398.40e^{0.021} = \$406.85$. The value of the long per share is
>>$$f = 398.40e^{-0.014 \times 0.75} - 412e^{-0.042 \times 0.75} = 394.2387 - 399.2243 = -\$4.99.$$
>>For 25,000 shares the long is worth $-\$124{,}639.72$. The fund holds a debt of that size. The dealer will take it for $\$95{,}000$, so the fund should accept. It gains $124{,}639.72 - 95{,}000 = \$29{,}639.72$ today.
>>
>>The dealer's quote values the long at $f_A = -\$95{,}000$, above $f = -\$124{,}639.72$. A third party takes the short side at $K$ with the dealer and pays the dealer $\$95{,}000$. It funds the payment with a nine-month loan, which grows by the factor $e^{0.0315} = 1.0320$. It also enters a new long at $F_t$ on 25,000 shares.
>>
>>| Position | Today | In nine months |
>>|---|---|---|
>>| Short at $\$412.00$ with the dealer | $-\$95{,}000.00$ | $25{,}000\,(412 - S_T)$ |
>>| New long at $\$406.85$ | $\$0$ | $25{,}000\,(S_T - 406.85)$ |
>>| Borrow at $4.2\%$ | $+\$95{,}000.00$ | $-\$98{,}040.13$ |
>>| Total | $\$0$ | $+\$30{,}588.24$ |
>>
>>With the unrounded $F_t$, the two forwards pay $25{,}000\,(412 - F_t) = \$128{,}628.37$ whatever the index does. The profit at $T$ is $128{,}628.37 - 98{,}040.13 = \$30{,}588.24$. Its present value is $30{,}588.24e^{-0.0315} = \$29{,}639.72$, which is $f_A - f$. The trade works even though both $f$ and $f_A$ are negative.
