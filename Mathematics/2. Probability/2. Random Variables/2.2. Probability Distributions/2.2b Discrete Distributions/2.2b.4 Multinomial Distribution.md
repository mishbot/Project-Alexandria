>[!note] Multinomial Distribution
>A discrete random vector $\mathbf{X} = (X_1, \dots, X_k)$ follows the **Multinomial distribution** $\mathbf{X} \sim \text{Mult}(n, \mathbf{p})$ with $n \in \mathbb{N}$ trials and probability vector $\mathbf{p} = (p_1, \dots, p_k)$ satisfying $p_i > 0$ and $\sum_{i=1}^k p_i = 1$, defined by the [[2.2b.1 Probability Mass Function|probability mass function]]
>$$p_{\mathbf{X}}(x_1, \dots, x_k) = \frac{n!}{x_1!\, x_2! \cdots x_k!}\,p_1^{x_1}\,p_2^{x_2}\cdots p_k^{x_k} = \binom{n}{x_1, \dots, x_k}\prod_{i=1}^k p_i^{x_i},$$
>where $x_i \in \{0, 1, \dots, n\}$ and $\sum_{i=1}^k x_i = n$. The **multinomial coefficient** $\binom{n}{x_1, \dots, x_k} = \frac{n!}{x_1!\cdots x_k!}$ counts the number of ways to partition $n$ objects into $k$ groups of sizes $x_1, \dots, x_k$.
>
>The Multinomial distribution models the **counts of outcomes** when $n$ independent trials are performed, each resulting in exactly one of $k \geq 2$ mutually exclusive categories. It is the natural extension of the [[2.2b.3 Binomial Distribution|Binomial distribution]] from two outcomes to $k$ outcomes.
>
>Special cases:
>- If $k = 2$, the Multinomial reduces to the [[2.2b.3 Binomial Distribution|Binomial]]: $X_1 \sim \text{Bin}(n, p_1)$ and $X_2 = n - X_1$.
>- If $n = 1$, the Multinomial reduces to a single **categorical** trial (the multi-category analogue of [[2.2b.2 Bernoulli Distribution|Bernoulli]]).
>
>>[!warning] Components Are Not Independent
>>Although the $n$ trials are independent, the components $X_1, \dots, X_k$ of a Multinomial vector are **negatively correlated** because they are constrained to sum to $n$. For $i \neq j$, $\text{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = -np_ip_j < 0$: observing more outcomes in category $i$ necessarily leaves fewer for category $j$.

# Intuition
The Multinomial distribution generalizes the Binomial from coin flips (two outcomes) to dice rolls ($k$ outcomes). Imagine rolling a (possibly weighted) $k$-sided die $n$ times and counting how many times each face appears. The result is a vector of counts $(X_1, \dots, X_k)$ with $\sum X_i = n$, and this vector follows $\text{Mult}(n, \mathbf{p})$.

Because the counts must sum to $n$, specifying any $k - 1$ of them determines the last. This constraint introduces negative dependence between components—if one category gets more than expected, the others must collectively get less. Despite this marginal dependence, each individual component $X_i$ is simply $\text{Bin}(n, p_i)$, and all the complexity lies in the joint structure.

# Properties
#### (*Marginals*) Each component is Binomial: $X_i \sim \text{Bin}(n, p_i)$ for $i = 1, \dots, k$.
#### (*Mean*) $\mathbb{E}[X_i] = np_i$.
#### (*Variance*) $\text{Var}(X_i) = np_i(1 - p_i)$.
#### (*Covariance*) For $i \neq j$, $\text{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = -np_ip_j$.
This follows from the constraint $\sum X_i = n$: taking the variance of both sides gives $\sum_i \text{Var}(X_i) + 2\sum_{i < j}\text{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = 0$, which, combined with the marginal variances, yields the result.

#### (*Correlation*) For $i \neq j$, $\rho(X_i, X_j) = \dfrac{-p_ip_j}{\sqrt{p_i(1-p_i)\,p_j(1-p_j)}} = -\sqrt{\dfrac{p_ip_j}{(1-p_i)(1-p_j)}}$.
#### (*Aggregation*) If categories $i$ and $j$ are merged into a single category with probability $p_i + p_j$, the result is $\text{Mult}(n, \mathbf{p}')$ with $k - 1$ categories. Multinomial structure is preserved under grouping.
#### (*Conditioning*) Conditional on $X_1 = x_1$, the remaining counts $(X_2, \dots, X_k)$ follow $\text{Mult}\!\left(n - x_1,\; \frac{p_2}{1 - p_1}, \dots, \frac{p_k}{1 - p_1}\right)$.
#### (*Sum of Independent Multinomials*) If $\mathbf{X} \sim \text{Mult}(n, \mathbf{p})$ and $\mathbf{Y} \sim \text{Mult}(m, \mathbf{p})$ are independent, then $\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{Y} \sim \text{Mult}(n + m, \mathbf{p})$.
#### (*Bayesian Conjugacy*) The [[2.2c.10 Dirichlet Distribution|Dirichlet distribution]] is the conjugate prior for the Multinomial likelihood. If $\boldsymbol{\Theta} \sim \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})$ and $\mathbf{X} \mid \boldsymbol{\Theta} \sim \text{Mult}(n, \boldsymbol{\Theta})$, then $\boldsymbol{\Theta} \mid \mathbf{X} \sim \text{Dir}(\alpha_1 + x_1, \dots, \alpha_k + x_k)$.

# Examples
>[!example] Genetic Offspring Ratios
>In a genetics experiment, offspring of a particular cross fall into three phenotype categories with theoretical probabilities $\mathbf{p} = (9/16,\; 3/16,\; 4/16)$ under Mendelian inheritance. In a sample of $n = 160$ offspring, the observed counts are $(x_1, x_2, x_3) = (86, 35, 39)$.
>>[!example]- What are the expected counts, and how can goodness of fit be assessed?
>>Under $\text{Mult}(160, \mathbf{p})$, the expected counts are
>>$$\mathbb{E}[X_1] = 160 \cdot \tfrac{9}{16} = 90, \quad \mathbb{E}[X_2] = 160 \cdot \tfrac{3}{16} = 30, \quad \mathbb{E}[X_3] = 160 \cdot \tfrac{4}{16} = 40.$$
>>The Pearson chi-square statistic is
>>$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^3 \frac{(x_i - np_i)^2}{np_i} = \frac{(86-90)^2}{90} + \frac{(35-30)^2}{30} + \frac{(39-40)^2}{40} = \frac{16}{90} + \frac{25}{30} + \frac{1}{40} \approx 1.04.$$
>>Under the null hypothesis, $\chi^2 \stackrel{\text{approx}}{\sim} \chi^2_{k-1} = \chi^2_2$. Since $1.04$ is well below the $95\%$ critical value of $5.99$, there is no evidence against the Mendelian model.

>[!example] Dice Fairness
>A six-sided die is rolled $n = 600$ times, yielding counts $(X_1, \dots, X_6)$.
>>[!example]- What is the covariance between $X_1$ (count of ones) and $X_6$ (count of sixes)?
>>If the die is fair, $\mathbf{p} = (1/6, \dots, 1/6)$. By the covariance property,
>>$$\text{Cov}(X_1, X_6) = -np_1p_6 = -600 \cdot \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{6} = -\frac{600}{36} = -\frac{50}{3} \approx -16.67.$$
>>The negative covariance confirms the intuition: more ones in $600$ rolls means fewer sixes. The correlation is $\rho(X_1, X_6) = -\frac{1/36}{\sqrt{(5/36)^2}} = -\frac{1}{5} = -0.2$.
