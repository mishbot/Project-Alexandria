>[!note] Infinite Line of Charge
>An **infinite line of charge** is a straight line carrying a constant linear charge density $\lambda$. At a perpendicular distance $R$ from the line, its field has the size
>$$E = \frac{2k\lambda}{R}.$$
>The field points straight away from the line when $\lambda > 0$ and straight toward it when $\lambda < 0$. It lies in the plane perpendicular to the line and has no component along the line. Its size depends only on $R$. It falls off as $1/R$, more slowly than the $1/r^2$ of a point charge.
>
>Written with the permittivity of free space, as from [[3.3.2. Gauss's Law]] onward,
>$$E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 R}.$$
>A real rod of length $2L$ behaves as infinite at points near its middle, at distances $R \ll L$.

# Intuition

Every piece of the line pushes on a test charge at P. The pieces above P push it partly down. The matching pieces below push it partly up by the same amount. Those components cancel. What survives is the push straight away from the line. Pieces far up or down the line are far from P and push mostly along the line. They add little to the outward push. The outward field therefore comes mostly from the stretch of line within a few times $R$ of P.

The animation lights up the part of the line within a distance $Y$ of P. A bar shows its share of the field. The part within $R$ already gives $71\%$. The part within $3R$ gives $95\%$.

That explains the $1/R$ law. Doubling the distance halves the field. For a point charge it would quarter the field. All of a point charge's field comes from one place. A farther point beside a line receives significant field from a longer stretch of the line. That longer stretch partly makes up for the greater distance.

# Derivation

Put the line along the $y$-axis and the field point P at $(R, 0)$. A piece of the line at height $y$ has length $dy$ and charge $dq = \lambda\,dy$. Its distance to P is $\sqrt{y^2 + R^2}$, so its field has the size
$$dE = k\,\frac{\lambda\,dy}{y^2 + R^2}.$$
The line is infinite and the charge is uniform. The piece at $-y$ therefore cancels the $y$-component of the piece at $+y$. Only the $x$-component survives. The angle $\theta$ between $d\vec E$ and the $x$-axis has $\cos\theta = R/\sqrt{y^2 + R^2}$, read from the right triangle with legs $R$ and $y$. Adding all the pieces, from $y = -\infty$ to $y = \infty$,
$$E_x = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} k\,\frac{\lambda\,dy}{y^2 + R^2}\cdot\frac{R}{\sqrt{y^2 + R^2}} = k\lambda R\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{dy}{(y^2 + R^2)^{3/2}}.$$
The integration variable is $y$. The quantities $k$, $\lambda$, and $R$ are constants and come outside.

Substitute $y = R\tan u$. The factor $R$ keeps the units right, since $\tan u$ has no units. Then $dy = R\sec^2 u\,du$ and $y^2 + R^2 = R^2\sec^2 u$. The limits $y = \pm\infty$ become $u = \pm\pi/2$:
$$E_x = k\lambda R\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \frac{R\sec^2 u\,du}{R^3\sec^3 u} = \frac{k\lambda}{R}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \cos u\,du = \frac{k\lambda}{R}\Big[\sin u\Big]_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} = \frac{2k\lambda}{R}.$$
Every point at distance $R$ gives the same answer, so the field has the same size all around the line.

# Properties

#### *(Two Symmetries Needed)* The field points straight out only because the line is both infinite and uniformly charged.

The cancellation along the line needs a matching piece at $-y$ for every piece at $+y$. An infinite line supplies the matching position. A constant $\lambda$ supplies the matching charge. Near the end of a finite rod the matching piece is missing, and the field tilts away from the perpendicular.

#### *(Close Means Infinite)* A finite rod looks infinite from points much closer to it than its length.

Most of the outward field comes from the part of the line within a few times $R$ of P. If the rod extends well beyond that on both sides, the missing far pieces barely matter. Far from a finite rod, $R \gg L$, the rod looks like a point charge, and the field falls off as $1/R^2$.

#### *(Same Result by Gauss's Law)* Gauss's law gives $E = \lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 R)$ with no integral at all.

Since $k = 1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$, the two forms agree: $2k\lambda/R = \lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 R)$. The Gauss's law route in [[3.3.2b Cylindrical Symmetry]] uses the same symmetry facts as this derivation. It needs them to hold exactly, so it cannot handle the finite rod either.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Field of a Charged Wire
>A long straight wire carries $\lambda = 3.0\ \text{nC/m}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field $20\ \text{cm}$ from the wire.
>>$$E = \frac{2k\lambda}{R} = \frac{2(8.99 \times 10^{9})(3.0 \times 10^{-9})}{0.20} = 270\ \text{N/C}.$$
>>The field points straight away from the wire.

>[!example] (Medium) Force on a Nearby Electron
>An electron sits $5.0\ \text{mm}$ from a long thread carrying $\lambda = -2.0\ \text{nC/m}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the force on the electron and say how it changes if the distance doubles.
>>The field has size
>>$$E = \frac{2(8.99 \times 10^{9})(2.0 \times 10^{-9})}{5.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 7.2 \times 10^{3}\ \text{N/C},$$
>>pointing toward the thread, since $\lambda < 0$. The electron is negative, so the force points away from the thread. Its size is
>>$$F = eE = (1.602 \times 10^{-19})(7.2 \times 10^{3}) = 1.2 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{N}.$$
>>At twice the distance the field and the force are halved, to $5.8 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{N}$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not work out the finite rod. The next example follows Giancoli section 21.7.

>[!example] (Hard) A Finite Rod
>A rod of length $2L$ carries constant $\lambda$. Point P lies a distance $R$ from the rod's midpoint, on the perpendicular bisector.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field at P, and compare it with the infinite line for $\lambda = 3.0\ \text{nC/m}$, $L = 0.50\ \text{m}$, and $R = 0.20\ \text{m}$.
>>The derivation above holds with new limits, $y = -L$ to $y = L$. The bisector is a mirror plane, so the components along the rod still cancel. After the substitution $y = R\tan u$, the limits become $\sin u = \pm L/\sqrt{L^2 + R^2}$. The integral of $\cos u$ is $\sin u$, so
>>$$E = \frac{k\lambda}{R}\Big[\sin u\Big] = \frac{2k\lambda}{R}\cdot\frac{L}{\sqrt{L^2 + R^2}}.$$
>>The second factor is less than $1$ and approaches $1$ as $L \to \infty$. With the numbers,
>>$$E = \frac{2(8.99 \times 10^{9})(3.0 \times 10^{-9})}{0.20}\cdot\frac{0.50}{\sqrt{0.50^2 + 0.20^2}} = 270 \times 0.928 = 250\ \text{N/C}.$$
>>The finite rod gives $93\%$ of the infinite-line field.
