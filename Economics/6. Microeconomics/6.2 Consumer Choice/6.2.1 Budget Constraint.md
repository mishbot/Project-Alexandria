>[!note] Budget Constraint
>The **budget constraint** describes which bundles a consumer can afford. With two goods the commodity set is $X = \mathbb{R}^2_+$, so a bundle $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2)$ has $x_1 \ge 0$ and $x_2 \ge 0$. The consumer takes the prices $p_1, p_2 > 0$ and the income $M > 0$ as given. A bundle is affordable, or **feasible**, when
>$$p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 \le M, \qquad x_1 \ge 0, \quad x_2 \ge 0.$$
>The left side is the cost of the bundle at market prices. The set of feasible bundles is the **budget set**, the triangle with corners $(0,0)$, $(M/p_1, 0)$, and $(0, M/p_2)$. Its outer edge, where the consumer spends all income, is the **budget line** $p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 = M$. Solving for $x_2$ gives
>$$x_2 = \frac{M}{p_2} - \frac{p_1}{p_2}\, x_1 .$$
>The intercepts $M/p_1$ and $M/p_2$ are the most of each good the consumer can buy. The slope $-p_1/p_2$ is the **market rate of exchange**. One more unit of good $1$ costs $p_1/p_2$ units of good $2$.
>
>Each intercept moves only when a variable in its formula changes. This gives the standard shifts:
>
>| Change | $M/p_1$ | $M/p_2$ | Slope $-p_1/p_2$ | Picture |
>|---|---|---|---|---|
>| $M$ rises | rises | rises | same | parallel shift outward |
>| $p_1$ rises | falls | same | steeper | tilt inward, pivot at $(0, M/p_2)$ |
>| $p_2$ rises | same | falls | flatter | tilt inward, pivot at $(M/p_1, 0)$ |
>| $p_1$, $p_2$, $M$ all times $t > 0$ | same | same | same | no change |
>
>The budget constraint is the constraint of [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|utility maximization]] and depends only on prices and income, never on preferences.

# Intuition

A consumer wants more of the goods but has a fixed income, and prices set how far that income goes. Preferences describe what the consumer wants. The budget constraint describes what the consumer can reach. The two are separate objects. The budget set is drawn from prices and income alone, and preferences enter only when the consumer picks a point in it.

Every bundle on the budget line costs exactly $M$. Moving down the line trades good $2$ for good $1$ at a fixed rate set by the market. Bundles below the line leave money unspent, and bundles above it cost more than $M$. The hatched triangle below is the budget set.

Income and prices move the line in two different ways. More income lets the consumer buy more of both goods at the old rate of exchange, so the line moves out without turning. A higher price of good $1$ does not affect a consumer who buys only good $2$. So the $x_2$ intercept stays fixed, and the line turns around it.

# Properties

#### *(Combined Changes)* When several variables change at once, recompute both intercepts and join them.

The table in the callout covers one change at a time. Exam questions often move two variables together. A rise of $10\%$ in both $M$ and $p_1$ leaves $M/p_1$ unchanged and raises $M/p_2$. The line then pivots outward around the $x_1$ intercept, the same picture as a fall in $p_2$. A fall in $p_1$ tilts the line outward around the same point $(0, M/p_2)$ that a rise in $p_1$ keeps fixed. Two points fix a line, so the two new intercepts are always enough to draw it.

#### *(Only Ratios Matter)* A demand function that changes when all prices and income double is wrong.

Multiplying $p_1$, $p_2$, and $M$ by $t > 0$ gives $t p_1 x_1 + t p_2 x_2 \le tM$. Dividing by $t$ returns the old inequality, so the consumer faces the same choices. Only the price ratio $p_1/p_2$ and the ratios $M/p_1$ and $M/p_2$ enter the budget set. A general inflation that raises every price and every income by $3\%$ therefore changes nothing a consumer can buy. The optimal bundle cannot change either, which is the content of [[6.3.2 Homogeneity of Demand]]. Plugging $t\mathbf{p}$ and $tM$ into a derived demand function is a fast check on the algebra.

#### *(Market Rate vs Personal Rate)* The budget slope is the same for every consumer, and the indifference-curve slope is personal.

The budget slope is $-p_1/p_2$. Its absolute value $p_1/p_2$ is the opportunity cost of good $1$ in units of good $2$ (what the consumer gives up to get it). It comes from the market. The slope of an indifference curve is the [[6.1.4 Indifference Curves and MRS|MRS]]. It measures how much good $2$ one consumer would give up for one more unit of good $1$. At an interior optimum the two rates are equal, which is the tangency condition of [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]]. Where they differ, the consumer can trade along the line and gain. The same reading applies to time and land in [[6.5.1 Opportunity Cost]].

#### *(Endowment Budgets)* When income comes from selling an endowment, a price change also changes income, and the line pivots around the endowment point.

The rule "a change in $p_1$ pivots the line around $(0, M/p_2)$" assumes $M$ does not depend on prices. In [[6.5.2 Labor Supply]] a worker owns $24$ hours and sells some of them at the wage $w$. The budget is $pc + wl \le M + 24w$, where $c$ is consumption at price $p$ and $l$ is leisure. The right side rises with $w$. The bundle $l = 24$, $c = M/p$ is affordable at every wage, so a wage change pivots the line around that point. The budget in [[6.5.3 Intertemporal Choice]] has the same structure. The endowment $(M_0, M_1)$ stays affordable at every interest rate.

#### *(A Best Bundle Exists)* With positive prices a continuous utility function reaches a maximum on the budget set.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The existence argument below uses the extreme value theorem, which the lectures did not state for this problem.

A continuous function on a closed and bounded set attains a maximum. The budget set is closed because it includes its edges, and bounded because $x_i \le M/p_i$. So a consumer with a continuous utility function always has at least one best affordable bundle. The argument fails if a price is zero. The set then runs off to infinity in that good, and a consumer who always wants more has no best bundle. The budget set is also a [[2.1 Convex Sets|convex set]], because a weighted average of two affordable bundles is affordable. Together with convex and strictly monotone preferences, this convexity makes a tangency point the best bundle. [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions]] covers the cases where preferences are not convex.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Weekly Food Budget
>A student has $M = \$60$ a week for food. A meal kit to cook at home costs $p_1 = \$5$. A restaurant meal costs $p_2 = \$15$. Let $x_1$ be meal kits and $x_2$ restaurant meals per week.
>
>>[!example]- Find the intercepts and the slope of the budget line, say what the slope means, and decide whether the bundles $(6, 2)$ and $(9, 2)$ are affordable.
>>The budget line is $5x_1 + 15x_2 = 60$. The intercepts are $M/p_1 = 60/5 = 12$ meal kits and $M/p_2 = 60/15 = 4$ restaurant meals. The slope is $-p_1/p_2 = -5/15 = -1/3$. One more meal kit costs one third of a restaurant meal. Equivalently, one restaurant meal costs three meal kits.
>>
>>The bundle $(6, 2)$ costs $5 \cdot 6 + 15 \cdot 2 = 60$. It lies on the budget line and uses all the money. The bundle $(9, 2)$ costs $45 + 30 = 75 > 60$. It lies outside the budget set and is not affordable.

>[!example] (Medium) Which Intercept Stays Fixed
>The same student faces a rise in the meal-kit price from $\$5$ to $\$6$. Income stays at $\$60$ and restaurant meals stay at $\$15$.
>
>>[!example]- Does the budget line (a) tilt around the point $x_2 = M/p_2$, (b) tilt around the point $x_1 = M/p_1$, or (c) shift inward in parallel? Compare with a fall in income to $\$50$ at the old prices.
>>The answer is (a). The $x_2$ intercept $M/p_2 = 60/15 = 4$ does not contain $p_1$, so it stays fixed. The $x_1$ intercept falls from $12$ to $60/6 = 10$. The slope becomes $-6/15 = -0.4$, steeper than $-1/3$. A student who eats only at restaurants is not affected.
>>
>>A fall in income to $\$50$ is option (c). The intercepts become $50/5 = 10$ and $50/15 \approx 3.33$, and the slope stays $-1/3$. Both changes cut the maximum number of meal kits to $10$. Only the income cut also reduces the number of restaurant meals the student can afford.

>[!example] (Hard) A Raise That Comes With Higher Prices
>The student's weekly allowance rises by $20\%$, from $\$60$ to $\$72$. In the same week restaurants raise their price by $20\%$, from $\$15$ to $\$18$. Meal kits still cost $\$5$.
>
>>[!example]- Describe the new budget line. Is the student better off, worse off, or is it unclear?
>>The $x_2$ intercept is $72/18 = 4$, the same as before. The $x_1$ intercept is $72/5 = 14.4$, up from $12$. The line pivots outward around $(0, 4)$ and its slope flattens from $-1/3$ to $-5/18 \approx -0.28$. This is the picture of a fall in the meal-kit price. The same line would arise with the old income of $\$60$ and a meal-kit price of $60/14.4 \approx \$4.17$.
>>
>>The student cannot be worse off. Take any bundle that was affordable before, so $5x_1 + 15x_2 \le 60$ and $x_2 \le 4$. Its new cost is
>>$$5x_1 + 18x_2 = (5x_1 + 15x_2) + 3x_2 \le 60 + 12 = 72.$$
>>Every old bundle is still affordable, so the student is at least as well off. Suppose the student's old choice was on the budget line and included some meal kits, so $x_2 < 4$. After buying that bundle again the student has $72 - 60 - 3x_2 = 12 - 3x_2 > 0$ dollars left. With [[6.1.2 Monotone and Convex Preferences|strictly monotone preferences]] this student is strictly better off.
