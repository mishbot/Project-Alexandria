>[!note] Confidence Interval
>A **confidence interval** at level $1 - \alpha$ for a parameter $\theta$ is a random interval $[L(X), U(X)]$, constructed from the data $X$, such that
>$$\mathbb{P}_\theta(L(X) \leq \theta \leq U(X)) = 1 - \alpha$$
>for all values of $\theta$. The quantity $1 - \alpha$ is the **confidence level** (commonly $0.95$ or $0.99$).
>
>For the sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ of i.i.d. observations from a population with mean $\mu$, a $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval based on the normal approximation is
>$$\bar{X}_n \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \text{SE}(\bar{X}_n),$$
>where $z_{\alpha/2}$ is the critical value such that $\mathbb{P}(Z > z_{\alpha/2}) = \alpha/2$ for $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$. Common values: $z_{0.025} = 1.96$ (95%), $z_{0.005} = 2.576$ (99%).
>
>When the population standard deviation is unknown and $n$ is small, the normal quantile $z_{\alpha/2}$ is replaced by the [[2.2c.8 Student's t Distribution|Student's $t$]] quantile $t_{\alpha/2, n-1}$:
>$$\bar{X}_n \pm t_{\alpha/2,\, n-1} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}.$$
>
>>[!warning] Frequentist Interpretation
>>A $95\%$ confidence interval does **not** mean "$\theta$ falls in this interval with probability $0.95$." The parameter $\theta$ is a fixed (unknown) constant—it either lies in the interval or it does not. The correct interpretation is: if we repeated the experiment many times and constructed an interval each time, $95\%$ of those intervals would contain the true $\theta$.

# Intuition
A confidence interval translates the uncertainty captured by the [[1.3.1 Standard Error|standard error]] into a concrete range of plausible values for the unknown parameter. The wider the interval, the less precise our estimate; the narrower, the more informative the data.

The construction relies on the sampling distribution of the estimator. By the [[3.3 Central Limit Theorem|CLT]], $\bar{X}_n$ is approximately normal for large $n$, so the interval $\bar{X}_n \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \text{SE}$ captures the central $(1-\alpha)$ mass of that distribution. For small samples where the CLT approximation is rough, the $t$-distribution—which has heavier tails—provides wider and more honest intervals.

# Properties
#### (*Width*) The width of the interval is $2z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \text{SE} = 2z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sigma/\sqrt{n}$. It shrinks as $O(1/\sqrt{n})$.
#### (*Duality with Hypothesis Testing*) A parameter value $\theta_0$ lies inside the $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval if and only if the [[1.4 Hypothesis Testing|hypothesis test]] of $H_0 : \theta = \theta_0$ at level $\alpha$ fails to reject.
#### (*Bootstrap Confidence Intervals*) When the sampling distribution of $\hat\Theta_n$ is unknown or non-normal, the [[1.5.1 Classical Bootstrap|bootstrap]] can construct confidence intervals without relying on the CLT—using either the **percentile method** (take the $\alpha/2$ and $1-\alpha/2$ quantiles of the bootstrap distribution) or the **bootstrap-$t$** method.
#### (*Bayesian Analogue*) The Bayesian counterpart is the **credible interval**, derived from the posterior $p(\theta \mid X)$. Unlike confidence intervals, credible intervals can be interpreted as probability statements about $\theta$ (conditional on the observed data and the prior).

# Examples
>[!example] Estimating a Population Mean
>A sample of $n = 25$ light bulbs has mean lifetime $\bar{x} = 1200$ hours with sample standard deviation $s = 100$ hours. Construct a $95\%$ confidence interval for the true mean lifetime $\mu$.
>>[!example]- Solution
>>Since $n = 25$ is moderate and $\sigma$ is unknown, use the $t$-distribution with $\nu = 24$ degrees of freedom. The critical value is $t_{0.025, 24} \approx 2.064$. The interval is
>>$$1200 \pm 2.064 \cdot \frac{100}{\sqrt{25}} = 1200 \pm 2.064 \cdot 20 = 1200 \pm 41.3,$$
>>giving $[1158.7, \; 1241.3]$. We are $95\%$ confident that the true mean lifetime lies in this range.

>[!example] Proportion Confidence Interval
>In a survey of $n = 500$ voters, $\hat{p} = 0.58$ support a policy. Construct a $99\%$ confidence interval for the true proportion $p$.
>>[!example]- Solution
>>The estimated standard error is $\widehat{\text{SE}}(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{0.58 \cdot 0.42 / 500} \approx 0.0221$. With $z_{0.005} = 2.576$,
>>$$0.58 \pm 2.576 \cdot 0.0221 = 0.58 \pm 0.057,$$
>>giving $[0.523, \; 0.637]$. Even at the $99\%$ level, the interval excludes $0.5$, providing strong evidence that the majority supports the policy.
