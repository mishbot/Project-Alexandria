>[!note] Cylindrical Capacitor
>A **cylindrical capacitor** is a rod of radius $a$ on the axis of a tube of inner radius $b$. Both are conductors, and both have length $L$. A **coaxial cable** is the standard example. With charges $\pm Q$, the charge per unit length is $\lambda = Q/L$. For $L \gg b$ the capacitance is
>$$C = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0 L}{\ln(b/a)},$$
>and the capacitance per unit length is $C/L = 2\pi\varepsilon_0/\ln(b/a)$. Between the conductors the field is radial with size $E = \lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$, where $r$ is the distance from the axis. The potential difference has size
>$$V = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{b}{a},$$
>with the positive conductor at the higher potential.

>Lecture did not cover the cylindrical capacitor or anything in this note. The note follows Giancoli section 24.2.

# Intuition

The field between the conductors is the field of a line charge. It falls as $1/r$, so the potential difference grows as a logarithm of the radii. Only the ratio $b/a$ enters, because scaling both radii by the same factor leaves the shape unchanged. The capacitance grows in proportion to the length. A cable twice as long holds twice the charge at the same potential difference.

A Gaussian cylinder grows from the rod to the tube, and the graph traces $E \propto 1/r$. The green area under the curve is $V$. Then $b$ doubles twice, and each doubling adds the same area, $\ln 2$.

# Derivation

Follow the recipe of [[3.5.1. Capacitance]]. Put $+Q$ on the rod and $-Q$ on the tube. Take a Gaussian cylinder of radius $r$ and length $\ell$, with $a < r < b$. It encloses charge $\lambda\ell$. [[3.3.2b Cylindrical Symmetry]] gives the outward field
$$E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}.$$
Integrate along a radial line from the rod at $r = a$ to the tube at $r = b$:
$$V_b - V_a = -\int_a^b \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\,dr = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{b}{a}.$$
The tube is lower, as expected for the negative conductor. Dividing $Q = \lambda L$ by the size of the potential difference gives $C = 2\pi\varepsilon_0 L/\ln(b/a)$.

# Properties

#### *(Weak Dependence on the Radii)* The radii enter only through $\ln(b/a)$, so large changes in them change $C$ only a little.

Doubling $b/a$ from $4$ to $8$ raises $\ln(b/a)$ from $1.39$ to $2.08$. The capacitance per unit length falls only from $40.1$ to $26.7\ \text{pF/m}$.

#### *(Thin-Gap Limit)* When the gap $b - a$ is small compared with $a$, the result becomes the parallel-plate formula.

Write $b/a = 1 + (b-a)/a$. For a small gap, $\ln(1 + x) \approx x$, so $\ln(b/a) \approx (b-a)/a$. Then $C \approx \varepsilon_0(2\pi aL)/(b-a)$. This is $\varepsilon_0 A/d$ with $A = 2\pi aL$, the rod's surface area, and $d = b - a$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) An Air-Filled Coaxial Cable
>A cable has a central wire of radius $0.50\ \text{mm}$ and a tube of inner radius $2.0\ \text{mm}$, with air between them. It is $10\ \text{m}$ long.
>
>>[!example]- Find its capacitance per unit length and its total capacitance.
>>The ratio is $b/a = 4.0$, and $\ln 4.0 = 1.386$. The capacitance per unit length is
>>$$\frac{C}{L} = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0}{\ln(b/a)} = \frac{5.56 \times 10^{-11}}{1.386} = 4.01 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{F/m} = 40.1\ \text{pF/m}.$$
>>For $10\ \text{m}$ the capacitance is $401\ \text{pF}$.

>[!example] (Medium) Designing a Cable
>A cable with a central wire of radius $0.50\ \text{mm}$ and air insulation must have $50\ \text{pF/m}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the inner radius of the tube.
>>Solving $C/L = 2\pi\varepsilon_0/\ln(b/a)$ for the logarithm gives
>>$$\ln\frac{b}{a} = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0}{C/L} = \frac{5.56 \times 10^{-11}}{50 \times 10^{-12}} = 1.112.$$
>>Then $b/a = e^{1.112} = 3.04$, so $b = (3.04)(0.50\ \text{mm}) = 1.52\ \text{mm}$.
