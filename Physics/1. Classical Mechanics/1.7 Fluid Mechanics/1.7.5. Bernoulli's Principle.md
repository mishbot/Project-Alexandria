>[!note] Bernoulli's Principle
>The [[1.7.4. Fluid Dynamics|flow of an ideal fluid]] (i.e., steady stream, no energy losses) also conserves energy. As a small element moves along a streamline, its pressure can do work, its height changes gravitational potential energy, and its motion changes kinetic energy. The sum of these per unit volume stays constant, forming **Bernoulli’s equation**:
>$$P + \rho g y + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2 = C.$$
>Here, $P$ is the pressure that the fluid exerts on the walls of the container, $\rho g y$ represents gravitational potential energy at height $y$ from the reference point, and $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2$ is kinetic energy. Faster flow corresponds to lower pressure if height is unchanged.
>
>This relationship explains effects such as the **Venturi effect**, where pressure drops in a constricted region according to
>$$P_1 - P_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho(v_2^2 - v_1^2),$$
>and **Torricelli’s law**, which gives the efflux speed from a tank as
>$$v = \sqrt{2gh}.$$
>Bernoulli’s principle thus extends the conservation laws of mechanics to fluids, linking pressure, motion, and height in ideal flow. Once viscosity or turbulence appears, additional energy losses must be considered, marking the transition from ideal to real fluid behavior.

>[!example] (Easy) Waterworks
>Water is brought from street level to an office building using a pump that pushes the water up through a pipe. A faucet was left open on the top floor, a height $H$ above the pump, as shown in the figure below. The pipe has a cross-sectional area $A_{1}$ after the pump and then tapers down to $A_{2}$. The gauge pressure at street level is $P_{g}$, and the initial flow velocity is $v_{1}$. Take $\rho$ to be the density of water. You may assume that the flow is steady and non-turbulent, and viscosity can be neglected.
>
>![[Screenshot 2025-11-03 at 17.43.12.png|200]]
>
>>[!example]- Determine the speed $v_{2}$ of the water in the thinner pipe.
>>Per the continuity equation, we have $A_{1}v_{1}=A_{2}v_{2} \implies v_{2}=\frac{A_{1}}{A_{2}}v_{1}$.
>
>>[!example]- Determine the absolute pressure $P_{2}$ in the top horizontal section of the thinner pipe.
>>Per the Bernoulli principle, 
>>$$P_{2}+\rho g H + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_{2}^2 = P_{g}+P_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\rho v_{1}^2,$$
>>such that $$P_{2} = P_{g} + P_{0} - \rho gH + \frac{1}{2}\rho\left( \frac{{A_{2}-A_{1}}}{A_{2}} \right)v_{1}^2.$$

>[!example] (Easy) Water Faucet
>The stream of water with initial speed $v_{0}$ from a faucet with radius $R$ decreases in radius as it falls.
>
>![[Screenshot 2025-11-03 at 1.24.49.png|100]]
>
>>[!example]- Derive the equation for the radius $r$ of the stream as a function of the distance $y$ below the faucet.
>>We begin with the continuity equation, which relates the baseline to any point with radius $r$, such that $\pi R^2v_{0}=\pi r^2v_{1}$. Bernoulli's principle then states that
>>$$P_{0}+\rho gh + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_{0}^2 = P_{0} + \rho g (h-y)+\frac{1}{2}\rho v_{1}^2,$$
>>where the $P_{0}$ term cancels out because the stream is always surrounded by solely atmospheric pressure (i.e., any force is not propagated since we take the exact end of the faucet to be the point with radius $R$ and speed $v_{0}$, and the water is not in hydrostatic equilibrium at that point). As such, the expression becomes
>>$$\rho v_{0}^2=\frac{\rho R^4}{r^4}v_{0}^2-2\rho gy \implies r(y)=R\left( \frac{v_{0}^2}{v_{0}^2+2gy} \right)^{1/4}.$$

>[!example] (Medium) Syringe
>Shown below is a syringe with a liquid whose density is $\rho$. The barrel of the syringe has a cross sectional area $A$, and the needle has a cross-sectional area $a$. The atmospheric pressure is $P_{0}$. A force of magnitude $F$ acts on the plunger, making the liquid flow out from the needle horizontally.
>
>![[Screenshot 2025-11-02 at 23.46.49.png|350]]
>
>>[!example]- Determine the speed of the liquid as it leaves the needle’s tip.
>>Per the continuity equation, we have that $Av_{A}=av_{B}$. Per Bernoulli's principle, we also have that
>>$$P_{A}+\frac{1}{2}\rho v_{A}^2=P_{B}+\frac{1}{2}\rho v_{B}^2.$$
>>
>>Here, we regard both the atmospheric pressure $P_{0}$, as well as the pressure exerted from the force $F$, such that $F=P / A$ since the force is normal to the surface area of the tube. As such, $P_{A}=P_{0}+F / A$ per Pascal's principle. However, this force is not passed onto the needle since it is not in hydrostatic equilibrium. As such, $P_{B}=P_{0}$. We conclude that
>>$$P_{0}+ \frac{F}{A} + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_{A}^2=P_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\rho v_{B}^2 \implies v_{B}=\sqrt{ \frac{2FA}{\rho(A^2-a^2)} }.$$

>[!example] (Hard) Connected Tank
>Two very large tanks are open to the atmosphere at the top and contain an incompressible fluid of density $\rho$. They are connected to each other as shown in the figure below. The fluid flows in the horizontal pipe and exits at point $D$, where the pipe is open to the atmosphere. 
>
>Between points $B$ and $D$, there is a region of the pipe where the cross-sectional area is half of what it is at $B$ and $D$. 
>
>In the lower part of the device, the fluid does not flow but can enter the vertical tube to react to the pressure at point $C$. You may consider that there is a vacuum between points $C$ and $E$.
>
>![[Screenshot 2025-11-03 at 15.54.25.png|300]]
>
>>[!example]- Determine the approximate speed $v_{A}$ of the fluid at point $A$.
>>We apply the continuity equation, such that $Av_{A}=A'v_{B}$. Since $A\gg A'$, we deduce that $v_{A} \approx 0$.
>
>>[!example]- Determine the speed $v_{D}$ of the fluid at point $D$.
>>Note that both $A$ and $D$ are exposed to atmospheric pressure. Applying Bernoulli's principle,
>>$$P_{0}+\rho gh_{A} + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_{A}^2 = \rho g h_{D} + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_{D}^2.$$
>>As noted before, $P_{0}$ cancels out, and $v_{A}=0$, which leaves us with
>>$$gh_{A}=gh_{D}+\frac{1}{2}v_{D}^2 \implies v_{D}=\sqrt{ 2gh_{1} },$$
>>where it is given to us that $h_{1}=h_{A}-h_{D}$.
>
>>[!example]- Determine the speed $v_{B}$ and pressure $P_{B}$ of the fluid at point $B$.
>>We note that since the tubes at point $B$ and $D$ have the same cross-sectional area, then by the continuity equation, $A'v_{B}=A'v_{D} \implies v_{B}=v_{D}=\sqrt{ 2gh_{1} }$ based on the answer from the previous subquestion.
>>
>>Then, we apply Bernoulli's principle, such that
>>$$P_{B}+\rho gh+\frac{1}{2}\rho v_{D}^2=P_{0}+\rho gh+\frac{1}{2}\rho v_{D}^2,$$
>>where $h=h_{D}=h_{B}=$. As such, all terms cancel out, and we are left with $P_{B}=P_{0}$, the atmospheric pressure.
>>
>>Intuitively, we can think about this from the perspective of Pascal's principle. Since both tubes A and B are in hydrostatic equilibrium, it follows that the atmospheric pressure $P_0$ on tank A gets transmitted onto tube B.
>
>>[!example]- Determine the speed $v_{C}$ and pressure $P_{C}$ of the fluid in the pipe at point $C$.
>>Applying the continuity equation, $A'v_{B}=\frac{A'}{2}v_{C} \implies v_{C}=2v_{B}$.
>>
>>Then, applying Bernoulli's principle,
>>$$P_{0}+\rho gh + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_{B}^2 = P_{C} + \rho g h + \frac{1}{2}\rho 4v_{B}^{2},$$
>>where $h_{B} \approx h_{C}=h=0$.
>>
>>Solving for $v_{B}^2=2gh_{1}$ as found previously, we get
>>$$P_{C}=P_{0}-3\rho gh_{1}.$$
>
>>[!example]- Determine the height $h_{2}$ of fluid in the vertical tube.
>>We begin by noting that the vacuum between $C$ and $E$ implies that the pressure at those two points is the same. Therefore, using Bernoulli's principle,
>>$$P_{0}=P_{0}-3\rho gh_{1} + \rho g h_{2} \implies h_{2}=3h_{1},$$
>>where point $F$ is regarded to be the baseline (zero) height.

>[!example] (Hard) Connected Tank II
>A non-viscous, incompressible fluid of density $\rho$ flows through a tube that bends at a $90°$ angle with a steady volumetric flow rate $Q$. The tube has diameter $d_1$ in the horizontal section and $d_2$ in the vertical section. 
>
>Points 1 and 2 along the flow are connected to a thinner U-shaped tube (a manometer) containing a denser fluid of density $\rho_m$, where $\rho_m > \rho$. The system is in static equilibrium within the manometer. 
>
>Let $H$ denote the height difference between the two fluid columns in the manometer, corresponding to points 1 and 2.
>
![[Screenshot 2025-11-03 at 18.35.46.png|250]]
>
>>[!example]- Determine the flow velocity of the fluid at point 1 and point 2.
>>Since the fluid is incompressible, it follows that $A_{1}v_{1}=A_{2}v_{2}=Q$. We have that $A_{1}=\frac{\pi d_{1}^2}{4}$ and $A_{2}=\frac{\pi d_{2}^2}{4}$. Therefore,
>>$$v_{1}=\frac{4Q}{\pi d_{1}^2}, \quad v_{2}=\frac{4Q}{\pi d_{2}^2}.$$
>
>>[!example]- Determine the pressure difference $P_{1}-P_{2}$.
>>Per Bernoulli's equation, we have
>>$$P_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\rho v_{1}^2 = P_{2} + mgH + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_{2}^2.$$
>>Since we know the values of $v_{1}$ and $v_{2}$ from the previous subpart, it follows that
>>$$P_{1}-P_{2}=\rho gH + \frac{8Q^2}{\pi^2}\rho\left( \frac{1}{d_{2}^4} - \frac{1}{d_{1}^4} \right).$$
>
>>[!example]- Determine the pressure difference $P_{1}-P_{2}$ using the static fluids in the manometer tube. You may use the unknown $h$ in your answer.
>>We utilize the hydrostatic law $P(h)=P_{0}+\rho gh$. We label the left side of the manometer fluid as point 3. Since we are going upwards from point $1$ there is a decrease in pressure, and then an increase as we go down to point 3. As such $P_{3}=P_{1}-\rho gH+\rho gh$. Similarly, we proceed from point 2, such that $P_{3}=P_{2}+\rho_{m}gh$. Equating, we get
>>$$P_{1}-\rho gH + \rho gh = P_{2}+\rho_{m}gh \implies P_{1}-P_{2}=\rho gH + (\rho_{m}-\rho)gh.$$
>
>>[!example]- Determine the height difference $h$ in the manometer tube.
>>We equate the two equations from the last 2 subparts:
>>$$\rho gH + \frac{8Q^2}{\pi^2}\rho\left( \frac{1}{d_{2}^4} - \frac{1}{d_{1}^4} \right)=\rho gH + (\rho_{m}-\rho)gh.$$
>>As such, we get
>>$$h=\frac{8Q^2\rho}{\pi^2(\rho_{m}-\rho)g}\left( \frac{1}{d_{2}^4} - \frac{1}{d_{1}^4} \right).$$
