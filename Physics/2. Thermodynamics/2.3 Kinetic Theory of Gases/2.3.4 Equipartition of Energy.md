>[!note] Equipartition of Energy
>The **equipartition theorem** states that in thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$, every accessible degree of freedom of a molecule holds an average energy of
>$$\tfrac{1}{2}\, kT.$$
>A **degree of freedom** is an independent way for the molecule to store energy: motion along one axis, rotation about one axis, or one vibration counted twice, once for its kinetic energy and once for its potential energy. A gas of $N$ molecules with $d$ accessible degrees of freedom each has a total molecular energy, written $E_{\text{int}}$, of
>$$E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2}\, NkT = \tfrac{d}{2}\, nRT,$$
>and that energy changes by $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2} Nk\, \Delta T$ whatever the process. The next section names $E_{\text{int}}$ the [[2.4.1 Internal Energy|internal energy]] and builds on it.
>
>The count $d$ is the number of degrees of freedom that the available energy can excite, not the number that exist. A monatomic gas has $d = 3$, for the three directions of motion. A diatomic gas between roughly $-100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $800\ ^\circ\text{C}$ has $d = 5$, three translations and two rotations about axes perpendicular to the bond. Above that range the bond vibrates and $d$ rises toward $7$. Below it the rotations freeze out and $d$ falls to $3$. A solid has $d = 6$, three directions of motion and three spring-like bonds, so $E_{\text{int}} = 3NkT$. That count is what makes the heat needed to warm a solid, in the next section, come out to $3R$ per mole, the Dulong-Petit value.
>
>| Substance | Accessible degrees of freedom $d$ | $E_{\text{int}}$ |
>|---|---|---|
>| Monatomic gas (He, Ne, Ar) | $3$ | $\tfrac{3}{2} NkT$ |
>| Diatomic gas near room temperature (N$_2$, O$_2$, H$_2$) | $5$ | $\tfrac{5}{2} NkT$ |
>| Diatomic gas above about $800\ ^\circ\text{C}$ | $7$ | $\tfrac{7}{2} NkT$ |
>| Solid near room temperature | $6$ | $3 NkT$ |
>
>Translation never freezes out, so $d$ is never below $3$ for a gas. The theorem is classical, and the freeze-out is a quantum effect: rotation and vibration come in energy steps, and a step larger than $kT$ cannot be taken by a typical molecule.

# Intuition

The [[2.3.3 Maxwell Speed Distribution|Maxwell distribution]] gives each direction of motion an average kinetic energy of $\tfrac{1}{2} kT$, and the three directions add to $\tfrac{3}{2} kT$. Nothing in that calculation used the fact that the energy was translational. The Boltzmann factor $e^{-E/kT}$ weights every state by its energy, and any energy that is a square of a coordinate or a velocity gives the same $\tfrac{1}{2} kT$ when averaged. Rotation about an axis has kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2} I \omega^2$, a square. A vibrating bond has kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2} \mu \dot{x}^2$ and potential energy $\tfrac{1}{2} \kappa x^2$, two squares. Each square is one degree of freedom and takes one share of $\tfrac{1}{2} kT$. The energy of a gas is shared equally among all the ways its molecules can move, which is what the name says. The average energy of one molecule is the sum of its parts,
$$\overline{E} = \overline{\tfrac{1}{2} M v_{\text{cm}}^2} + \overline{E_{\text{rot}}} + \overline{E_{\text{vib}}} = \tfrac{3}{2} kT + \overline{E_{\text{rot}}} + \overline{E_{\text{vib}}},$$
where $M$ is the mass of the molecule and $v_{\text{cm}}$ is the speed of its center of mass. Each accessible square term in the last two parts adds another $\tfrac{1}{2} kT$.

The count for a diatomic molecule needs care. Its center of mass moves in three directions. It rotates about two axes perpendicular to the bond, each with a sizeable [[1.5.2. Rotational Inertia|rotational inertia]] $2m(L/2)^2$ set by the bond length $L$. Rotation about the bond axis itself does not count. A point mass $m$ at distance $r$ from an axis has $I = mr^2$. Nearly all of an atom's mass sits in its tiny nucleus, and both nuclei lie on the bond axis. So $r$ and $I$ for that axis are almost zero. The same argument gives a single atom no rotation at all, which is why a monatomic gas has $d = 3$. Vibration along the bond would add two more. So the full count is $3 + 2 + 2 = 7$. At room temperature the measured value is $5$, because the bond is a stiff spring, and a molecule needs an energy step far larger than $kT$ to start it vibrating. The sketch shows the modes.

The freeze-out order follows the size of the energy steps. Vibration has the largest steps, so it is the first to disappear as the gas cools. Rotation has smaller steps and survives to lower temperature. Translation has steps so small that they never matter, so a gas always has at least $d = 3$. A few billion molecules out of $10^{22}$ may be vibrating at room temperature, and they make no difference to the average.

A molecule such as $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ or $\text{CH}_4$ has atoms that do not lie on one line. No axis passes through every nucleus. It rotates about all three axes. A rigid nonlinear molecule therefore has $d = 3 + 3 = 6$, so $E_{\text{int}} = 3NkT$ and $C_V = 3R$ per mole. A linear molecule such as $\text{CO}_2$ counts like a diatomic one, with two rotations.

# Properties

#### *(Energy Is Shared Equally)* Every accessible degree of freedom, whatever its physical nature, holds the same $\tfrac{1}{2} kT$ on average.

A heavy molecule and a light one, a translation and a rotation, all take the same share. The share is set by the temperature and by nothing else. This is why the [[2.3.1 Molecular Interpretation of Temperature|kinetic energy per molecule]] is the same for helium and for nitrogen at the same temperature. The share is an average over molecules and over time. A single molecule at a single instant can have any energy, and the [[2.3.3 Maxwell Speed Distribution|distribution]] says how likely each value is.

#### *(Only Accessible Modes Count)* The count $d$ is set by comparing $kT$ with the energy step of each mode.

Quantum mechanics allows a rotation or a vibration only in steps of a definite size. When $kT$ is much larger than the step, the mode behaves classically and takes its $\tfrac{1}{2} kT$. When $kT$ is much smaller than the step, a typical molecule cannot afford even the first step and the mode holds no energy. The transition is gradual, and the diagram shows it as a smooth rise rather than a jump. For nitrogen the rotational step corresponds to about $3\ \text{K}$ and the vibrational step to about $3400\ \text{K}$, so at room temperature rotation is fully active and vibration is fully frozen.

#### *(Temperature Alone)* The energy of an ideal gas depends on $T$ and $d$, and on nothing else.

Pressure and volume enter only through $PV = NkT$. Two samples of the same gas at the same temperature have the same energy per molecule, whether one is compressed and the other expanded. Substituting $NkT = PV$ gives $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2} PV$, so a change in energy can be read from the pressures and volumes at the start and end as $\tfrac{d}{2}(P_2 V_2 - P_1 V_1)$. Later sections use both facts on every page: a change at fixed temperature leaves $E_{\text{int}}$ alone, and any change can be read from its endpoints.

#### *(Heat Capacity Measures $d$)* Measuring how much energy a gas needs per kelvin counts its degrees of freedom.

Warming a gas in a rigid container by $\Delta T$ adds $\tfrac{d}{2} Nk\, \Delta T$ to its energy, so the energy per mole per kelvin is $\tfrac{d}{2} R$, a quantity later called the [[2.5.4 Molar Specific Heats|molar specific heat at constant volume]] $C_V$. Helium gives $12.5\ \text{J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K})$, which is $\tfrac{3}{2} R$. Nitrogen gives $20.8$, which is $\tfrac{5}{2} R$. The numbers were measured long before anyone knew what a molecule looked like, and they were the first evidence that diatomic molecules rotate but do not vibrate at room temperature. The failure of the classical count at low temperature was one of the puzzles that led to quantum mechanics.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Energy in the Air of a Room
>A room measures $4.0 \times 5.0 \times 3.0\ \text{m}$ and holds air at $1\ \text{atm}$ and $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the internal energy of the air.
>>Air is diatomic, so $d = 5$ and $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{5}{2} NkT = \tfrac{5}{2} PV$. The number of molecules and the temperature are not needed separately,
>>$$E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{5}{2}\,(1.013 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa})(60\ \text{m}^3) = 1.5 \times 10^{7}\ \text{J}.$$
>>That is $15\ \text{MJ}$, the energy in about half a liter of gasoline. It cannot be extracted as work, because there is nothing colder nearby to dump heat into. The [[2.7.2 Second Law of Thermodynamics|second law]] explains why.

>[!example] (Medium) Same Heat, Different Gases
>One mole of helium and one mole of nitrogen each receive $1000\ \text{J}$ of heat in rigid containers.
>
>>[!example]- Find the temperature rise of each.
>>At constant volume no work is done, so all the heat becomes internal energy, $Q = \tfrac{d}{2} nR\, \Delta T$. For helium $d = 3$,
>>$$\Delta T = \frac{Q}{\tfrac{3}{2} nR} = \frac{1000}{(1.5)(1)(8.314)} = 80\ \text{K}.$$
>>For nitrogen $d = 5$ and $\Delta T = 1000 / (2.5 \times 8.314) = 48\ \text{K}$. The nitrogen warms less because two fifths of the heat goes into rotation, which a thermometer does not see. Temperature measures the translational share only.

>[!example] (Hard) How Fast a Molecule Spins
>A nitrogen molecule has two atoms of mass $2.33 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg}$ separated by $0.110\ \text{nm}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the rms angular speed about one axis perpendicular to the bond at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>>Each atom sits at $L/2 = 0.055\ \text{nm}$ from the center, so the rotational inertia is
>>$$I = 2m\left(\tfrac{L}{2}\right)^2 = 2\,(2.33 \times 10^{-26})(0.055 \times 10^{-9})^2 = 1.41 \times 10^{-46}\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2.$$
>>Rotation about this axis is one degree of freedom, so $\tfrac{1}{2} I\, \overline{\omega^2} = \tfrac{1}{2} kT$ and
>>$$\omega_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{kT}{I}} = \sqrt{\frac{(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(293)}{1.41 \times 10^{-46}}} = 5.4 \times 10^{12}\ \text{rad/s},$$
>>about $10^{12}$ revolutions per second. Rotation about the bond axis would have a far smaller $I$, because nearly all the mass sits in the two nuclei, which lie on that axis. Its energy steps are correspondingly larger than $kT$, and the mode stays frozen at every temperature the molecule survives.
