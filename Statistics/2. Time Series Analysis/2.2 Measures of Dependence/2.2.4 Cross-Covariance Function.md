>[!note] Cross-Covariance Function
>The **cross-covariance function** between two [[2.1. Time Series|time series]] $\{X_t\}$ and $\{Y_t\}$ measures the linear dependence between $X_s$ and $Y_t$ at possibly different time points, defined as
>$$\gamma_{XY}(s, t) = \text{Cov}(X_s, Y_t) = \mathbb{E}[(X_s - \mu_X(s))(Y_t - \mu_Y(t))],$$
>where $\mu_X(s) = \mathbb{E}[X_s]$ and $\mu_Y(t) = \mathbb{E}[Y_t]$ are the respective [[2.2.1 Mean Function|mean functions]]. This extends the [[2.2.2 Autocovariance Function|autocovariance function]] from a single series to a pair of series, and is the time series analogue of [[2.5b Second-Order Moments|covariance]] between two random variables.
>
>When both processes are **jointly stationary**, the cross-covariance depends only on the lag $h = s - t$:
>$$\gamma_{XY}(h) = \text{Cov}(X_{t+h}, Y_t) \quad \text{for all } t.$$
>
>Unlike autocovariance, the cross-covariance is **not symmetric** in the lag: $\gamma_{XY}(h) \neq \gamma_{XY}(-h)$ in general. Instead, swapping the roles of $X$ and $Y$ reverses the lag: $\gamma_{XY}(h) = \gamma_{YX}(-h)$.
>
>>[!warning] Direction Matters
>>The sign of $h$ encodes **lead-lag structure**. If $\gamma_{XY}(h)$ is large at $h > 0$, then $X$ leads $Y$ (values of $X$ covary with future values of $Y$). If $\gamma_{XY}(h)$ is large at $h < 0$, then $Y$ leads $X$. This asymmetry is what makes cross-covariance useful for identifying causal or predictive relationships between series.

# Intuition
The cross-covariance function answers: **does knowing the value of series $X$ at time $s$ tell you anything about series $Y$ at time $t$?** While [[2.2.2 Autocovariance Function|autocovariance]] looks at memory within a single series, cross-covariance looks at **coupling between two series** across time.

This is essential in multivariate time series analysis. For example, does a change in interest rates ($X$) predict a future change in stock prices ($Y$)? If $\gamma_{XY}(h)$ peaks at a positive lag $h$, it suggests that $X$ leads $Y$ by $h$ periods. The cross-covariance quantifies the strength and timing of such lead-lag relationships, though like all covariance measures, it captures only **linear** associations.

# Properties
#### (*Asymmetry*) $\gamma_{XY}(h) = \gamma_{YX}(-h)$, but $\gamma_{XY}(h) \neq \gamma_{XY}(-h)$ in general.
This reflects the fact that "$X$ leads $Y$" is a different statement from "$Y$ leads $X$."

#### (*Autocovariance as Special Case*) When $Y_t = X_t$, the cross-covariance reduces to the autocovariance: $\gamma_{XX}(h) = \gamma_X(h)$.
#### (*Linearity*) If $Z_t = aX_t + bY_t$, then
$$\gamma_{ZW}(h) = a\,\gamma_{XW}(h) + b\,\gamma_{YW}(h)$$
for any third series $\{W_t\}$.

#### (*Scale Dependence*) Like autocovariance, $\gamma_{XY}(h)$ has units of $(\text{units of } X) \times (\text{units of } Y)$ and is not directly comparable across different pairs of series. The [[2.2.5 Cross-Correlation Function|cross-correlation function]] $\rho_{XY}(h)$ normalizes this.
#### (*Independence*) If $\{X_t\}$ and $\{Y_t\}$ are independent, then $\gamma_{XY}(h) = 0$ for all $h$. The converse does not hold in general (zero cross-covariance implies only linear uncorrelatedness).

# Examples
>[!example] Lagged Signal
>Let $Y_t = X_{t-d} + W_t$, where $\{X_t\}$ is a stationary signal with autocovariance $\gamma_X(h)$, $\{W_t\} \sim \text{WN}(0, \sigma^2_W)$ is independent of $\{X_t\}$, and $d > 0$ is a fixed delay.
>>[!example]- Derive the cross-covariance $\gamma_{XY}(h)$.
>>We have
>>$$\gamma_{XY}(h) = \text{Cov}(X_{t+h}, Y_t) = \text{Cov}(X_{t+h}, X_{t-d} + W_t) = \text{Cov}(X_{t+h}, X_{t-d}) = \gamma_X(h + d),$$
>>since $W_t$ is independent of $X$. The cross-covariance is maximized when $h = -d$, because $\gamma_X(h+d)$ is largest at $h + d = 0$. This correctly identifies that $Y$ **lags** $X$ by $d$ periods: the peak at negative $h$ means values of $Y$ are most strongly associated with **past** values of $X$.

>[!example] Uncorrelated but Dependent Series
>Let $X_t \sim \text{WN}(0, 1)$ and $Y_t = X_t^2$.
>>[!example]- Show that $\gamma_{XY}(0) = 0$ despite $Y_t$ being a deterministic function of $X_t$.
>>We have
>>$$\gamma_{XY}(0) = \text{Cov}(X_t, X_t^2) = \mathbb{E}[X_t^3] - \mathbb{E}[X_t]\mathbb{E}[X_t^2].$$
>>Since $X_t$ has a symmetric distribution around $0$, $\mathbb{E}[X_t^3] = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[X_t] = 0$, so $\gamma_{XY}(0) = 0$. The two series are **linearly uncorrelated** at every lag, yet $Y_t$ is entirely determined by $X_t$. This illustrates that cross-covariance captures only linear relationships.
