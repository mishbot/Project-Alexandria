>[!note] Poisson Process
>A **Poisson process** $N \sim PP(\lambda)$ for some **arrival rate** $\lambda > 0$ is a continuous-time stochastic process $N=\{ N_{t} \}_{t \geq 0}$, such that each $N_{t} \sim \operatorname{Pois}(\lambda t)$ is a [[2.2b.9 Poisson Distribution|Poisson random variable]] that denotes the number of arrivals at time $t$,
>$$N_{t}=\max\{n \geq 0: T_{n} \leq t  \},$$ 
>where $T_{n}=\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1}S_{i} \sim \operatorname{Erlang}(n, \lambda)$ is the cumulative time (i.e., [[2.2c.4 Exponential Distribution|the sum of n Exponential]], independent, and identically distributed arrival times $S_{i} \sim \operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)$) from $t=0$ at which the $n$-th event occurs:

>>[!warning] Memorylessness
>>The Poisson process inherits the [[2.2b.9 Poisson Distribution|memoryless property]] from the Poisson (through the $N_{t}$'s) and Exponential distribution (through the $S_{i}$'s). Note that the Erlang-distributed $T_{n}$'s **are not memoryless**. 
# Rationale
At the core of Poisson Processes are the independent and identically distributed inter-arrival times $S_{i}$. Given that they are Exponentially distributed, we can determine the distribution of every arrival $N_{t}$. We proceed as follows:




# Properties
#### (*Stationary Increments*) $N_{t+\tau}-N_{t} \sim \operatorname{Pois}(\lambda \tau)$.

#### (*Independent Increments*) All $I_{t}=N_{t}-N_{t-1}$ are mutually independent.
#### (*Small Increments*) $\mathbb{P}(N_{t+dt}-N_{t}=k)=\begin{dcases}1-\lambda dt+o(dt) & \text{if } k=0, \\ \lambda dt + o(dt) & \text{if } k=1, \\ o(dt) & \text{if } k \geq 2.\end{dcases}$ 
We know that $I_{t+dt}=N_{t+dt}-N_{t} \sim \operatorname{Pois}(\lambda dt)$. Given this, we have:
$$p_{I_{t+dt}}(k)=e^{-\lambda dt}\frac{(\lambda dt)^k}{k!}=\begin{cases}e^{-\lambda dt} & k=0, \\ \lambda dte^{-\lambda dt} & k=1, \\ \frac{1}{2}(\lambda dt)^2e^{-\lambda dt} & k \geq 2.\end{cases}$$
When $k=0$, 

#### (*Poisson Merging*) Let $N \sim \operatorname{PP}(\lambda)$ and $M \sim \operatorname{PP}(\mu)$ be mutually independent. Then, $N+M \sim \operatorname{PP}(\lambda + \mu)$.
#### (*Poisson Splitting*) Let $N \sim \operatorname{PP}(\lambda)$ and $B_{1},B_{2},\ldots \sim_{\text{iid}} \operatorname{Ber}(p)$ be independent of $N$. Then, $K_{t}=|\{i : B_{i}=0, i \leq N_{t}\}|$ and $J_{t}=|\{ i : B_{i}=1,i \leq N_{t}\}|$ such that $K \sim \operatorname{PP}(\lambda p)$, and $J \sim \operatorname{PP}(\lambda[1-p])$ are mutually independent.
In poisson splitting, each arrival to $N_{t}$ is routed to $K_{t}$ or $J_{t}$ according to independent Bernoulli trials, where $B_{i}$ determines the probability of the $i$-th arrival going to $K_t$ or $J_{t}$.

#### (*Random Incidence Paradox*) $\mathbb{E}[(T_{n+1}-t^*)+(t^*-T_{n})]=\dfrac{2}{\lambda}$.
Consider some random arrival time $t^*$ dividing the inter-arrival time $S_{n+1}=T_{n+1}-T_{n}$ into two distinct intervals, $L=t^*-T_{n}$ and $M=T_{n+1}-t^*$. Then, because inter-arrival times are memoryless, $L \sim \operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)$ and $M \sim \operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)$, and $L,M$ are independent of one another. By the linearity of expectation, 
$$\mathbb{E}[L]+\mathbb{E}[M]=1/\lambda+1/\lambda=2/\lambda.$$
This is a paradox for a reason: wouldn't $\mathbb{E}[T_{n+1}-T_{n}]=1/\lambda$? The answer is also yes! We can make the following rationale:
1. $\mathbb{E}[T_{n+1}-T_{n}]=1/\lambda$ is the expected inter-arrival time in a Poisson process.
2. $\mathbb{E}[(T_{n+1}-t^*)+(t^*-T_{n})]=2/\lambda$ is the expected inter-arrival time in the interval which some random $t^*$ falls into.

It turns out that this result leads us to the conclusion that since we are choosing our time $t^*$ randomly, it’s more likely it lands in a longer interval than a short one regardless of its distribution. Longer intervals have a better chance to be chosen, while shorter intervals have a lower chance. Think about that the next time you are trying to catch the next bus!
# Examples
>[!example] Bus Station
>You are waiting for a bus at a bus station. A-line buses arrive at the station according to a Poisson process with an average arrival time of 10 minutes. 
>>[!example]- If the A-line has been running for a long time and you arrive at the bus station at a random time, what is your expected waiting time?
>>The expected time of the Poisson Process $\text{PP}(0.1)$ is $1/0.1=10$ minutes. Therefore, on average, you will have to wait 10 minutes for the next bus.
>
>>[!example]- On average, how many minutes ago did the last A-line bus leave?
>>The memoryless property still applies backwards in time, so on average, the last bus arrived 10 minutes ago as well.
>
>The bus station you are waiting at also services B- and C-line buses, and you can use the C-line to reach your destination. There is a 95% chance that the next C-line bus will arrive within 26 minutes.
>
>>[!example]- You are in a rush to make it to your probability class–what is the chance that the next C-line bus will arrive within 6 minutes?
>>We start by observing that $F_{T}(x=26)=1-e^{-26\lambda}=0.95$. Solving, we obtain $\lambda \approx 0.115$ (i.e., 8.7 minutes on average per bus). We then plug in $F_{T}(x=6)=1-e^{-6 \cdot 0.115} \approx 0.5$.
>
>The B-line buses arrive every 5 minutes on average. However, B-line buses take you on a completely different route, and are not useful.
>>[!example]- What is the chance that an A-line or C-line bus will arrive before a B-line bus?
>>Assume all three lines are independent Poisson processes, so the time until the next bus on each line is exponential with rates $\lambda_{A}=1/10$, $\lambda_{B}=23/200$, $\lambda_{C}=1/5$. We merge these 3 processes and look at the result:
>>$$\mathbb{P}(\min(T_{A}, T_{C}) < T_{B})=\frac{\lambda_{A}+\lambda_{C}}{\lambda_{A}+\lambda_{B}+\lambda_{C}} \approx 0.52.$$

>[!example] Spam/Ham (Poisson Merging)
>Emails to Bob gets automatically classified, and those considered to be Spam are routed to a Spam folder. Emails not classified as spam are routed to Bob's Inbox. Regular email arrives as a Poisson Process with rate **10 msgs/hr** and Spam arrives at a rate of **20 msgs/hr**. A regular email is classified as **spam with probability 0.05** and a spam email is classified as **regular mail with probability 0.01**. 
>>[!example]- Bob checks his Inbox after a long time and selects the most recent message. What is the probability it is spam?
>>Genuine mails that pass the spam filter arrive at $\lambda_{\text{regular}}=10 \cdot (1-0.05)=9.5$ msgs/hr. Spam mails that pass the spam filter arrive at $\lambda_{\text{spam}}=20 \cdot 0.01=0.2$ msgs/hr.
>>
>>As such, the probability of spam mail in Bob's Inbox is $\dfrac{0.2}{9.5+0.2}=0.0206$.
>
>>[!example]- What’s the probability that the top email in his spam mail is regular mail?
>>We use the logic from the previous problem: genuine mail gets rejected by the spam filter at a rate of $\lambda_{\text{reg, rej}}=10 \cdot 0.05=0.5$ msgs/hr, whereas spam gets rejected at a rate of $\lambda_{\text{spam, rej}}=20 \cdot (1-0.01)=19.8$ msgs/hr.
>>
>>As such, the probability of regular mail in Bob's spam mail is $\dfrac{0.5}{19.8+0.5}=0.0246$.
