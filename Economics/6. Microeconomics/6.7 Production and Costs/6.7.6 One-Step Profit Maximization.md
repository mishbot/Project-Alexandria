>[!note] One-Step Profit Maximization
>In **one-step profit maximization** a price-taking firm chooses its inputs directly. Revenue is $py = p f(L, K)$, cost is $wL + rK$, and the firm solves
>$$\max_{L, K}\ p f(L, K) - wL - rK.$$
>- First-order conditions: $p f'_L(L^*, K^*) = w$ and $p f'_K(L^*, K^*) = r$. Each input is hired until its **value of marginal product**, the price times its marginal product, equals its price.
>- Second-order conditions: the Hessian of profit needs $p f''_{LL} < 0$ and
>$$|H| = \begin{vmatrix} p f''_{LL} & p f''_{LK} \\ p f''_{LK} & p f''_{KK} \end{vmatrix} = p^2\left[f''_{LL} f''_{KK} - \left(f''_{LK}\right)^2\right] > 0.$$
>- Diminishing marginal products are not enough. The cross term $f''_{LK}$ must not be too large.
>- For $f = AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta}$, $f''_{LL} f''_{KK} - (f''_{LK})^2 = \alpha\beta\,(1 - \alpha - \beta)\,f^2/(L^2K^2)$. The SOC holds exactly under DRS, $\alpha + \beta < 1$. Under CRS $|H| = 0$, and under IRS $|H| < 0$.
>- The solution gives the **input demand functions** $L^*(p, w, r)$ and $K^*(p, w, r)$ and the supply $y^* = f(L^*, K^*)$. They depend on $p$. The conditional demands $L^*(w, r, y)$ of [[6.7.3 Cost Minimization]] depend on $y$ instead.
>- Comparative statics, from the [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] and Cramer's rule:
>$$\frac{\partial L^*}{\partial w} = \frac{p f''_{KK}}{|H|} < 0, \qquad \frac{\partial K^*}{\partial r} = \frac{p f''_{LL}}{|H|} < 0, \qquad \frac{\partial L^*}{\partial r} = \frac{\partial K^*}{\partial w} = -\frac{p f''_{LK}}{|H|}.$$
>- The cross effect has the sign opposite to $f''_{LK}$. Cobb-Douglas has $f''_{LK} > 0$, so a higher $r$ lowers $L^*$. The conditional demand of cost minimization instead rises with $r$.
>- When the SOC holds, both routes give the same answer. Dividing the FOCs gives the cost-minimizing tangency $f'_L/f'_K = w/r$. The cost-minimization multiplier equals MC, and the FOCs set it equal to $p$, which is the rule $p = MC$ of [[6.7.5 Profit Maximization and Supply]].
>- Cobb-Douglas with DRS:
>$$y^* = \left[A\left(\frac{p\beta}{w}\right)^{\beta}\left(\frac{p\alpha}{r}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha-\beta}}, \qquad L^* = \frac{p\beta\,y^*}{w}, \qquad K^* = \frac{p\alpha\,y^*}{r}.$$

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The term value of marginal product, the isoprofit diagram, the Cobb-Douglas Hessian formula and closed form, the proof that the multiplier equals MC, and the split of the cross effect into substitution and scale effects are extensions. The lecture left $\partial L^*/\partial r$ blank and stated that its sign depends on $f''_{LK}$.

# Intuition

The two-step route first finds the cheapest way to make each output and then picks the output. The one-step route skips the cost function and picks the inputs. One more worker adds $f'_L$ units of output, which sell for $p f'_L$, and costs $w$. The firm hires while $p f'_L > w$. Diminishing marginal product lowers the value of each extra worker, so the firm stops where $p f'_L = w$. The firm takes $p$ as given because it is small relative to its market. If it charged more than the market price, it would sell nothing. If it charged less, it would give up revenue for no gain. So it charges the market price.

The diagram shows one input with $f(L) = 2\sqrt{L}$, $p = 4$, and $w = 2$. An **isoprofit line** collects all pairs $(L, y)$ with the same profit $\pi = py - wL$. It is the line $y = \pi/p + (w/p)L$, with slope $w/p$, and higher lines mean more profit. The firm wants the highest isoprofit line that still touches the production function. At the tangency, $f'(L) = w/p$, which is the FOC $p f'(L) = w$. Here $f'(4) = 1/2 = w/p$, so $L^* = 4$, $y^* = 4$, and $\pi = 16 - 8 = 8$.

# Properties

#### *(Cross-Term Condition)* A maximum needs profit to bend down in every direction, including the direction where both inputs grow together.

The conditions $f''_{LL} < 0$ and $f''_{KK} < 0$ say that profit bends down along each axis. Moving both inputs at once adds the cross term $2p f''_{LK}\,dL\,dK$ to the second-order change in profit. If the inputs are strong complements, this term outweighs diminishing returns along the diagonal, and expanding both inputs keeps raising profit. For Cobb-Douglas, $|H| > 0$ exactly when $\alpha + \beta < 1$. This is the one-step form of the two-step result that only DRS gives a well-behaved supply. Under CRS, profit is linear along each ray from the origin, so $|H| = 0$. Under IRS, profit bends up along rays. If $f$ is concave, profit is concave too, and by [[7.1 Unconstrained Optimality Conditions]] any point that satisfies the FOCs is a global maximum. The surface below is $\pi = 8\,(LK)^{1/4} - L - K$ from the Medium example, with its peak at $(4, 4)$.

#### *(Own-Price Effects Are Negative)* A higher wage always lowers labor demand, and a higher rental rate always lowers capital demand.

In $\partial L^*/\partial w = p f''_{KK}/|H|$, the numerator is negative and the SOC makes $|H|$ positive. The result holds for any technology that satisfies the SOC. A firm has no budget constraint, so a price change has no income effect. This is why input demand cannot slope up, while a consumer's demand for a [[6.3.4 Price Changes|Giffen good]] can.

#### *(Substitution Versus Scale)* A rise in $r$ pushes the firm toward labor at a given output, but it also raises MC and cuts output.

The substitution effect is $\partial L^*(w, r, y)/\partial r > 0$ from cost minimization, at fixed $y$. The scale effect comes from the fall in $y^*$: with $L^*(p, w, r) = L^*(w, r, y^*(p, w, r))$, the chain rule gives
$$\frac{\partial L^*(p, w, r)}{\partial r} = \frac{\partial L^*(w, r, y)}{\partial r} + \frac{\partial L^*(w, r, y)}{\partial y}\,\frac{\partial y^*}{\partial r}.$$
The second term is negative when labor rises with output and output falls with $r$. With $f''_{LK} > 0$, less capital also makes each worker less productive. For Cobb-Douglas the scale effect is larger, and $L^*$ falls. The Hard example measures both effects.

#### *(Choosing a Route)* The one-step route gives input demands directly, and the two-step route also handles CRS and IRS.

Under CRS the one-step FOCs have either no solution or a whole ray of solutions, and the Hessian test fails, as in the Easy example. The two-step route reads off the answer by comparing $p$ with the constant $MC = AC$. The two-step route also produces the cost and supply curves needed for market analysis in [[6.8.1 Aggregation]].

# Derivation

We want the comparative statics and the equivalence of the two routes. Write the FOCs as $F_1 = p f'_L(L, K) - w = 0$ and $F_2 = p f'_K(L, K) - r = 0$. Their derivatives with respect to $(L, K)$ form the Hessian $H$. Differentiating both FOCs with respect to $w$ gives $H\,(\partial L^*/\partial w,\ \partial K^*/\partial w)^T = (1, 0)^T$. Cramer's rule replaces the first column of $H$ by $(1, 0)^T$:
$$\frac{\partial L^*}{\partial w} = \frac{1}{|H|}\begin{vmatrix} 1 & p f''_{LK} \\ 0 & p f''_{KK} \end{vmatrix} = \frac{p f''_{KK}}{|H|}, \qquad \frac{\partial L^*}{\partial r} = \frac{1}{|H|}\begin{vmatrix} 0 & p f''_{LK} \\ 1 & p f''_{KK} \end{vmatrix} = -\frac{p f''_{LK}}{|H|}.$$
The derivative with respect to $r$ uses the right side $(0, 1)^T$. Replacing the second column instead gives $\partial K^*/\partial w = -p f''_{LK}/|H|$ and $\partial K^*/\partial r = p f''_{LL}/|H|$. An equivalent form keeps $\partial F_1/\partial w = -1$ in the matrix: $\partial L^*/\partial w = -\begin{vmatrix} -1 & p f''_{LK} \\ 0 & p f''_{KK} \end{vmatrix} \big/ |H|$.

For the equivalence, cost minimization has FOCs $w = \lambda f'_L$ and $r = \lambda f'_K$, with $f(L^*, K^*) = y$. Differentiating $c(y) = wL^*(y) + rK^*(y)$ gives
$$c'_y(y) = wL^{*\prime}(y) + rK^{*\prime}(y) = \lambda\left(f'_L L^{*\prime}(y) + f'_K K^{*\prime}(y)\right) = \lambda\,\frac{d}{dy} f\big(L^*(y), K^*(y)\big) = \lambda.$$
The last step uses $f(L^*(y), K^*(y)) = y$, so its derivative is $1$. This matches the [[7.3 Envelope Theorem]]. The two-step condition $p = c'_y(y)$ then means $\lambda = p$, so $w = p f'_L$ and $r = p f'_K$, the one-step FOCs.

For Cobb-Douglas, $f''_{LL} = \beta(\beta - 1)f/L^2$, $f''_{KK} = \alpha(\alpha - 1)f/K^2$, and $f''_{LK} = \alpha\beta f/(LK)$. So $f''_{LL}f''_{KK} - (f''_{LK})^2 = \alpha\beta\left[(1-\alpha)(1-\beta) - \alpha\beta\right]f^2/(L^2K^2) = \alpha\beta\,(1 - \alpha - \beta)\,f^2/(L^2K^2)$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Constant-Returns Print Shop
>A print shop has $f = L^{1/2}K^{1/2}$ and $w = r = 1$.
>
>>[!example]- Show that the one-step FOCs have no solution at $p = 3$ and a whole ray of solutions at $p = 2$. What does the shop do at each price?
>>The FOCs are $\frac{p}{2}\sqrt{K/L} = 1$ and $\frac{p}{2}\sqrt{L/K} = 1$. Multiplying them gives $p^2/4 = 1$, so a solution needs $p = 2$. At $p = 2$ every pair with $L = K$ solves both. Here $|H| = 0$, since $f''_{LL}f''_{KK} = (f''_{LK})^2 = 1/(16LK)$. The two-step route gives the answer. The cheapest way to make $y$ is $L = K = y$, so $c(y) = 2y$ and $MC = AC = 2$. At $p = 3$ profit is $(3 - 2)\,y$, which grows without bound. At $p = 2$ any output earns zero. Below $p = 2$ the shop produces nothing.

>[!example] (Medium) Both Routes
>A firm has $f = L^{1/4}K^{1/4}$, $p = 8$, and $w = r = 1$.
>
>>[!example]- Solve the one-step problem, check the SOCs, and confirm the answer with the two-step route.
>>The FOCs are $2L^{-3/4}K^{1/4} = 1$ and $2L^{1/4}K^{-3/4} = 1$. Dividing them gives $K/L = 1$, so $L = K$ and $2L^{-1/2} = 1$. So $L^* = K^* = 4$, $y^* = (16)^{1/4} = 2$, and $\pi^* = 16 - 8 = 8$. At $(4, 4)$, $f''_{LL} = f''_{KK} = -3/128$ and $f''_{LK} = 1/128$. So $p f''_{LL} = -3/16 < 0$ and $|H| = 64\,(9 - 1)/16384 = 1/32 > 0$. The Cobb-Douglas formula agrees: $\frac{1}{16}\cdot\frac12\cdot\frac{4}{256}\cdot 64 = 1/32$. In the two-step route, cost minimization gives $L = K = y^2$ and $c(y) = 2y^2$. Then $p = MC = 4y$ gives $y^* = 2$ and $L = K = 4$, the same answer.

>[!example] (Hard) A Rise in the Rental Rate
>The firm of the Medium example faces a rise in $r$ from $1$ to $4$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the new input demands, output, and profit. Split the change in $L^*$ into a substitution effect at the old output and a scale effect.
>>Solving the FOCs for general prices gives $L^* = p^2/(16\,w^{3/2}r^{1/2})$ and $K^* = p^2/(16\,w^{1/2}r^{3/2})$. At $r = 4$, $L^* = 64/32 = 2$ and $K^* = 64/128 = 0.5$. Output is $(2 \times 0.5)^{1/4} = 1$ and profit is $8 - 2 - 2 = 4$. Labor falls from $4$ to $2$, even though capital became the more expensive input.
>>
>>At the old output $y = 2$ and the new prices, cost minimization sets $K/L = w/r = 1/4$ with $LK = 16$, so $L = 8$ and $K = 2$. The substitution effect moves labor from $4$ to $8$. The new cost function is $c(y) = 4y^2$, so $MC = 8y = p$ gives $y^* = 1$ and $L = 2$. The scale effect moves labor from $8$ to $2$. The total is $+4 - 6 = -2$. Near $r = 1$, the formula gives $\partial L^*/\partial r = -p f''_{LK}/|H| = -8 \cdot \frac{1}{128} \cdot 32 = -2$.
