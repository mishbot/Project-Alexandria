>[!note] Electromotive Force
>A **source**, such as a battery or a power supply, keeps a potential difference between its two terminals. Inside it, a process that is not electric moves positive charge from the negative terminal to the positive terminal. The charge moves against the electric field, so the process must do work on it. In a battery the process is a chemical reaction. The **electromotive force**, or **emf**, of the source is the work it does per unit charge:
>$$\mathcal{E} = \frac{W}{q}.$$
>Here $W$ is the work the source does on a charge $q$. The charge passes through the source from the negative terminal to the positive one. The symbol is a script $\mathcal{E}$, and the unit is the volt, $1\ \text{V} = 1\ \text{J/C}$. The classroom derivations and many circuit diagrams write $V$ for a source's emf. These notes write $\mathcal{E}$ for the emf and $V$ or $V_{ab}$ for the potential difference across an element.
>
>An **ideal source** holds its terminals at the potential difference $\mathcal{E}$ whatever current it delivers. The EE note [[2.1c Voltage Source]] treats it as a circuit element with a fixed voltage. Its symbol is the pair of long and short lines from [[3.5.1. Capacitance]], with the long line at the positive terminal. A source that drives a current $I$ does work at the rate
>$$P = \mathcal{E} I.$$
>This is the power the source delivers to the rest of the circuit. It comes out in watts when $\mathcal{E}$ is in volts and $I$ in amperes.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture gave the name and the symbol. It treated a source as something that gives energy to the charge passing through it. The non-electric work inside the source and the chemistry of a battery follow Giancoli sections 25.1 and 26.1.

# Intuition

A charge that passes through a resistor moves from high potential to low potential. It loses electric potential energy, and the resistor turns that energy into heat. A current that flows around a closed circuit needs something that raises each charge back to high potential. A source does this, and its emf measures the energy it gives each coulomb. An ideal $9.0\ \text{V}$ source gives $9.0\ \text{J}$ to every coulomb that passes through it.

The word "electromotive" means "causing electricity to move." The name describes what a source does to charge. An emf is measured in volts, so it is an energy per unit charge. It is not a force, despite the name.

A positive test charge goes once around the loop of the Hard example. The graph traces its potential, which rises $12.0\ \text{V}$ in the source and falls $12.0\ \text{V}$ in the resistor.

# Derivation

The power formula follows from the definition. In a short time $dt$, a charge $dq = I\,dt$ passes through the source. The source does work $dW = \mathcal{E}\,dq$ on it. Dividing by $dt$ gives the rate of work:
$$P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \mathcal{E}\,\frac{dq}{dt} = \mathcal{E} I.$$
The rate of work is the power, and $dq/dt$ is the current. This matches $P = IV$ from [[3.6.5. Electric Power]], with the emf in place of $V$.

Connect an ideal source across a single resistor $R$. The wires hold the resistor's ends at the potentials of the source's terminals, so the resistor has $V_{ab} = \mathcal{E}$ across it. [[3.6.4. Ohm's Law]] then gives the current $I = \mathcal{E}/R$. The resistor turns energy into heat at the rate $I^2R = I(IR) = \mathcal{E}I$. The source delivers energy at the same rate. The energy the source gives the charge equals the energy the resistor takes out.

# Properties

#### *(Why a Source Is Needed)* Around a closed loop the electrostatic field does zero net work, so it cannot keep a current flowing.

The electric force is conservative, as in [[3.4.1. Electric Potential Energy]]. A conservative force does zero work around any closed path. A charge in a resistor still loses energy in collisions with the atoms. Without a source, the charges would lose energy on every lap, and the current would stop. The source does the positive work that the resistors take out. Inside the source, the non-electric process moves positive charge toward higher potential, against the electric field.

#### *(Kinds of Sources)* Any process that moves charge toward higher potential, against the electric field, can supply an emf.

In a battery, chemical reactions at the two terminals move the charge. In a generator, a changing magnetic flux does it, as [[3.10.2. Faraday's Law]] will show. In every case the emf is a work per unit charge, measured in volts.

#### *(Source Power Has a Sign)* A source delivers the power $\mathcal{E}I$ when current leaves its positive terminal and absorbs it when current enters there.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture noted that the work done by a device and the work done on it differ only by a sign. It did not treat a source that absorbs energy. Giancoli section 26.4 covers battery charging.

When the rest of the circuit drives current into the positive terminal, charge passes through the source from $+$ to $-$. It moves toward lower potential, and the source's process runs backward. A rechargeable battery being charged works this way, and its chemical energy grows by $\mathcal{E}$ for each coulomb. In a circuit with several sources, some can deliver energy while others absorb it. [[3.7.5. Kirchhoff's Loop Rule]] handles both cases with one sign rule.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Work on a Batch of Charge
>An ideal $9.0\ \text{V}$ source moves $0.25\ \text{C}$ of charge from its negative terminal to its positive terminal.
>
>>[!example]- Find the work the source does, and the energy it gives each electron that passes through.
>>The work is the emf times the charge:
>>$$W = \mathcal{E}q = (9.0\ \text{V})(0.25\ \text{C}) = 2.25\ \text{J}.$$
>>Each electron carries a charge of size $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$. The source gives each one $e\mathcal{E} = (1.602 \times 10^{-19})(9.0) = 1.44 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}$. Inside the source, electrons move from the positive terminal to the negative terminal. That is opposite to the positive charge in the definition, and the energy per unit charge is the same.

>[!example] (Medium) Energy in a Phone Battery
>A phone battery has an emf of $3.85\ \text{V}$ and a rated capacity of $4000\ \text{mAh}$. A milliampere-hour (mAh) is the charge that a $1\ \text{mA}$ current carries in one hour.
>
>>[!example]- Find the charge the battery can move and the energy it can deliver. Treat it as an ideal source.
>>One milliampere-hour is $(10^{-3}\ \text{A})(3600\ \text{s}) = 3.6\ \text{C}$. The rated charge is therefore
>>$$q = (4000)(3.6\ \text{C}) = 1.44 \times 10^{4}\ \text{C}.$$
>>The source does work $\mathcal{E}$ on each coulomb, so the energy it can deliver is
>>$$W = \mathcal{E}q = (3.85\ \text{V})(1.44 \times 10^{4}\ \text{C}) = 5.54 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}.$$
>>That is about $55\ \text{kJ}$, or $15.4\ \text{Wh}$, since one watt-hour is $3600\ \text{J}$. A phone that draws $0.50\ \text{W}$ on average would run for $5.54 \times 10^{4}/0.50 = 1.1 \times 10^{5}\ \text{s}$. That is about 31 hours.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not separate the work of the electric field from the work of the source inside a battery. The last part of the next example follows Giancoli section 26.1.

>[!example] (Hard) Energy Around a Loop
>An ideal $12.0\ \text{V}$ source is connected across a $4.0\ \Omega$ resistor for $5.0$ minutes.
>
>>[!example]- Find the charge that passes through the source, the work the source does, and the heat the resistor produces. Then find the work the electric field does on that charge inside the source and inside the resistor.
>>The resistor has the source's emf across it, so the current is $I = \mathcal{E}/R = 12.0/4.0 = 3.0\ \text{A}$. In $t = 300\ \text{s}$ the charge that passes is $q = It = 900\ \text{C}$. The source does work
>>$$W_{\text{source}} = \mathcal{E}q = (12.0\ \text{V})(900\ \text{C}) = 1.08 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}.$$
>>The resistor produces heat at the rate $I^2R = 36\ \text{W}$. In $300\ \text{s}$ it produces $1.08 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}$. The two amounts agree, as energy conservation requires.
>>
>>Inside the source, the charge moves from the negative terminal to the positive terminal. Its potential rises by $12.0\ \text{V}$, so its electric potential energy rises by $q\mathcal{E}$. The electric field therefore does work $-q\mathcal{E} = -1.08 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}$ on it there. Inside the resistor, the charge drops through the same $12.0\ \text{V}$, and the field does $+1.08 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}$. The field's total work around the loop is zero. The source's non-electric process supplies the energy, and the resistor turns it into heat.
