>[!note] Vertical Spread
>A **vertical spread** buys one European option and sells another of the same type on the same stock. Both legs have the same expiry $T$ and different strikes $K_1 < K_2$. A **bull spread** gains when the stock rises, and a **bear spread** gains when it falls. Each can be built from calls or from puts. Let $c_1, c_2$ and $p_1, p_2$ be the call and put premiums at the two strikes. Calls get cheaper as the strike rises and puts get more expensive, so $c_1 > c_2$ and $p_1 < p_2$. The four versions are listed below.
>
>| Spread | Long | Short | Payoff for $S_T \le K_1$ | Payoff for $S_T \ge K_2$ | Net premium today |
>|---|---|---|---|---|---|
>| Bull call spread | $K_1$ call | $K_2$ call | $0$ | $K_2 - K_1$ | pays $c_1 - c_2$ |
>| Bull put spread | $K_1$ put | $K_2$ put | $K_1 - K_2$ | $0$ | receives $p_2 - p_1$ |
>| Bear call spread | $K_2$ call | $K_1$ call | $0$ | $K_1 - K_2$ | receives $c_1 - c_2$ |
>| Bear put spread | $K_2$ put | $K_1$ put | $K_2 - K_1$ | $0$ | pays $p_2 - p_1$ |
>
>Between the strikes the payoff moves one for one with $S_T$, upward for a bull spread and downward for a bear spread. For the bull call spread, the payoff at $T$ is
>$$\max(S_T - K_1, 0) - \max(S_T - K_2, 0) = \begin{cases} 0, & S_T \le K_1, \\ S_T - K_1, & K_1 < S_T < K_2, \\ K_2 - K_1, & S_T \ge K_2. \end{cases}$$
>The first term is the long call and the second is the short call. The profit at $T$ is the payoff minus the net premium, ignoring interest on the premium. The bull call spread breaks even at $S_T = K_1 + (c_1 - c_2)$. Its maximum gain is $K_2 - K_1 - (c_1 - c_2)$, reached for $S_T \ge K_2$, and its maximum loss is $c_1 - c_2$, reached for $S_T \le K_1$. The bull put spread breaks even at $S_T = K_2 - (p_2 - p_1)$. Its maximum gain is the premium $p_2 - p_1$, and its maximum loss is $K_2 - K_1 - (p_2 - p_1)$. A bear spread is the other side of the bull spread with the same strikes and option type. Its profit is the bull spread's profit with the sign changed. A trader may build a bull spread from puts to receive the premium up front. A vertical spread suits a view on direction with a capped cost and a capped gain. A [[2.3.2b Collar|collar]] on a share has the same payoff shape as a bull call spread, raised by $K_1$.

# Intuition

A long call pays on every rise in the stock, including large rises the buyer does not expect. The buyer pays premium for all of that payoff. A trader who expects a moderate rise can also sell a call with a higher strike $K_2$. The sale brings in $c_2$ and lowers the cost. In exchange, the trader gives up every gain above $K_2$. The position costs less than the call alone and pays at most $K_2 - K_1$. The name comes from how quotes are listed. In an option chain (the table of quotes for one stock), the strikes for one expiry run down a column. Both legs come from the same column, one above the other.

# Properties

#### *(Price Bounds)* No arbitrage keeps the net premium of a vertical spread between zero and the present value of its largest payoff.

The bull call spread pays between $0$ and $K_2 - K_1$ in every state. A portfolio that never pays less than another cannot cost less today. With $r$ the continuously compounded risk-free rate, this gives
$$0 \le c_1 - c_2 \le (K_2 - K_1)e^{-rT}.$$
The upper bound is the price of a zero-coupon bond that pays $K_2 - K_1$ at $T$. The same argument for the bear put spread gives $0 \le p_2 - p_1 \le (K_2 - K_1)e^{-rT}$. Per dollar of strike, a call price falls by at most $e^{-rT}$ and a put price rises by at most $e^{-rT}$. Suppose call quotes break the upper bound. A trader then sells the bull call spread and lends the premium at $r$. The loan repays more than the most the spread can owe. These bounds extend the single-option bounds of [[2.3.1b Option Price Bounds]].

#### *(Calls or Puts)* A bull call spread and a bull put spread on the same strikes have the same profit once interest is counted.

The bull call payoff minus the bull put payoff equals $K_2 - K_1$ in every state. [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity|Put-call parity]] gives the matching price identity. With $S$ the stock price today, subtract $c_2 - p_2 = S - K_2e^{-rT}$ from $c_1 - p_1 = S - K_1e^{-rT}$ to get
$$(c_1 - c_2) + (p_2 - p_1) = (K_2 - K_1)e^{-rT}.$$
The debit of the call version and the credit of the put version add up to the present value of the strike gap. Write $D = c_1 - c_2$. With interest, the call version's profit is its payoff minus $De^{rT}$. The put version's payoff is lower by $K_2 - K_1$, and its credit grows to $(K_2 - K_1) - De^{rT}$ by $T$. Its profit is therefore the same. The two versions differ only in the timing of cash. The put version pays the trader today and may cost up to $K_2 - K_1$ at expiry. The call version costs money today and never costs money at expiry.

#### *(Choosing the Strikes)* Moving both strikes up makes a bull call spread cheaper, but the stock must then rise further to pay off.

Take a stock at $\$60$ with six-month calls priced by the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]] at $30\%$ volatility and $r = 5\%$. The call prices are $\$8.63$, $\$5.78$, $\$3.69$, $\$2.25$, and $\$1.32$ at strikes $55$, $60$, $65$, $70$, and $75$. Three spreads of width $\$10$ compare as follows.

| Strikes | Net premium | Max gain | Max gain / max loss | Break-even |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $55$ / $65$ | $\$4.94$ | $\$5.06$ | $1.02$ | $\$59.94$ |
| $60$ / $70$ | $\$3.53$ | $\$6.47$ | $1.83$ | $\$63.53$ |
| $65$ / $75$ | $\$2.37$ | $\$7.63$ | $3.22$ | $\$67.37$ |

The ratio of gain to loss rises quickly as the strikes move above the stock price. The break-even price rises with it. A high ratio reflects a lower chance of ending above the strikes, so it does not make the trade better by itself. Widening the spread raises both the cost and the maximum gain.

#### *(Narrow Spreads)* A narrow bull call spread, divided by its width, prices a bet that pays $\$1$ when the stock ends above the strike.

Take strikes $K$ and $K + h$ and hold $1/h$ units of the bull call spread. The payoff is $0$ below $K$, $1$ above $K + h$, and linear in between. As $h \to 0$ the position becomes a **digital call** (a claim that pays $\$1$ if $S_T > K$ and nothing otherwise). Its price tends to $-\partial c / \partial K$. Under [[2.3.3b Risk-Neutral Valuation|risk-neutral valuation]] that price equals $e^{-rT}\hat{\mathbb{P}}[S_T > K]$. For the $\$60$ stock above, the $60$ / $61$ spread costs $5.78 - 5.30 = \$0.48$. The digital call at $K = 60$ is worth $\$0.4922$, which implies $\hat{\mathbb{P}}[S_T > 60] = 0.5047$. The bound of $e^{-rT}$ per dollar of strike, from the first property, says that this probability is at most one.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Profit From a Bull Call Spread
>A stock trades at $\$60$. A six-month European call with strike $\$55$ costs $\$8.63$, and one with strike $\$65$ costs $\$3.69$. A trader expects a moderate rise and buys the bull call spread.
>
>>[!example]- Find the net premium, the break-even price, and the profit at expiry if the stock ends at $\$50$, $\$62$, or $\$70$.
>>The trader pays $8.63 - 3.69 = \$4.94$ today. The break-even price is $55 + 4.94 = \$59.94$.
>>
>>At $S_T = 50$ both calls expire worthless. The profit is $-\$4.94$, the maximum loss. At $S_T = 62$ the long call pays $\$7$ and the short call pays nothing, so the profit is $7 - 4.94 = \$2.06$. At $S_T = 70$ the long call pays $\$15$ and the short call costs $\$5$. The payoff is the cap $K_2 - K_1 = \$10$, and the profit is $\$5.06$, the maximum gain.

>[!example] (Medium) A Bear Put Spread
>A stock trades at $\$40$. A trader creates a bear spread with three-month European puts. She sells a put with strike $\$36$ for $\$1.01$ and buys a put with strike $\$42$ for $\$3.70$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the initial investment, and the payoff and profit at expiry when the stock ends at $\$33$, $\$39$, and $\$45$.
>>The initial investment is $3.70 - 1.01 = \$2.69$. The payoff at expiry is $\max(42 - S_T, 0) - \max(36 - S_T, 0)$.
>>
>>At $S_T = 33$ both puts are exercised. The long put pays $\$9$ and the short put costs $\$3$, so the payoff is $\$6 = K_2 - K_1$. The profit is $6 - 2.69 = \$3.31$, the maximum gain. At $S_T = 39$ only the long put pays, $42 - 39 = \$3$. The profit is $3 - 2.69 = \$0.31$. At $S_T = 45$ both puts expire worthless, and the trader loses the whole $\$2.69$.
>>
>>The break-even price is $42 - 2.69 = \$39.31$. The trader profits only if the stock falls by more than about $1.7\%$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The next example combines two vertical spreads into a box spread. The modules do not cover box spreads.

>[!example] (Hard) A Box Spread From a Mispriced Put
>A stock trades at $\$80$ and pays no dividends. Six-month European calls are quoted at $\$6.41$ (strike $\$80$) and $\$2.70$ (strike $\$90$). Six-month European puts are quoted at $\$4.82$ (strike $\$80$) and $\$10.50$ (strike $\$90$). The risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Show that a bull call spread plus a bear put spread on these strikes is mispriced, profit from it, and find the bad quote.
>>Combine the bull call spread and the bear put spread with strikes $80$ and $90$. For $S_T \le 80$ the call spread pays $0$ and the put spread pays $10$. For $S_T \ge 90$ the call spread pays $10$ and the put spread pays $0$. In between, the two pay $S_T - 80$ and $90 - S_T$. The sum is $\$10$ in every state. This combination is a **box spread**. It is a zero-coupon bond, so it must cost $10e^{-0.04 \times 0.5} = \$9.80$.
>>
>>The quotes give a cost of $(6.41 - 2.70) + (10.50 - 4.82) = 3.71 + 5.68 = \$9.39$. The box is $\$0.41$ too cheap. Buy it: buy the $\$80$ call, sell the $\$90$ call, buy the $\$90$ put, and sell the $\$80$ put. Borrow $\$9.39$ at $4\%$ to pay for it. At expiry the box pays $\$10$ and the loan costs $9.39e^{0.02} = \$9.58$. The trader keeps $\$0.42$ in every state.
>>
>>To find the bad quote, check put-call parity at each strike. At $K = 80$, $S - Ke^{-rT} = 80 - 78.42 = \$1.58$, and the quotes give $c - p = \$1.59$, equal up to rounding. At $K = 90$, $S - Ke^{-rT} = 80 - 88.22 = -\$8.22$, while the quotes give $2.70 - 10.50 = -\$7.80$. Parity puts the $\$90$ put at $2.70 + 8.22 = \$10.92$. Its quote is $\$0.42$ too low, which accounts for the whole gap up to rounding.
