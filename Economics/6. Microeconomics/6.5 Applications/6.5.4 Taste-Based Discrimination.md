>[!note] Taste-Based Discrimination
>The **taste-based discrimination** model, inspired by Becker (1957), explains pay and employment gaps between equally productive workers through an employer's dislike of one group. Two workers, A and B, each produce one widget per day. Each has **reservation wage** $\bar u$, the lowest daily wage at which the worker accepts a job, equal to the pay of her best outside option. A firm sells widgets at a given price $p > \bar u$. It maximizes profit, $p$ times the number of widgets minus the wage bill, minus a disutility $d > 0$, measured in dollars, if it employs B. The **net surplus** of a job is the value it creates for the firm and the worker together beyond the worker's reservation wage:
>$$\text{net surplus from A} = p - \bar u, \qquad \text{net surplus from B} = p - \bar u - d.$$
>The two differ only by the firm's disutility $d$. A job is filled only when its net surplus is positive. If $\bar u < p < \bar u + d$, the firm employs A but not B. If $p > \bar u + d$, it employs both. Each wage equals $\bar u$ plus the worker's share of the net surplus, and the share depends on market structure:
>
>| Case | Market structure | Wage of A | Wage of B |
>|---|---|---|---|
>| I | monopolist and monopsonist, no union | $\bar u$ | $\bar u$ if employed |
>| II | monopolist and monopsonist, union, surplus split in half | $\bar u + \frac{1}{2}(p - \bar u)$ | $\bar u + \frac{1}{2}(p - \bar u - d)$ if employed |
>| III | perfect competition, every firm has $d > 0$ | $p = \bar u$ | not employed |
>| IIIb | perfect competition, at least one firm has $d = 0$ | $p = \bar u$ | $p = \bar u$ |
>
>A **monopsony** is a market with a single buyer, here the only employer. The single employer keeps the whole net surplus unless a union bargains for part of it. The equal split in Case II is an assumption about bargaining power. Under perfect competition the widget price falls to the cost of production, so $p = \bar u$. In Case IIIb the firm with $d = 0$ hires B at the same wage as A. In Case IIIb the pay and employment gap between A and B disappears. Wages also fall relative to Case II, because firms have smaller profits to share with workers. Black and Strahan (2001), American Economic Review, document this pattern.

>[!warning] Reservation Wage Notation
>In this model $\bar u$ is a reservation wage in dollars per day. It is not a utility level, unlike the $\bar u$ in the Hicksian demand $h_i(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$ of [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]].

# Intuition

A and B produce the same output, so any gap between them comes from the firm's taste. The firm pays for this taste. A job for B creates less net surplus than a job for A. When that surplus turns negative, the firm gives up a hire that would raise its profit. Whether the firm or worker B bears this cost depends on who captures the surplus from a job. That in turn depends on market structure.

The diagram splits the daily value $p = 100$ of each worker's output. Both workers must receive at least the reservation wage $\bar u = 60$. For A, the remaining $40$ is net surplus. For B, the firm also subtracts its disutility $d = 30$, which leaves a net surplus of $10$. With a union that splits each surplus in half, the wages are $80$ for A and $65$ for B. Without a union, the firm keeps both surpluses and pays each worker $60$.

# Properties

#### *(Where the Gap Shows Up)* A pay gap appears only in markets where workers share the surplus.

In Case I the firm pays every worker $\bar u$, so A and B earn the same whenever both are hired. The firm's taste then shows up only in employment, when $p < \bar u + d$. In Case II each worker receives half of her own net surplus, so the pay gap is $w_A - w_B = d/2$. The union passes half of the firm's disutility on to B. In this model a pay gap therefore exists only with **rent sharing**, where workers receive part of the firm's profit. Within the model, a worker who is not hired takes her outside option, worth $\bar u$. In Cases I and III, A also earns $\bar u$, so B's exclusion costs her the job while her income equals A's.

#### *(Empty Bargaining Range)* The firm does not hire B when no wage is acceptable to both sides.

The firm gains from employing B at wage $w$ only if $p - w - d \ge 0$, that is, if $w \le p - d$. B accepts only if $w \ge \bar u$. A wage acceptable to both exists only when $\bar u \le p - d$, which is the hiring condition $p \ge \bar u + d$. The wage lies somewhere in the range from $\bar u$ to $p - d$, at a point set by the market structure. When the range is empty, no wage exists under any market structure. For this reason the hiring rule is the same in Cases I and II. A union cannot win B a job that the firm does not want to fill.

#### *(One Fair Firm Is Enough)* Under perfect competition, a single firm with $d = 0$ removes the gap for every B worker.

Firms compete for workers, so each worker's wage rises to the highest value any firm places on her. The firm with $d = 0$ values A and B equally at $p$, so it offers both the wage $p$. A discriminating firm would earn $p - p - d = -d < 0$ from B at that wage, so it does not bid for B. B therefore works at the firm with $d = 0$. Each worker produces one widget whatever the size of the firm. This one firm can therefore hire every B worker without lowering its profit per worker. The discriminating firms lose nothing, because they earn zero profit on A as well. The result depends on this constant output per worker. If the firm with $d = 0$ could hire only a few workers, the remaining B workers would face only discriminating firms.

#### *(Price Falls to Cost)* The price $p$ is fixed in Cases I and II and falls to $\bar u$ under competition.

In Cases I and II the firm faces a given price $p > \bar u$ and earns positive profit. Under perfect competition, new firms enter while profit is positive. As firms enter, the widget price falls until it equals the cost of producing a widget, which is the wage of one worker for one day. Workers are available at $\bar u$, so in equilibrium both the price and the wage equal $\bar u$. The statement that both workers earn $p$ in Case IIIb therefore means that both earn $\bar u$. The level effect is the fall of A's wage from $\bar u + \frac{1}{2}(p - \bar u)$ in Case II to $\bar u$. It occurs because the monopoly profit that the union shared no longer exists.

#### *(Reservation Wage)* The reservation wage is the worker's opportunity cost of taking the job.

A worker who takes the job gives up her best alternative, such as another job or time at home. The reservation wage $\bar u$ is the dollar value of that alternative. By the principle of [[6.5.1 Opportunity Cost]], the net surplus of a job is the value it creates beyond everything the worker gives up. [[6.5.2 Labor Supply]] shows how a reservation wage for hours of work arises from preferences. A Cobb-Douglas worker with non-labor income $M$ starts to work only above the wage $(1-\alpha)M/(24\alpha)$. The discrimination model takes $\bar u$ as given and equal for A and B, so every difference in outcomes comes from the firm.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Who Gets Hired
>A widget firm is the only employer in its town and sells widgets at $p = \$100$ each. Workers A and B each produce one widget per day and have reservation wage $\bar u = \$60$. There is no union. Compare an owner whose disutility from employing B is $d = \$30$ with an owner whose disutility is $d = \$50$.
>
>>[!example]- Whom does each firm employ, at what wages, and what is the lowest widget price at which each firm hires B?
>>The net surplus from A is $100 - 60 = 40 > 0$, so both firms hire A. Without a union the firm keeps all the surplus and pays $\bar u = \$60$.
>>
>>With $d = 30$, the net surplus from B is $100 - 60 - 30 = 10 > 0$, so the firm hires B at $\$60$ as well. B's widget brings $\$40$ of profit, and the owner's disutility of $\$30$ leaves a net gain of $\$10$. With $d = 50$, the net surplus from B is $100 - 60 - 50 = -10 < 0$. The firm leaves B's job unfilled and gives up $\$40$ of profit to avoid a disutility of $\$50$.
>>
>>The firm hires B only if $p > \bar u + d$. This means $p > \$90$ for $d = 30$ and $p > \$110$ for $d = 50$.

>[!example] (Medium) A Union Contract
>The workers at the same town firm, with $p = \$100$ and $\bar u = \$60$, form a union. The union and the firm split the net surplus of each job in half.
>
>>[!example]- Find the wages of A and B when $d = 30$ and when $d = 50$. Why is the pay gap half of $d$?
>>A's net surplus is $40$ in both cases, so $w_A = 60 + 20 = \$80$.
>>
>>With $d = 30$, B's net surplus is $10$, so $w_B = 60 + 5 = \$65$. The gap is $80 - 65 = \$15 = d/2$. Each wage is $\bar u$ plus half of a surplus, and B's surplus is smaller than A's by exactly $d$. Half of that difference reaches the wage. Without the union both workers earned $\$60$. With it, B earns $\$15$ less than A.
>>
>>With $d = 50$, B's net surplus is $-10$. The firm would pay B at most $p - d = \$50$, while B needs at least $\$60$, so no wage works for both. The firm does not hire B, and the union cannot change this. B takes her outside option worth $\$60$, while A earns $\$80$.

>[!example] (Hard) Which Market Pays B the Most
>Keep $\bar u = \$60$, and let every discriminating firm have $d = \$30$. Under a monopsony the widget price is $p = \$100$. Under perfect competition, entry drives the price down to cost.
>
>>[!example]- Rank Cases I, II, III, and IIIb by B's pay. Why does competition need only one firm with $d = 0$ to remove the gap?
>>Under competition the price falls to the cost of a widget, $\bar u = \$60$. The outcomes are:
>>
>>| Case | Wage of A | Outcome for B |
>>|---|---|---|
>>| I | $60$ | hired at $60$ |
>>| II | $80$ | hired at $65$ |
>>| III | $60$ | not hired, outside option $60$ |
>>| IIIb | $60$ | hired at $60$ |
>>
>>B earns the most in Case II, the union monopsony, even though it is the only case with a pay gap. The union shares the firm's monopoly profit, and B receives a share of it, smaller than A's. With competition and at least one firm with $d = 0$ (Case IIIb), the gap disappears and B is employed. The profit that a union could share also disappears, so pay falls to $\$60$ for both workers.
>>
>>One firm with $d = 0$ is enough because it values B as much as A. Because firms compete for workers, it must pay B the full value $p$ of her widget. A discriminating firm would lose $d = \$30$ per day on B at that wage, so it does not bid for her. B joins the fair firm, which can hire any number of workers because each one produces one widget regardless of firm size. The discriminating firms keep hiring A at zero profit and lose nothing by leaving B to the fair firm.
