>[!note] Reversible Process
>A **reversible process** is one that can be run backward. The system and its surroundings then both return to their initial states, with no trace left anywhere. The definition has two parts. First, the process must be able to run in reverse, back through the same states to the start. Second, it must not raise the total disorder of the system and its surroundings. Running a process backward is not enough by itself. The second part becomes exact once [[2.7.6 Entropy|entropy]] is defined. A process is reversible when it leaves the total entropy of the system and its surroundings unchanged. That test is the [[2.7.7 Entropy Statement of the Second Law|entropy statement]] of the [[2.7.2 Second Law of Thermodynamics|second law]] with the equality sign. A process that fails either part is **irreversible**. Any real process is irreversible to some degree.
>
>Two conditions make a process reversible in practice. It must be slow enough that the system passes through a sequence of equilibrium states. Each state has one pressure and one temperature. It must also be free of dissipation: no friction, no turbulence, and no heat flow across a finite temperature difference. Every path drawn on a $P$-$V$ diagram is assumed reversible. The diagram can only show states with a single $P$ and $T$. The four standard [[2.5.3 Thermodynamic Processes|processes]] are reversible when carried out slowly. Reversible processes are the building blocks of the [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot cycle]], the most efficient cycle possible.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not state the practical conditions in the second paragraph above. These are slowness, no friction or turbulence, and the rule that $P$-$V$ paths are reversible. Lecture defined reversibility by the two parts in the first paragraph. The conditions follow Giancoli section 20.3.

# Intuition

Fill the gap between two cylinders with glycerin, inject a thin line of fluorescent dye, and turn the inner cylinder a few times. The line smears into a spiral band that looks thoroughly mixed. Turn the cylinder back the same number of times and the line reappears. Each layer of glycerin was carried a definite distance around and is carried back the same distance. The dye barely diffuses through the glycerin, so no disorder was added, and nothing was lost. That is a reversible process, or close to one. The demonstration shows only the first part of the definition, running backward. The dye passes the second part too, because its disorder did not grow.

Now pour hot water into cold water. The molecules mix, and no amount of stirring the other way will separate the hot ones from the cold ones. The chance of them sorting themselves out is zero for any practical purpose. That is an irreversible process. It has increased the disorder of $10^{22}$ molecules, and disorder does not undo itself. Energy is conserved either way, so the first law would allow the reverse. The second law forbids it.

# Properties

#### *(Slow and Frictionless)* A reversible process is quasi-static, with no friction and no heat flow across a temperature gap.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover quasi-static processes or friction as sources of irreversibility. This property follows Giancoli section 20.3.

Quasi-static means the system is always very close to equilibrium, so that reversing the external conditions reverses the process. A piston pushed slowly compresses a gas reversibly. A piston slammed in sends a pressure wave through the gas and heats it unevenly, and pulling the piston back does not undo that. Friction converts work into heat on both the forward and the backward stroke, so a process with friction can never return the surroundings to their initial state. Heat that flows from a body at $T_H$ to a body at $T_L < T_H$ cannot be sent back without work. Heat exchange is therefore reversible only when the two bodies differ by a vanishing amount in temperature.

#### *(The Surroundings Count Too)* Returning the system to its initial state is not enough; the surroundings must be restored as well.

A gas that expands into a vacuum can be pushed back to its original volume by a piston, so the system's path can be retraced. The pushing costs work, and that work ends up as heat in the bath that holds the gas at its temperature. The gas is back where it started, but the surroundings have lost work and gained heat, and by the [[2.7.2 Second Law of Thermodynamics|second law]] that heat cannot be turned back into the work without some further change. The process was irreversible even though the system got back to where it started. Reversibility is a property of the whole, system and surroundings together.

#### *(Reversible Means Maximal)* Between two given states, a reversible process does the most work an expansion can do and needs the least work a compression can take.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not state that reversible paths maximize work in general. It only noted that the Carnot cycle's isothermal legs turn all their heat into work. This property follows Giancoli section 20.3.

Irreversibility wastes work. A gas expanding against a piston that moves slowly delivers the full $\int P\,dV$. The same gas expanding suddenly against a lower pressure delivers less, and expanding into a vacuum delivers nothing. The [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot engine]] is the most efficient engine because every leg of its cycle is reversible. Any irreversibility in a real engine shows up as extra heat rejected to the cold reservoir and less work out.

#### *(Only Reversible Paths Can Be Drawn)* A reversible process is the only kind whose every intermediate state is a point on the $P$-$V$ diagram.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not discuss which processes can be drawn on a $P$-$V$ diagram. This property is standard textbook material.

An irreversible process has a definite start and a definite end, but in between the gas has no single pressure or temperature, so there is nothing to plot. This is why every formula in section 2.5, from $W = \int P\,dV$ to $PV^{\gamma} = \text{const}$, is written for reversible processes. It is also why later notes define [[2.7.6 Entropy|entropy]] along reversible paths and then use a reversible stand-in to handle free expansion, mixing, and heat flow across a temperature gap, none of which is reversible.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Sorting Processes
>Classify each process as reversible or irreversible: a gas compressed slowly by a piston in an insulated cylinder; an ice cube melting in a glass of water at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$; a gas expanding into an evacuated chamber; a block sliding to a stop on a rough table.
>
>>[!example]- Give the reason in each case.
>>The slow adiabatic compression is reversible: it is quasi-static, there is no friction, and no heat crosses a temperature difference. Withdrawing the piston slowly returns the gas to its initial state. The melting ice is irreversible: heat flows from water at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to ice at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$, across a finite gap, and it will not flow back. Free expansion is irreversible: the gas passes through no sequence of equilibrium states, and the molecules will not gather back into the original chamber. The sliding block is irreversible: friction has turned kinetic energy into heat, and the heat will not spontaneously push the block back.

>[!example] (Medium) Heating a Block Reversibly
>A metal block at $300\ \text{K}$ must be warmed to $400\ \text{K}$.
>
>```tikz
>\usepackage{tikz}
>\begin{document}
>\definecolor{hot}{HTML}{D9483B}
>\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1cm,y=1cm,>=latex]
>\draw[very thick] (0.4,0.2) rectangle (1.4,0.9);
>\node at (0.9,1.2) {block, $300\ \mathrm{K}$};
>\draw[hot,thick] (-0.2,-0.6) rectangle (2.0,0.1);
>\node at (0.9,-0.25) {$400\ \mathrm{K}$};
>\node at (0.9,-1.0) {one bath};
>\foreach \x/\t in {3.4/301, 4.4/302, 5.4/303} {
>\draw[hot,thick] (\x,-0.6) rectangle (\x+0.9,0.1);
>\node at (\x+0.45,-0.25) {\small \t};
>}
>\node at (6.8,-0.25) {$\cdots$};
>\draw[hot,thick] (7.3,-0.6) rectangle (8.2,0.1);
>\node at (7.75,-0.25) {\small 400};
>\draw[very thick] (3.4,0.2) rectangle (4.3,0.9);
>\draw[->,thick] (4.4,0.55) -- (5.3,0.55);
>\node at (5.8,-1.0) {a series of baths, $1\ \mathrm{K}$ apart};
>\end{tikzpicture}
>\end{document}
>```
>
>>[!example]- Compare dropping it into a bath at $400\ \text{K}$ with passing it through a long series of baths at $301$, $302$, and so on up to $400\ \text{K}$.
>>Dropping the block into the $400\ \text{K}$ bath makes heat flow across a gap of up to $100\ \text{K}$. That flow will not run backward on its own, so the process is irreversible, though the block reaches the same final state. The series of baths passes heat across a gap of only $1\ \text{K}$ at each step. Run backward, with the block carried down through the same baths, each bath takes back almost exactly the heat it gave, and the block returns to $300\ \text{K}$ with the baths nearly as they were. The mismatch is of order one part in a hundred, and shrinks as the steps shrink. In the limit of infinitely many baths the process is reversible. The price is time: the block has to reach equilibrium with each bath in turn, and a reversible process takes forever.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not compare slow and rapid compressions. This example is standard textbook material and follows Giancoli section 20.3.

>[!example] (Hard) Reversible in Practice
>A gas is compressed isothermally from $V$ to $V/2$ in contact with a bath at $300\ \text{K}$, first slowly and then in a single rapid stroke.
>
>>[!example]- Compare the work required, and explain which process the $P$-$V$ diagram describes.
>>The slow compression follows the isotherm $PV = NkT$, and the work done on the gas is $NkT \ln 2$, the area under the curve. The gas stays at $300\ \text{K}$ throughout and gives the same $NkT \ln 2$ to the bath as heat. The rapid stroke compresses the gas before heat can escape, so the pressure the piston pushes against rises above the isotherm and the work done on the gas exceeds $NkT \ln 2$. The gas then cools back to $300\ \text{K}$ and gives the extra work to the bath as heat too. Only the slow process can be drawn on the diagram, because only the slow process has a pressure to plot at each volume. The rapid process starts and ends on the isotherm, but in between the gas has no single pressure. The reversible path is the one drawn, and it is also the one that costs the least work.
