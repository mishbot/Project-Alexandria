>[!note] Investment in a Risky Asset
>A person with wealth $w$ and utility of money $u$, $u' > 0$, splits her wealth between two assets. The **bond** B returns $1$ for each dollar, so it keeps its value. The **stock** S returns $1 + r$ for each dollar, where $r = r_+ > 0$ with probability $p$ and $r = r_- < 0$ with probability $1 - p$. Its expected net return, which is also its expected return over the bond, is $Er = p\,r_+ + (1-p)\,r_- > 0$. She puts a share $\alpha$ of her wealth into the stock, so her final wealth is $w[(1-\alpha) + \alpha(1 + r)] = w[1 + \alpha r]$. She solves
>$$\max_{0 \le \alpha \le 1}\ (1-p)\,u\big(w[1 + \alpha r_-]\big) + p\,u\big(w[1 + \alpha r_+]\big).$$
>- A risk-neutral person has $u(x) = bx$ with $b > 0$ (adding a constant changes nothing). Her objective is $bw + \alpha\,bw\,Er$, which rises with $\alpha$, so $\alpha^* = 1$.
>- A risk-averse person has $u'' < 0$. Her first-order condition at an interior optimum is
>$$0 = (1-p)\,(w r_-)\,u'\big(w[1 + \alpha r_-]\big) + p\,(w r_+)\,u'\big(w[1 + \alpha r_+]\big).$$
>- Second-order condition: the second derivative $(1-p)(w r_-)^2u''(\cdot) + p\,(w r_+)^2u''(\cdot)$ is negative, so the objective is strictly concave and the optimum is unique.
>- At $\alpha = 0$ the slope of the objective is $w\,u'(w)\,Er > 0$. So $\alpha^* = 0$ is never optimal, and a risk-averse person always holds some of an asset whose expected return beats the bond.
>- Upper corner: $\alpha^* = 1$ exactly when the slope at $\alpha = 1$ is still non-negative, $(1-p)\,r_-\,u'(w[1 + r_-]) + p\,r_+\,u'(w[1 + r_+]) \ge 0$. If instead $Er \le 0$, a risk-averse person holds no stock.
>- With $u = \ln x$ the share has a closed form, $\alpha^* = \dfrac{Er}{r_+\,|r_-|}$ when this is at most $1$. It does not depend on $w$.
>- Example with $p = 1/2$, $r_+ = 0.5$, $r_- = -0.3$, so $Er = 0.1$: a person with $\sqrt x$ holds $\alpha^* = 1$, with $\ln x$ holds $2/3$, and with $-1/x$ holds $0.33$.
>
>[[6.6.3 Insurance]] studies the opposite kind of bet, one that loses money on average and pays when the person is poor.

# Intuition

Each extra share of wealth in the stock raises expected wealth by $w\,Er$. It also widens the gap between the good outcome and the bad one. A risk-neutral person sees only the mean and puts everything in the stock. A risk-averse person also counts the spread against her. She stops where one more share adds no expected utility.

The diagram plots final wealth per dollar of initial wealth against $\alpha$, for a stock with $p = 1/2$, $r_+ = 0.5$, and $r_- = -0.3$. At $\alpha = 0$ the two outcomes coincide at $1$ and there is no risk. Both the mean and the spread grow in proportion to $\alpha$. The cost of risk does not. By [[6.6.2 Risk Aversion|the small-risk approximation]], the risk premium grows with the variance, which grows with $\alpha^2$. Near $\alpha = 0$ the gain in mean is first order and the cost of risk is second order. So the first small share always pays, and every risk-averse person holds some stock.

# Properties

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Every property in this section goes beyond the lecture.

#### *(Small Positions Nearly Riskless)* Every risk-averse investor values the first small share of stock as a risk-neutral one does.

The diagram measures the value of each portfolio by its [[6.6.2 Risk Aversion|certainty equivalent]] per dollar of wealth, with $p = 1/2$, $r_+ = 0.5$, and $r_- = -0.3$. The risk-neutral line is $1 + \alpha\,Er$. The curves for $\ln x$ and $-1/x$ leave $\alpha = 0$ tangent to that line, with the same slope $Er = 0.1$. They bend away from it as the risk premium builds up. For moderate risks the approximation $\text{RP} \approx \tfrac12 r_A V$ gives
$$\frac{\text{CE}(\alpha)}{w} \approx 1 + \alpha\,Er - \tfrac12\,r_R\,\alpha^2\,V(r), \qquad \alpha^* \approx \frac{Er}{r_R\,V(r)},$$
where $V(r) = p(1-p)(r_+ - r_-)^2 = 0.16$ is the variance of the return. The approximation gives $0.625$ for $\ln x$ and $0.31$ for $-1/x$, close to the exact $0.667$ and $0.328$.

#### *(Slope at Zero)* Comparing this model with insurance shows why one bet is taken only with a positive mean and the other even with a negative mean.

For the stock, $EU'(0) = w\,u'(w)\,Er$, because the stock's payoff is unrelated to any other risk. Only its expected return matters at the margin. For insurance, the slope at zero coverage splits as
$$EU'(0) = (p - q)\,u'(w) + (1-q)\,p\,\big[u'(w - L) - u'(w)\big].$$
The first term is the expected return of one unit of coverage. The second is a hedging term, positive because coverage pays in the state where wealth is low and marginal utility is high. With $q > p$ the first term is negative, and the person still buys when the hedging term is larger. The stock has no hedging term, so a risk-averse person holds it only when $Er > 0$.

#### *(Checking the Corners)* The lower corner never binds when $Er > 0$, and the upper corner binds when the first-order condition asks for more than all of wealth.

The constraint $0 \le \alpha \le 1$ rules out borrowing and short selling. Since $EU'$ is decreasing, $\alpha^* = 1$ exactly when $EU'(1) \ge 0$. This happens for low risk aversion or a high $Er$, as for $\sqrt x$ in the Hard example. A risk lover with a convex $u$ also picks $\alpha^* = 1$. Her objective is convex in $\alpha$, so its maximum is at an endpoint, and $EU(1) \ge u(w[1 + Er]) > u(w) = EU(0)$. If $Er \le 0$, then $EU'(0) \le 0$ and a risk-averse person picks the [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions|corner]] $\alpha^* = 0$.

#### *(Share and Wealth)* With constant relative risk aversion the share in stock does not depend on wealth.

With $u = \ln x$, $u'(wy) = 1/(wy)$, so $w$ cancels from the first-order condition and $\alpha^*$ is the same for a student with $\$1{,}000$ and a retiree with $\$1{,}000{,}000$. The same holds for every $u$ with constant $r_R$. With constant absolute risk aversion, $u = -e^{-ax}$, the dollar amount $\alpha w$ held in stock is fixed instead, so the share falls as wealth rises. This matches the split between fixed and proportional gambles in [[6.6.2 Risk Aversion]].

# Derivation

We want the first-order condition, the sign of the slope at $\alpha = 0$, the second-order condition, and the log closed form. Final wealth is $W_- = w(1 + \alpha r_-)$ in the bad state and $W_+ = w(1 + \alpha r_+)$ in the good state, with $dW_\pm/d\alpha = w\,r_\pm$. By the chain rule,
$$EU'(\alpha) = (1-p)\,w r_-\,u'(W_-) + p\,w r_+\,u'(W_+).$$
Setting it to zero gives the first-order condition. Differentiating again,
$$EU''(\alpha) = (1-p)\,(w r_-)^2\,u''(W_-) + p\,(w r_+)^2\,u''(W_+) < 0 .$$
At $\alpha = 0$ both states have wealth $w$, so $EU'(0) = w\,u'(w)\,[(1-p)\,r_- + p\,r_+] = w\,u'(w)\,Er > 0$. By [[7.1 Unconstrained Optimality Conditions]], a strictly concave function with a positive slope at $0$ is not maximized at $0$, so $\alpha^* > 0$. For $u(x) = bx$, $EU(\alpha) = bw + \alpha\,bw\,Er$ is linear and increasing, so $\alpha^* = 1$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The log closed form below goes beyond the lecture.

For $u = \ln x$, $u'(W) = 1/W$ and the factor $w$ cancels:
$$\frac{(1-p)\,r_-}{1 + \alpha r_-} + \frac{p\,r_+}{1 + \alpha r_+} = 0 .$$
Multiplying by $(1 + \alpha r_-)(1 + \alpha r_+)$ gives $(1-p)\,r_- + p\,r_+ + \alpha\,r_+r_-\big[(1-p) + p\big] = 0$, so $Er + \alpha\,r_+r_- = 0$ and $\alpha^* = -Er/(r_+r_-) = Er/(r_+|r_-|)$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) The Risk-Neutral Investor
>A stock returns $+50\%$ or $-30\%$ with equal odds. The bond returns nothing.
>
>>[!example]- Find the risk-neutral person's share in the stock and her expected final wealth. Then redo the problem for a stock that returns $+20\%$ or $-30\%$, for her and for a risk-averse person.
>>Here $Er = 0.5 \cdot 0.5 + 0.5 \cdot (-0.3) = 0.1 > 0$. Her objective $bw + \alpha\,bw\,Er$ rises with $\alpha$, so $\alpha^* = 1$. Her expected final wealth is $1.1w$, even though she ends with $0.7w$ half the time.
>>
>>The second stock has $Er = 0.5 \cdot 0.2 + 0.5 \cdot (-0.3) = -0.05 < 0$. Her objective now falls with $\alpha$, so $\alpha^* = 0$. A risk-averse person has $EU'(0) = w\,u'(w)\,Er < 0$ and a concave objective, so she also picks $\alpha^* = 0$.

>[!example] (Medium) The Log Investor
>An investor with $u = \ln x$ and $w = \$10{,}000$ faces the first stock of the Easy example.
>
>>[!example]- Find her share, her wealth in each state, and her share if her wealth doubles.
>>The closed form gives $\alpha^* = \frac{0.1}{0.5 \times 0.3} = \frac23$, so she puts $\$6{,}667$ into the stock. Her final wealth is $10{,}000(1 + \tfrac23 \cdot 0.5) = \$13{,}333$ in the good state and $10{,}000(1 - \tfrac23 \cdot 0.3) = \$8{,}000$ in the bad state. The first-order condition checks out: $\frac{0.5 \times (-0.3)}{0.8} + \frac{0.5 \times 0.5}{1.333} = -0.1875 + 0.1875 = 0$.
>>
>>With $w = \$20{,}000$ the share is still $2/3$, because $w$ does not appear in the closed form. She doubles her dollar holding to $\$13{,}333$.

>[!example] (Hard) Three Investors and a Corner
>Three investors face the first stock of the Easy example. Their utilities are $\sqrt x$, $\ln x$, and $-1/x$, with relative risk aversion $\tfrac12$, $1$, and $2$.
>
>>[!example]- Find each share in the stock.
>>For $\sqrt x$, $u'(x) = \tfrac12 x^{-1/2}$, and the first-order condition becomes $0.3\,(1 - 0.3\alpha)^{-1/2} = 0.5\,(1 + 0.5\alpha)^{-1/2}$. Squaring gives $0.09(1 + 0.5\alpha) = 0.25(1 - 0.3\alpha)$, so $0.12\,\alpha = 0.16$ and $\alpha = 4/3$. This violates $\alpha \le 1$. The slope at $\alpha = 1$ has the sign of $-0.3\,(0.7)^{-1/2} + 0.5\,(1.5)^{-1/2} = -0.359 + 0.408 > 0$, so the objective still rises at $1$ and $\alpha^* = 1$.
>>
>>For $\ln x$ the Medium example gives $\alpha^* = 2/3$. For $-1/x$, $u'(x) = x^{-2}$, and the first-order condition becomes $0.3\,(1 - 0.3\alpha)^{-2} = 0.5\,(1 + 0.5\alpha)^{-2}$. Taking square roots gives $\frac{1 + 0.5\alpha}{1 - 0.3\alpha} = \sqrt{5/3} = 1.291$, so $0.887\,\alpha = 0.291$ and $\alpha^* = 0.328$. All three hold a positive share, and the share falls as relative risk aversion rises.
