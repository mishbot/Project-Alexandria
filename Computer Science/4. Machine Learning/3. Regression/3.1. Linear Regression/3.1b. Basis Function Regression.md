>[!note] Basis Function Regression
>**Basis function regression** fits a curve by running linear regression on fixed transformations of the input. It picks $M$ functions $\phi_1, \dots, \phi_M$ of $x$, called **basis functions**, adds the constant $\phi_0(x) = 1$, and models
>$$y_i = \sum_{j=0}^{M} \beta_j \phi_j(x_i) + \varepsilon_i, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n,$$
>where $\beta_0$ is the intercept and $\varepsilon_i$ is noise with mean zero. The model can be nonlinear in $x$, but it is linear in $\boldsymbol\beta$. Evaluating each basis function at each input gives the **feature matrix**
>$$\boldsymbol\Phi = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \phi_1(x_1) & \cdots & \phi_M(x_1) \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 1 & \phi_1(x_n) & \cdots & \phi_M(x_n) \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (M+1)},$$
>whose row $i$ holds the features of sample $i$. Least squares with $\boldsymbol\Phi$ in place of the design matrix $\mathbf{X}$ gives
>$$\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi)^{-1}\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\mathbf{y},$$
>which needs the $M + 1$ columns of $\boldsymbol\Phi$ to be linearly independent. Everything in [[3.1a. Multiple Linear Regression|multiple linear regression]] carries over. This includes the hat matrix, the $t$-tests and the residual variance with $n - M - 1$ degrees of freedom.
>
>Four bases are common for a scalar input $x$:
>
>- **Polynomial**: $\phi_j(x) = x^j$ for $j = 1, \dots, M$, which fits a polynomial of degree $M$.
>- **Piecewise linear**: the linear term $x$ plus one ramp $\mathrm{ReLU}(x - \tau_k) = \max(0, x - \tau_k)$ for each knot $\tau_k$. Each ramp, also called a hinge function, bends the fitted line at its knot. The [[3. Activation Functions|ReLU]] here is the same function that neural networks use.
>- **Gaussian radial basis function (RBF)**: $\phi_j(x) = \exp\!\big(-(x - \mu_j)^2 / (2s^2)\big)$, a bump with center $\mu_j$ and width $s$.
>- **Fourier**: the pair $\cos(2\pi f_k t)$ and $\sin(2\pi f_k t)$ for each known frequency $f_k$, which fits a periodic signal of time $t$. This is the sinusoidal model of [[2.4 Time Series Regression|time series regression]].
>
>The number of basis functions $M$ sets the flexibility of the curve. Adding a basis function never raises the training error, so $M$ is chosen on validation data or by [[2.3.2. Cross-Validation|cross-validation]]. Too few functions underfit and too many overfit, as described in [[2.3. Generalization]]. When the knots, centers or frequencies are learned as well, the model is no longer linear in its parameters. It then becomes a [[3.3. Nonlinear Regression|nonlinear regression]] or a [[2. Neural Networks|neural network]].

# Intuition

A straight line cannot follow a curve. Least squares, however, works with any columns in the design matrix, whatever function produced them. Basis function regression keeps the least squares machinery and changes the inputs. Each basis function turns $x$ into a new column, such as $x^2$ or a ramp that starts at $x = 3$. OLS then picks the weight of each column. The name comes from linear algebra. The fitted curve is a linear combination of the chosen functions, so it always lies in the space they span.

Each basis allows a different family of curve shapes. A polynomial is smooth everywhere, and moving one data point changes the whole curve. A ramp changes the slope only to the right of its knot. A Gaussian bump changes the curve only near its center. Sines and cosines repeat, so they suit data that cycle with a known period. A good basis needs few functions for the shapes the data show, which keeps the variance of the fit low.

# Properties

#### *(Slope Changes at Knots)* In the piecewise-linear basis, each ramp coefficient is the change in slope at its knot.

Take a continuous piecewise-linear function with knots $\tau_1 < \dots < \tau_K$. Its slope is $s_0$ left of the first knot and $s_k$ right of knot $\tau_k$. It can be written as
$$g(x) = c + s_0 x + \sum_{k=1}^{K} (s_k - s_{k-1})\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x - \tau_k),$$
where $c + s_0 x$ is the line before the first knot. Each ramp is zero left of its knot, so it adds its slope change only from the knot onward. OLS on this basis therefore estimates an intercept, a starting slope and $K$ slope changes. Since the linear term equals $\mathrm{ReLU}(x) - \mathrm{ReLU}(-x)$, the whole model is a one-hidden-layer ReLU network with fixed first-layer weights. With enough knots placed close together, this basis approximates any continuous function on a closed interval (CS 182 lecture 1).

#### *(Fixed Versus Learned Basis)* Fixed knots keep the fit a convex least squares problem, and learned knots turn the model into a neural network.

A hidden ReLU unit $\mathrm{ReLU}(w_k x + b_k)$ with $w_k \neq 0$ has its elbow at $x = -b_k/w_k$. If only the output weights are trained, the hidden units form a fixed basis. The fit is then OLS or [[3.2.1. Ridge Regression|ridge regression]] on $\boldsymbol\Phi$, solved in closed form. Training every layer by [[6.4. Gradient Descent|gradient descent]] also moves the elbows toward the inputs where the target bends. This is feature learning in its simplest form. With the same number of units, learned elbows can beat randomly placed fixed elbows (CS 182 homework 1). The loss is then no longer a convex quadratic in the parameters. Training is slower and can stop at a local minimum.

#### *(Polynomial Instability)* High-degree polynomial bases are badly conditioned and oscillate strongly near the ends of the data.

On $[0, 1]$ the columns $x^9$ and $x^{10}$ are nearly parallel, so $\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi$ is close to singular. Take $50$ equally spaced inputs on $[0, 1]$. The condition number of $\boldsymbol\Phi$ is then about $1.2 \times 10^2$ at degree $3$ and $2 \times 10^7$ at degree $10$. Forming $\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi$ squares it to about $4 \times 10^{14}$. Solving the normal equations in double precision can then lose all but one digit. Rescaling $x$ to $[-1, 1]$ lowers the degree-10 value to about $2.9 \times 10^3$, and orthogonal polynomials lower it further. The second problem remains after any rescaling. Interpolating $1/(1 + 25x^2)$ at equally spaced points on $[-1, 1]$ shows it. The maximum error is $1.92$ at degree $10$ and $59.8$ at degree $20$. The worst errors sit near the ends of the interval. This is **Runge's phenomenon**. Piecewise bases such as ramps avoid it, because each piece is a low-degree polynomial.

#### *(Choosing the Number of Basis Functions)* Training error alone always favors more basis functions, so the count is chosen on held-out data.

Adding a column to $\boldsymbol\Phi$ can only enlarge its column space, so the training residual sum of squares cannot rise. With $M + 1 = n$ independent columns, $\boldsymbol\Phi$ is square and invertible, and the fit passes through every point. A polynomial of degree $n - 1$ through $n$ distinct inputs is the standard case. Test error usually falls at first and then rises, which is the tradeoff in [[2.3.1. Bias-Variance Decomposition]]. There are two practical methods. The first compares a few values of $M$, or a few knot sets, by validation error or cross-validation. The second keeps a large basis and adds a ridge penalty, with $\lambda$ chosen by cross-validation.

#### *(Orthogonal Fourier Columns)* At the Fourier frequencies $j/n$, the sine and cosine columns are orthogonal, so each coefficient is a single sum.

Take times $t = 1, \dots, n$ and frequencies $f_j = j/n$ with $0 < j < n/2$. The columns $\cos(2\pi f_j t)$ and $\sin(2\pi f_j t)$ are then orthogonal to each other and to the constant column. They are also orthogonal to every other such pair. Each has squared length $n/2$. The matrix $\boldsymbol\Phi^\top\boldsymbol\Phi$ is diagonal, and OLS reduces to
$$\hat\beta_0 = \bar y, \qquad a_j = \frac{2}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} y_t \cos(2\pi f_j t), \qquad b_j = \frac{2}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} y_t \sin(2\pi f_j t),$$
where $a_j$ and $b_j$ are the cosine and sine coefficients at $f_j$. Adding or dropping a frequency leaves the other coefficients unchanged. The sum $a_j^2 + b_j^2$ is the scaled [[2.3.2 Periodogram|periodogram]] at $f_j$ (STAT 153 lecture 12). A frequency between the Fourier frequencies breaks the orthogonality. An unknown frequency makes the model nonlinear.

#### *(Growth with Input Dimension)* A full polynomial basis of degree $M$ in $p$ inputs has $\binom{p + M}{M}$ functions.

With a vector input $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p$, a polynomial basis contains every product of inputs up to total degree $M$. Examples are $x_1 x_2$ and $x_3^2$. Counting these monomials, including the constant, gives $\binom{p + M}{M}$. Ten inputs at degree $3$ need $286$ columns, and $100$ inputs need $176{,}851$. OLS needs at least as many samples as columns. A grid of bumps or knots grows faster, since $m$ centers per input give $m^p$ functions. This is one form of the [[7.2. Curse of Dimensionality|curse of dimensionality]]. Neural networks avoid a fixed grid by learning a small number of features from the data.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Income Tax as a Sum of Ramps
>A tax code charges $10\%$ on the first \$10k of income, $20\%$ on income from \$10k to \$40k, and $35\%$ above \$40k. Income $x$ is in thousands of dollars.
>
>>[!example]- Write the tax owed in the piecewise-linear basis. What is the tax on \$55k?
>>The tax is piecewise linear with knots at $10$ and $40$. Its slope starts at $0.10$, rises by $0.10$ at the first knot and rises by $0.15$ at the second. So
>>$$T(x) = 0.10\,x + 0.10\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x - 10) + 0.15\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x - 40).$$
>>At $x = 55$ this gives $5.5 + 4.5 + 2.25 = 12.25$, or \$12,250. The bracket calculation agrees, since $0.10 \cdot 10 + 0.20 \cdot 30 + 0.35 \cdot 15 = 1 + 6 + 5.25$. Suppose an analyst has tax bills for at least four distinct incomes, with every bracket represented. OLS on the columns $1$, $x$, $\mathrm{ReLU}(x - 10)$ and $\mathrm{ReLU}(x - 40)$ then returns $0$, $0.10$, $0.10$ and $0.15$ exactly. Each ramp coefficient is a change in the marginal tax rate.

>[!example] (Medium) Seasonal Sales with a Fourier Basis
>A toy store records quarterly sales, in thousands of units, for two years: $36, 7, 28, 57, 38, 5, 26, 59$. The pattern repeats every four quarters, so the frequency is $1/4$ cycle per quarter. The model is $y_t = \beta_0 + a\cos(2\pi t/4) + b\sin(2\pi t/4) + \varepsilon_t$ for $t = 1, \dots, 8$.
>
>>[!example]- Fit the model without inverting a matrix. How large is the seasonal swing, and which quarter peaks?
>>With $n = 8$, the frequency $1/4 = 2/8$ is a Fourier frequency. The cosine column is $(0, -1, 0, 1, 0, -1, 0, 1)$, and the sine column is $(1, 0, -1, 0, 1, 0, -1, 0)$. They are orthogonal to each other and to the constant, and each has squared length $4 = n/2$. So $\hat\beta_0 = \bar y = 256/8 = 32$, and
>>$$a = \tfrac{2}{8}(-7 + 57 - 5 + 59) = 26, \qquad b = \tfrac{2}{8}(36 - 28 + 38 - 26) = 5.$$
>>The fitted values for quarters 1 to 4 are $37$, $6$, $27$ and $58$. They repeat in the second year. Every residual is $\pm 1$, so the residual sum of squares is $8$. The amplitude is $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{701} \approx 26.5$ thousand units around the mean of $32$. Sales peak in the fourth quarter, which matches holiday shopping. A general OLS solver returns the same three coefficients.

>[!example] (Hard) A Knot at Every Data Point
>A sensor gives readings $y = (2, 4, 3, 5, 4)$ at inputs $x = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5)$. An analyst builds a piecewise-linear basis with a knot at every input. The columns are $1$, $x$ and $\mathrm{ReLU}(x - k)$ for $k = 1, \dots, 5$.
>
>>[!example]- Which columns are redundant? What does OLS return, and how does it compare with a straight line?
>>The matrix $\boldsymbol\Phi$ has $7$ columns but only $5$ rows, so its rank is at most $5$. The column $\mathrm{ReLU}(x - 5)$ is zero at every input. The column $\mathrm{ReLU}(x - 1)$ equals $x - 1$ on the data, a combination of $1$ and $x$. Dropping both leaves $5$ independent columns, so $\boldsymbol\Phi$ becomes square and invertible. The fit passes through every point:
>>$$\hat y = 2x - 3\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x - 2) + 3\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x - 3) - 3\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x - 4).$$
>>The slope alternates between $2$ and $-1$, so the slope changes of $\pm 3$ follow the noise. The training error is zero. The OLS line is $\hat y = 2.1 + 0.5x$, with residual sum of squares $2.7$. Leave-one-out [[2.3.2. Cross-Validation|cross-validation]] refits each model with knots at the remaining inputs. It drops the redundant ramps at the first and last remaining knots, as above. It gives a mean squared error of $4.95$ for the interpolating curve and $1.67$ for the line. The line generalizes better. A ridge penalty on the ramp coefficients would give a fit between these two extremes.
