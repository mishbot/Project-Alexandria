>[!note] Linear Momentum
>**Linear momentum** ($\text{kg} \cdot \text{m} / \text{s}$) is a measure of an object’s tendency to stay in motion and is conserved in isolated systems. Mathematically, the momentum of an object is defined as the product of its mass and its velocity, such that
>$$\vec{p} = m \vec{v}.$$
>Alternatively, since a force is required to change the momentum of an object, [[1.1g.2 Newton's Second Law|net force]] can be defined as the derivative of momentum with respect to time, such that
>$$\sum\vec{F} = \frac{d}{dt}\vec{p} = \frac{d}{dt}(m\vec{v})=m\vec{a}.$$
>The equation above implies that if ==no external force== acts on an object, then the total momentum of that object is said to be conserved. As such, the sum of the momenta of two colliding objects $A$ and $B$ is conserved when
>$$\underbrace{m_A\vec{v}_A + m_B\vec{v}_B}_\text{momentum before collision} = \underbrace{m_A\vec{v}'_A + m_B\vec{v}'_B}_\text{momentum after collision}.$$

# Intuition
Although the law of conservation of linear momentum was discovered experimentally, we can prove it using Newton's laws of motion. Consider a large number of objects colliding, such that their linear momentum is equal to $\vec{P}=\vec{p}_1+\vec{p}_2+\ldots=\sum\vec{p}_i$. Consider also that there are external forces acting on the objects, such as friction, drag, gravity, etc. Momentum is conserved if we measure it immediately before the collision, and immediately after.

Differentiating $\vec{P}$, we find that
$$\frac{d}{dt}\vec{P}=\sum\frac{d}{dt}\vec{p}_i=\sum\vec{F}_i.$$

For any object $i$, $\vec{F}_i$ represents its net force. The forces are either external (exerted by objects outside of the system) or internal (exerted by objects inside the system). Since by Newton's third law internal forces occur in pairs, they cancel each other out. Therefore, $\vec{F}_i$ consists only of external forces. If $\vec{F}_i=0$, then the linear momentum of object $i$ is conserved.
