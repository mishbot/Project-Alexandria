>[!note] Welfare Applications
>**Welfare analysis** ranks market outcomes by **total surplus** $W$, the sum of consumer surplus, producer surplus, and government revenue ([[6.8.5 Consumer and Producer Surplus]]). The **deadweight loss** (DWL) of a policy is the fall in $W$ it causes. A transfer of money from one group to another leaves $W$ unchanged.
>- **Per-unit tax $t$.** With $p$ the price consumers pay, the equilibrium solves $Y^S(p - t, w, r) = X^D(p, M)$ ([[6.8.4 Tax Incidence]]). Quantity falls from $Y^*$ to $Y_t$. Consumers lose the area between $p^*$ and $p$, producers lose the area between $p^*$ and $p - t$, and the government collects $R = tY_t$. With $P^D$ and $P^S$ the inverse demand and supply curves, the DWL is the triangle over the units no longer traded:
>$$DWL = \int_{Y_t}^{Y^*}\big(P^D(Y) - P^S(Y)\big)\,dY, \qquad DWL = \tfrac12\,t\,(Y^* - Y_t) \ \text{ for linear curves}.$$
>- The DWL is zero when the tax leaves quantity unchanged. This happens with perfectly inelastic supply ($\varepsilon_{S,p} = 0$, producers bear the tax) or perfectly inelastic demand ($\varepsilon_{D,p} = 0$, consumers bear it). With perfectly elastic supply ($\varepsilon_{S,p} \to \infty$), consumers bear the whole tax and the DWL is positive.
>- **Opening to trade.** Without trade the market clears at price $p_D^*$ and quantity $X_D^*$. If the good is available abroad at $p_T^* < p_D^*$, the domestic price falls to $p_T^*$. Consumption rises to $X_T^* = X^D(p_T^*) > X_D^*$, domestic production falls to $Y^S(p_T^*)$, and imports are $X_T^* - Y^S(p_T^*)$. Consumers gain more than firms lose:
>$$\Delta W = \int_{p_T^*}^{p_D^*}\big(X^D(p) - Y^S(p)\big)\,dp > 0.$$
>- Total surplus rises, but domestic firms lose profit and some employment. So opening to trade is a difficult trade-off.
>- **Rent control** caps the rent at $\bar p < p^*$. Landlords rent out $Y^S(\bar p) < Y^*$ units, tenants want $X^D(\bar p) > Y^*$, and the gap is a **shortage**. Cost 1: some units are no longer rented, a DWL triangle between demand and supply from $Y^S(\bar p)$ to $Y^*$. Cost 2: the units that are rented may go to tenants who value them less than tenants who are turned away. Cost 2 adds to the triangle.
>- Every application follows three steps: find the new price and quantity, measure the change in each group's area, and add the changes.

# Intuition

In a competitive market, the units traded are exactly those whose value to some buyer exceeds their cost to some seller. That is why the equilibrium maximizes total surplus. A policy can then change $W$ in two ways. It can move money between groups, which changes who gains but not $W$. Or it can change which units are traded, which changes $W$. A tax does both. The revenue rectangle moves money from buyers and sellers to the government. The triangle to its right is the surplus on units that a buyer valued above their cost but that are no longer traded, because the gap is smaller than $t$. Nobody receives this triangle. The diagram uses the market of the Easy example.

# Properties

#### *(Elasticity and the Loss)* The DWL comes from the fall in quantity, so it is zero only when one side of the market does not respond to price.

Who bears a tax and how much surplus is lost are different questions. Incidence depends on the ratio of the two elasticities, as in [[6.8.4 Tax Incidence]]. The DWL depends on how far quantity falls. With a vertical supply curve, as when capacity is fixed, quantity stays at $Y^*$. Producers absorb the whole tax as a lower price, and the revenue equals their loss, so the DWL is zero. With a horizontal supply curve, as under constant returns to scale, the producer price cannot fall. Consumers pay the whole tax, quantity falls along the demand curve, and the triangle appears. The figure shows both cases.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture showed the tax triangle on a graph. The formula below for how fast it grows with $t$ is an extension.

#### *(Quadratic Growth of DWL)* Doubling a tax roughly quadruples its deadweight loss.

Suppose inverse demand falls by $b$ per unit and inverse supply rises by $s$ per unit. The tax opens a wedge $t = (b + s)(Y^* - Y_t)$, so quantity falls by $t/(b + s)$. The triangle has base $t/(b + s)$ and height $t$, so $DWL = t^2/\big(2(b + s)\big)$. Revenue $tY_t$ grows less than in proportion to $t$, because the tax base $Y_t$ shrinks. The loss per dollar of revenue therefore rises with the rate. For a government that must raise a fixed sum, small taxes on several goods in separate markets lose less than one large tax. For curved demand and supply the result holds for small $t$.

#### *(Gains From Trade)* The net gain from opening a market equals the area between demand and supply between the two prices.

When the price falls from $p_D^*$ to $p_T^*$, consumers gain $\int_{p_T^*}^{p_D^*} X^D(p)\,dp$ and producers lose $\int_{p_T^*}^{p_D^*} Y^S(p)\,dp$. The difference integrates $X^D(p) - Y^S(p)$, the imports the country would buy at each price. The integrand is zero at $p_D^*$ and positive below it, so the net gain is positive whenever the world price is lower. The producers' loss is fully offset by part of the consumers' gain. The gain is a triangle when the curves are linear.

>[!warning] Lecture Wording: Lower World Price
>The lecture says that opening to trade is irrelevant unless the world price is lower than $p_D^*$. This holds if domestic firms cannot sell abroad. If they can, a higher world price makes the country an exporter. Producers then gain more than consumers lose. The formula for $\Delta W$ still gives a positive number, because the integrand and the direction of integration both change sign.

#### *(Misallocation Under Rent Control)* The triangle assumes that the rented units go to the tenants who value them most, and a cap does not ensure this.

At the market rent, a tenant who values a unit more outbids one who values it less, so the units go to the highest values. Under a cap, many tenants want a unit at $\bar p$ and the units go by luck, by waiting, or to those who already live there. A tenant who values a unit at $\$2{,}100$ can hold it while one who values it at $\$3{,}900$ is turned away. Cost 2 is the value lost in such matches. The Hard example shows that it can exceed Cost 1. In San Francisco and Berkeley, rent control limits yearly rent increases to about $1$ to $2\%$ on all rental units built before 1979. Its stated aim is to keep the area affordable.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Taxing a Small Market
>Market supply is $Y^S = 25p$ and market demand is $X^D = 100 - 50p$, so $p^* = 4/3$, $Y^* = 100/3$, $CS = 11.11$, and $PS = 22.22$. The government adds a tax of $t = 0.6$ per unit.
>
>>[!example]- Find the new prices and quantity, the four areas, and the share of the tax paid by consumers.
>>The equilibrium solves $25(p - 0.6) = 100 - 50p$, so $75p = 115$ and $p = 1.533$. Producers receive $p - t = 0.933$, and $Y_t = 100 - 50(1.533) = 23.33$. Consumer surplus is $\frac12 \cdot 23.33 \cdot (2 - 1.533) = 5.44$. Producer surplus is $\frac12 \cdot 23.33 \cdot 0.933 = 10.89$. Revenue is $0.6 \cdot 23.33 = 14$. The DWL is $\frac12 \cdot 0.6 \cdot (33.33 - 23.33) = 3$. The four areas add up to $5.44 + 10.89 + 14 + 3 = 33.33$, the total surplus before the tax.
>>
>>The consumer price rose by $0.2$, so consumers pay $0.2/0.6 = 1/3$ of the tax. At the old equilibrium $\varepsilon_{S,p} = 1$ and $\varepsilon_{D,p} = -50 \cdot \frac{4/3}{100/3} = -2$. The formula $\varepsilon_{S,p}/(\varepsilon_{S,p} - \varepsilon_{D,p}) = 1/3$ agrees. Demand is the more elastic side, so producers bear more.

>[!example] (Medium) Opening the Rice Market
>A country's rice market has demand $X^D = 100 - 2p$ and supply $Y^S = 3p$, with $p$ in dollars per bag and quantities in millions of bags. Rice becomes available abroad at $p_T^* = 10$.
>
>>[!example]- Find production, consumption, and imports before and after, and each group's gain or loss. Could the government make everyone better off?
>>Without trade $100 - 2p = 3p$, so $p_D^* = 20$ and $X_D^* = 60$, all produced at home. At $p_T^* = 10$, consumption is $80$, domestic production is $30$, and imports are $50$. Consumers gain $\int_{10}^{20}(100 - 2p)\,dp = 700$. Producers lose $\int_{10}^{20} 3p\,dp = 450$, three quarters of their surplus of $600$. The net gain is $\int_{10}^{20}(100 - 5p)\,dp = 250 = \frac12 \cdot 10 \cdot 50$, in millions of dollars. Domestic output halves, so jobs in the industry fall.
>>
>>The government could collect $450$ from consumers as a lump sum and pay it to the producers. Producers would then be exactly as well off as before, and consumers would still gain $250$. Such transfers are hard to run in practice, which is why trade remains a difficult trade-off.

>[!example] (Hard) Who Gains From Rent Control
>A city's apartment market has demand $X^D = 40 - p$ and supply $Y^S = p - 10$, with $p$ in hundreds of dollars per month and quantities in thousands of units. The city caps rent at $\bar p = 20$. Tenants' values are spread evenly along the demand curve.
>
>>[!example]- Compute Cost 1. Then assume the rented units go at random to the tenants who want one at $\bar p$, and compute Cost 2. Do tenants as a group gain?
>>The market rent is $p^* = 25$ with $Y^* = 15$. At $\bar p = 20$, landlords rent $10$ and tenants want $20$, a shortage of $10$. At $Y = 10$ the demand price is $30$ and the supply price is $20$, so Cost 1 is $\frac12 \cdot 5 \cdot (30 - 20) = 25$.
>>
>>The $20$ thousand tenants who want a unit value it between $20$ and $40$. A random winner values it at $30$ on average, so the $10$ units yield total value $300$. Given to the top $10$ thousand, with values between $30$ and $40$, they would yield $350$. Cost 2 is $50$, twice Cost 1. Total surplus falls from $225$ to $150$.
>>
>>Before the cap, tenants had $CS = \frac12 \cdot 15 \cdot 15 = 112.5$. After it, the winners gain $300 - 10 \cdot 20 = 100$, so tenants as a group lose $12.5$. Landlords lose $112.5 - 50 = 62.5$. Lucky tenants gain. Tenants as a group, whom the policy aims to help, end up worse off.
