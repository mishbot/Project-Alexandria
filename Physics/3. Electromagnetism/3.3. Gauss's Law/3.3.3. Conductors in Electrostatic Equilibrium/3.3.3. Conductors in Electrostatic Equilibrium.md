>[!note] Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium
>A [[3.1.3. Conductors and Insulators|conductor]] is in **electrostatic equilibrium** when none of its charges move. Three facts then hold for a conductor of any shape:
>- The field inside the conducting material is zero. An applied field $\vec E_{\text{app}}$ pushes the free electrons opposite to itself. The electrons pile up on one surface and leave positive ions exposed on the other. These separated charges make an induced field $\vec E_{\text{ind}}$ that opposes the applied one. The electrons keep moving until $\vec E_{\text{app}} + \vec E_{\text{ind}} = 0$ inside.
>- Any net charge of the conductor sits on its surface. The interior of the material holds no net charge.
>- Right outside the surface, the field is perpendicular to the surface. It points away from the surface where the charge there is positive, and toward it where the charge is negative.
>
>The field right outside depends on the local **surface charge density** $\sigma$, the charge per area of the surface. The field component along the outward perpendicular is
>$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}.$$
>A negative $\sigma$ gives a negative $E$, a field pointing into the surface. The field jumps from $0$ inside the metal to $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ right outside. The density $\sigma$, and with it the field, can change from place to place on the surface.
>
>These facts hold only while no charge flows. A conductor carrying a [[3.6.1. Electric Current|current]] has a field inside it. The zero field inside is known without any symmetry. [[3.3.2. Gauss's Law|Gauss's law]] can therefore locate the charge on a conductor of any shape.

# Intuition

A metal holds an enormous number of free electrons, about $10^{23}$ in each cubic centimeter. Any field inside the metal pushes on them, and they move. They keep moving until their new arrangement cancels the field. A tiny shift of charge is enough, so the metal settles almost at once. A field along the surface would push charge along the surface in the same way. That charge would move until the field along the surface was gone. Only a perpendicular field can remain.

A classroom demonstration, Faraday's ice-pail experiment, shows where the charge goes. A charged ball was placed in a copper beaker wired to an electroscope. No charge was found on the beaker's inner surface. The charge sat on its outer surface.

The animation places a neutral metal slab in a uniform field. Electrons pile up on one face and leave positive ions on the other. Their field grows until it cancels the applied field inside the metal.

# Derivation

The zero field inside comes from the motion of the free electrons, as described above. The other facts follow from it with Gauss's law.

Gauss's law first shows that the interior holds no net charge. Suppose a charge $q_{\text{in}}$ sat somewhere inside the material. Draw a small Gaussian surface around it, lying entirely in the metal. The field is zero at every point of that surface, so the flux is zero. Gauss's law then gives $q_{\text{in}} = 0$. Any net charge must therefore sit on the surface.

The field at the surface is perpendicular for a mechanical reason. A field component along the surface would push the surface charges along it. They would move until that component disappeared.

The size $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ comes from a pillbox, a short closed cylinder through the surface. One flat face of area $A$ lies inside the metal. The other lies right outside, parallel to the surface. The side wall is perpendicular to the surface. The inner face has zero flux, because $E = 0$ in the metal. The side wall has zero flux too. Outside, $\vec E$ runs along the wall, and inside, $\vec E$ is zero. Only the outer face contributes, with flux $EA$. The pillbox is small, so $E$ is constant across it, and it encloses the charge $\sigma A$. Gauss's law gives
$$EA = \frac{\sigma A}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}.$$
The area $A$ cancels, so the result holds at every spot of the surface.

# Properties

#### *(Twice the Sheet Field)* The field at a conductor's surface is $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$, twice the $\sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)$ of a lone sheet with the same density.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not compare the two results. The comparison uses the sheet field of [[3.3.2c Planar Symmetry]], from Giancoli chapter 22.

A [[3.3.2c Planar Symmetry|lone charged sheet]] sends flux out of both faces of a pillbox. A conductor sends none through the inner face, because the field in the metal is zero. All the flux leaves through the outer face, so the field there is doubled. Superposition gives the same answer. The local patch of charge makes $\sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)$ on each side of itself. All the other charges together make another $\sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)$ at that spot, pointing outward. Inside the metal the two cancel, and outside they add. A thin isolated metal plate with charge $Q$ and face area $A$ is a test case. Each face holds $Q/2$, so each face has $\sigma = Q/(2A)$. The field outside is then $Q/(2\varepsilon_0 A)$. That is the sheet result for the total density $Q/A$.

#### *(Cavities of Any Shape)* A charge $q$ inside a cavity of any shape puts $-q$ on the cavity's wall.

Draw a Gaussian surface inside the metal that goes around the cavity. The surface can follow any bends of the metal. The field is zero at every point of it, so its flux is zero. The enclosed charge, $q$ plus the charge on the cavity wall, must then be zero. A charge in a box-shaped hole inside a metal brick therefore puts $-q$ on the hole's walls. The brick's outer surface carries the rest of the brick's charge, by [[3.1.2. Conservation of Charge|conservation of charge]]. Neither step uses symmetry. The shapes of the cavity and the brick affect only how the charge spreads over each wall.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Reading the Surface Charge
>Right outside a metal object, a field of $2.4 \times 10^{5}\ \text{N/C}$ points toward the surface.
>
>>[!example]- Find the surface charge density at that spot.
>>The field points into the surface, so the charge there is negative. Its size follows from $E = |\sigma|/\varepsilon_0$:
>>$$|\sigma| = \varepsilon_0 E = (8.85 \times 10^{-12})(2.4 \times 10^{5}) = 2.1 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C/m}^2.$$
>>The density is therefore $\sigma = -2.1\ \mu\text{C/m}^2$.

>[!example] (Medium) A Charged Metal Sphere
>A solid metal sphere of radius $R = 12\ \text{cm}$ carries $Q = +30\ \text{nC}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the surface charge density and the field right outside. Check the field with Gauss's law for a sphere.
>>The charge sits on the surface. It spreads evenly, since the sphere looks the same from every direction:
>>$$\sigma = \frac{Q}{4\pi R^2} = \frac{30 \times 10^{-9}}{4\pi (0.12)^2} = 1.66 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{C/m}^2.$$
>>The field right outside points away from the surface with size
>>$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{1.66 \times 10^{-7}}{8.85 \times 10^{-12}} = 1.87 \times 10^{4}\ \text{N/C}.$$
>>A Gaussian sphere of radius $R$ encloses $Q$, which gives $E = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2) = 1.87 \times 10^{4}\ \text{N/C}$. The two routes agree. Inside the metal the field is zero.

>[!example] (Hard) How Thin Is the Surface Charge?
>Air starts to conduct when the field in it exceeds about $3.0 \times 10^{6}\ \text{N/C}$. A metal in air can hold at most about this field right outside its surface. Aluminum has $1.81 \times 10^{23}$ free electrons per cubic centimeter, and its atoms sit about $2.55 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{cm}$ apart.
>
>>[!example]- Find the largest surface charge density an aluminum surface can hold in air. What fraction of the free electrons in one atomic layer does it involve?
>>The surface rule gives the largest density:
>>$$|\sigma|_{\max} = \varepsilon_0 E_{\max} = (8.85 \times 10^{-12})(3.0 \times 10^{6}) = 2.655 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{C/m}^2.$$
>>One square centimeter of surface then holds $2.655 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{C}$. In electrons, that is
>>$$N = \frac{2.655 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{C}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}} = 1.66 \times 10^{10}.$$
>>One atomic layer, one square centimeter in area, holds $(1.81 \times 10^{23})(2.55 \times 10^{-8}) = 4.62 \times 10^{15}$ free electrons. The fraction is
>>$$\frac{1.66 \times 10^{10}}{4.62 \times 10^{15}} = 3.6 \times 10^{-6}.$$
>>Even the largest possible surface charge changes a few millionths of the electrons in the outer atomic layer.
