>[!note] Adiabatic Process
>An **adiabatic process** changes the state of a gas with no heat flowing in or out,
>$$Q = 0.$$
>The [[2.5.2 First Law of Thermodynamics|first law]] then says that the work done by the gas comes entirely from its internal energy,
>$$W = -\Delta E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2}\,(P_1 V_1 - P_2 V_2) = \tfrac{d}{2}\, Nk\,(T_1 - T_2).$$
>An adiabatic expansion does work and cools. An adiabatic compression receives work and warms. Along the path an ideal gas with $d$ accessible [[2.3.4 Equipartition of Energy|degrees of freedom]] obeys
>$$T V^{2/d} = \text{const}, \qquad P V^{\gamma} = \text{const}, \qquad T V^{\gamma - 1} = \text{const}, \qquad \gamma = 1 + \frac{2}{d},$$
>where $\gamma$ is the **adiabatic constant**, equal to $5/3 = 1.67$ for a monatomic gas and $7/5 = 1.40$ for a diatomic gas near room temperature. The next note shows that the same $\gamma$ is the ratio $C_P / C_V$ of the two [[2.5.4 Molar Specific Heats|molar specific heats]]. The curve $P \propto V^{-\gamma}$ is called an **adiabat**. Because $\gamma > 1$ it is steeper than the isotherm $P \propto V^{-1}$ through the same point: an adiabatic expansion drops the pressure faster, because the gas cools as it expands.
>
>The other three standard processes fix one of $P$, $V$, and $T$. The adiabatic process fixes none of them and instead fixes $Q$, so its path has to be derived from the first law rather than read off the gas law. A process is adiabatic when the container is insulated, or when it happens too fast for heat to cross the walls. A rapid compression is adiabatic in practice even in a bare metal cylinder.

# Intuition

Push the plunger of a sealed syringe down hard and fast. The gas inside has no time to give its energy to the walls, so all the work of the push stays in the gas as kinetic energy of the molecules. The temperature climbs. Compress air to one fifteenth of its volume this way and it reaches about $600\ ^\circ\text{C}$, hot enough to light a scrap of paper at the bottom. That is the fire-syringe demonstration, and it is the working principle of a diesel engine. A diesel has no spark plug. Compression alone ignites the fuel. The reverse process, adiabatic expansion, is what cools the air rising in a thunderstorm and what chills the gas leaving an aerosol can.

The adiabat falls below the isotherm at every larger volume, because the expanding gas cools and a colder gas at the same volume has lower pressure. The area under the adiabat is therefore smaller. An adiabatic expansion does less work than an isothermal one between the same volumes, and it pays for that work out of its own internal energy instead of drawing on a reservoir.

# Properties

#### *(Steeper Than an Isotherm)* Through any state, the adiabat is steeper than the isotherm by the factor $\gamma$.

Differentiating $PV = \text{const}$ gives a slope of $-P/V$. Differentiating $PV^{\gamma} = \text{const}$ gives $-\gamma P/V$. Since $\gamma > 1$ the adiabat is the steeper curve. This is the fact that lets a cycle enclose area: the [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot cycle]] uses two isotherms and two adiabats, and the loop closes only because the two families of curves cross.

#### *(Endpoints Give the Work)* No integral is needed, because $W = -\Delta E_{\text{int}}$ and $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$ depends on the endpoints alone.

The area under the adiabat could be found by integrating $P = \text{const}/V^{\gamma}$, but the first law gives it at once as $\tfrac{d}{2}(P_1 V_1 - P_2 V_2)$. The only extra step is finding the missing coordinate of the endpoint. Given $V_1$, $V_2$, and $P_1$, the relation $P_2 = P_1 (V_1 / V_2)^{\gamma}$ supplies $P_2$. Given the temperatures instead, $W = \tfrac{d}{2} Nk (T_1 - T_2)$.

#### *(Fast Means Adiabatic)* A process that is quick compared with the time for heat to cross the container is adiabatic, whatever the walls are made of.

Heat crosses a container by [[2.6.1 Thermal Conduction|conduction]], which is slow. A piston stroke that takes a hundredth of a second exchanges almost no heat with a metal cylinder, so the compression stroke of an engine is close to adiabatic. Sound waves in air are adiabatic for the same reason, which is why the [[1.1w.1 Sound|speed of sound]] involves $\gamma$. The opposite limit, slow enough to stay at the bath temperature, is the [[2.5.3c Isothermal Process|isothermal]] process.

#### *(The Fire Syringe Rule)* Compressing by a volume factor $r$ multiplies the absolute temperature by $r^{\gamma - 1}$.

From $T V^{\gamma - 1} = \text{const}$, $T_2 / T_1 = (V_1 / V_2)^{\gamma - 1}$. For air, $\gamma - 1 = 0.4$, so a compression ratio of $10$ gives $10^{0.4} = 2.5$ and a compression ratio of $20$ gives $3.3$. A diesel engine with a ratio near $20$ takes air from $300\ \text{K}$ to about $1000\ \text{K}$ on the compression stroke alone. The same rule in reverse is why the temperature falls about $10\ ^\circ\text{C}$ for every kilometer of altitude in dry air.

#### *(One Member of a Family)* Every standard process is a path $PV^x = \text{const}$, and for $x \neq 1$ its work is $(P_1 V_1 - P_2 V_2)/(x - 1)$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture covered only the four standard processes. It did not treat paths $PV^x = \text{const}$ with other exponents. The homework and past exams use them.

The isobar has $x = 0$, the isotherm has $x = 1$, and the adiabat has $x = \gamma$. The isochore is the limit $x \to \infty$. Problems also use other exponents, such as $PV^2 = \text{const}$. On such a path $P = P_1 V_1^x / V^x$, and for $x \neq 1$ the work integral is
$$W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} \frac{P_1 V_1^x}{V^x}\,dV = \frac{P_1 V_1 - P_2 V_2}{x - 1} = \frac{Nk\,(T_1 - T_2)}{x - 1}.$$
For $x = \gamma$ this is the adiabatic work, since $1/(\gamma - 1) = d/2$. The heat follows from the first law, $Q = \Delta E_{\text{int}} + W$,
$$Q = \left(\frac{d}{2} - \frac{1}{x - 1}\right) Nk\,(T_2 - T_1).$$
For $x = \gamma$ the bracket is zero, as it must be. For $1 < x < \gamma$ the bracket is negative. The gas then gives off heat while it warms, and absorbs heat while it cools. In a cycle, the sign of each leg's $Q$ decides which legs make up $Q_H$. The endpoints of such a path obey $P_1 V_1^x = P_2 V_2^x$, like the adiabat's $P_1 V_1^\gamma = P_2 V_2^\gamma$.

#### *(Sudden Compression)* A piston that jumps to a new load compresses the gas with $Q = 0$, but not along $PV^\gamma$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover sudden processes. The homework uses this setup.

Put a heavier load on a piston and let go. The gas is squeezed too fast for heat to flow, so $Q = 0$. But the gas is not in equilibrium on the way, so $PV^\gamma = \text{const}$ does not hold. The gas pushes back against the constant outside pressure $P_2$ of the new load. Once the piston settles, the work done by the gas is $W = P_2\,(V_2 - V_1)$, as in [[2.5.1 Thermodynamic Work]]. The first law with $Q = 0$ gives
$$\tfrac{d}{2} Nk\,(T_2 - T_1) = -P_2\,(V_2 - V_1).$$
Replace $NkT$ by $PV$ at each end and solve for the final volume,
$$\frac{V_2}{V_1} = \frac{\tfrac{d}{2} P_1 + P_2}{(\tfrac{d}{2} + 1)\, P_2}.$$
Compared with a slow adiabatic compression to the same pressure, it ends at a larger volume and a higher temperature. As $P_2$ grows without limit, $V_2/V_1$ approaches $2/(d + 2)$, while $T_2$ grows without limit. A sudden expansion works the same way with $P_2 < P_1$.

# Derivation

We want to find the path in the $P$-$V$ plane along which no heat flows. Start from the first law in differential form with $dQ = 0$,
$$dE_{\text{int}} = -P\,dV.$$
For an ideal gas $E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2} NkT$, so $dE_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2} Nk\,dT$. Rather than eliminate $T$ at once, keep it and replace $P$ by $NkT/V$,
$$\tfrac{d}{2}\, Nk\, dT = -\frac{NkT}{V}\,dV.$$
The factor $Nk$ cancels. Divide both sides by $T$ and by $\tfrac{d}{2}$,
$$\frac{dT}{T} = -\frac{2}{d}\,\frac{dV}{V}.$$
Each side is the differential of a logarithm, $d(\ln T) = -\tfrac{2}{d}\, d(\ln V)$, so $\ln T + \tfrac{2}{d} \ln V$ is constant along the path, and
$$T V^{2/d} = \text{const}.$$
This is the headline relation between temperature and volume. To get pressure and volume, substitute $T = PV/Nk$,
$$\frac{PV}{Nk}\, V^{2/d} = \text{const}, \qquad P V^{1 + 2/d} = \text{const}',$$
where the new constant absorbs $Nk$. Writing $\gamma = 1 + 2/d$ gives $PV^{\gamma} = \text{const}$, and since $2/d = \gamma - 1$ the temperature form is $T V^{\gamma - 1} = \text{const}$. The next note defines the molar specific heats $C_V = \tfrac{d}{2} R$ and $C_P = (\tfrac{d}{2} + 1) R$, whose ratio $(d + 2)/d = 1 + 2/d$ is this same $\gamma$.

The work follows without an integral. Along the path $Q = 0$, so integrating the first law gives $W = -\Delta E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2}(P_1 V_1 - P_2 V_2)$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) The Fire Syringe
>Air at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is compressed rapidly to one fifteenth of its volume.
>
>>[!example]- Find the final temperature.
>>The compression is fast, so it is adiabatic. Air is diatomic with $\gamma = 1.40$, and
>>$$T_2 = T_1 \left( \frac{V_1}{V_2} \right)^{\gamma - 1} = (293\ \text{K})(15)^{0.40} = 866\ \text{K},$$
>>which is $593\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Paper ignites near $230\ ^\circ\text{C}$, so a scrap of tissue at the bottom of the cylinder catches fire. A slow push would let the heat leak into the cylinder walls, the process would be closer to isothermal, and nothing would ignite.

>[!example] (Medium) Helium Expanding in Insulation
>One liter of helium at $5.00\ \text{atm}$ and $300\ \text{K}$ expands adiabatically to $3.00\ \text{L}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the final pressure, the final temperature, and the work done.
>>Helium is monatomic, $d = 3$ and $\gamma = 5/3$. The pressure follows from $PV^{\gamma} = \text{const}$,
>>$$P_2 = P_1 \left( \frac{V_1}{V_2} \right)^{5/3} = (5.00\ \text{atm})(1/3)^{5/3} = 0.801\ \text{atm},$$
>>and the temperature from $TV^{\gamma - 1} = \text{const}$, $T_2 = (300\ \text{K})(1/3)^{2/3} = 144\ \text{K}$. The work comes from the endpoints,
>>$$W = \tfrac{3}{2}\,(P_1 V_1 - P_2 V_2) = \tfrac{3}{2}\left[ (5.00)(1.00) - (0.801)(3.00) \right] \text{L}\cdot\text{atm} = 3.89\ \text{L}\cdot\text{atm} = 395\ \text{J}.$$
>>The gas did $395\ \text{J}$ of work and paid for it with $395\ \text{J}$ of internal energy, which is why it cooled by more than half. An isothermal expansion between the same volumes would have done $P_1 V_1 \ln 3 = 557\ \text{J}$ with the bath paying.

>[!example] (Hard) Locating the Endpoint of an Adiabat
>A diatomic gas at $P_1 = 2.00 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$, $V_1 = 1.00\ \text{L}$ is compressed adiabatically until its pressure is $8.00 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the final volume, the ratio of final to initial temperature, and the work done on the gas.
>>With $\gamma = 1.40$, the volume follows from $P_1 V_1^{\gamma} = P_2 V_2^{\gamma}$,
>>$$V_2 = V_1 \left( \frac{P_1}{P_2} \right)^{1/\gamma} = (1.00\ \text{L})(0.250)^{0.714} = 0.371\ \text{L}.$$
>>The temperature ratio is $T_2/T_1 = P_2 V_2 / (P_1 V_1) = (4.00)(0.371) = 1.49$. The work done by the gas is
>>$$W = \tfrac{5}{2}\,(P_1 V_1 - P_2 V_2) = \tfrac{5}{2}\left[ 200 - (8.00 \times 10^{5})(0.371 \times 10^{-3}) \right] = \tfrac{5}{2}\,(200 - 297) = -243\ \text{J},$$
>>so the surroundings did $243\ \text{J}$ of work on the gas, all of which became internal energy. Quadrupling the pressure adiabatically needed less than a threefold compression, because the rising temperature did part of the job.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover sudden processes. This example uses the property above.

>[!example] (Medium) A Load Dropped on a Piston
>A diatomic gas sits under a piston at $P_1 = 1.00\ \text{atm}$ and $V_1 = 2.00\ \text{L}$. Extra weights are dropped onto the piston at once, raising the outside pressure to $2.00\ \text{atm}$. The walls are insulated.
>
>>[!example]- Find the final volume and the ratio of final to initial temperature. Compare with a slow adiabatic compression to the same pressure.
>>The gas has $d = 5$. The sudden-compression formula gives
>>$$\frac{V_2}{V_1} = \frac{\tfrac52 (1.00) + 2.00}{\tfrac72 (2.00)} = \frac{4.50}{7.00} = 0.643,$$
>>so $V_2 = 1.29\ \text{L}$. The temperature ratio is $T_2/T_1 = P_2 V_2/(P_1 V_1) = (2.00)(0.643) = 1.29$. A slow adiabatic compression to the same pressure follows $PV^{1.4}$, with
>>$$\frac{V_2}{V_1} = \left(\frac{1.00}{2.00}\right)^{1/1.4} = 0.610, \qquad \frac{T_2}{T_1} = (2.00)(0.610) = 1.22.$$
>>The sudden push does more work on the gas for the same final pressure. The load keeps pushing at $2.00\ \text{atm}$ from the start, while a slow push starts at $1.00\ \text{atm}$. The extra work leaves the gas hotter and larger.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not treat paths $PV^x$ with other exponents. This example uses the differential form of the ideal gas law from [[2.2.3 Ideal Gas Law]].

>[!example] (Hard) A Path Set by a Heat Rule
>A diatomic gas changes state so that every small step has $dQ = \tfrac13\,dE_{\text{int}}$.
>
>>[!example]- Show that the path has the form $PV^x = \text{const}$ and find $x$. Does the gas absorb or release heat as it expands?
>>The first law in differential form is $dQ = dE_{\text{int}} + P\,dV$. With the heat rule,
>>$$\tfrac13\,dE_{\text{int}} = dE_{\text{int}} + P\,dV, \qquad P\,dV = -\tfrac23\,dE_{\text{int}} = -\tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac52 Nk\,dT = -\tfrac53 Nk\,dT.$$
>>Eliminate $dT$ with the differential form of the gas law, $Nk\,dT = P\,dV + V\,dP$,
>>$$P\,dV = -\tfrac53\,(P\,dV + V\,dP), \qquad \tfrac83\,P\,dV = -\tfrac53\,V\,dP.$$
>>Divide by $PV$ and integrate,
>>$$\frac{dP}{P} = -\frac85\,\frac{dV}{V}, \qquad \ln P = -\tfrac85 \ln V + \text{const}.$$
>>So $PV^{8/5}$ is constant, and $x = 1.6$. Since $x > 1$, the product $PV \propto V^{1 - x}$ falls as the gas expands. The temperature falls, so $dE_{\text{int}} < 0$ and $dQ = \tfrac13\,dE_{\text{int}} < 0$. The gas releases heat while it expands. The heat formula from the properties agrees, since $\tfrac52 - 1/0.6 = \tfrac56 = \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac52$.
