>[!note] Historical Volatility
>**Historical volatility** is the annualized standard deviation of a stock's returns, estimated from past prices. Take prices $S_0, S_1, \ldots, S_n$ observed at equally spaced times, such as daily closes. The return over period $i$ is the [[3.1a Simple Return|simple return]]
>$$R_i = \frac{S_i}{S_{i-1}} - 1, \qquad i = 1, \ldots, n.$$
>Here $i$ indexes equal sampling periods, which can be minutes, days, weeks, or any other fixed length. The $n$ returns have average $\bar R = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} R_i$ and sample variance $s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (R_i - \bar R)^2$. For daily returns the volatility is
>$$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{250}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(R_i - \bar R\big)^2} = s\sqrt{250}.$$
>The factor $250$ is the number of trading days in a year on US markets, called the annualization factor. Weekly returns use $52$, and monthly returns use $12$. The result is quoted per year because option models measure time in years.
>
>The method rests on a model. The returns $R_i$ are independent and identically distributed normal random variables. Then $\sigma$ is a constant, and the variance of the return over a period of $\Delta t$ years is $\sigma^2 \Delta t$. Volatility is the $\sigma$ that sets the up and down moves of a [[2.3.3c Binomial Tree|binomial tree]] and enters the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]].

# Intuition

An option's value depends on how far the stock price can move before expiry. Historical volatility turns a price history into one number for the size of those moves. Data come at different frequencies, so the number needs a common time unit. A daily standard deviation of $2\%$ and a weekly one of $4.5\%$ describe nearly the same stock. Annualized, they give $31.6\%$ and $32.4\%$.

Under the model, the variance of a return grows in proportion to its horizon. The standard deviation therefore grows with the square root of the horizon. The figure shows the standard deviation of the return over $t$ years for a stock with $\sigma = 30\%$.

# Properties

#### *(Reading the Number)* A volatility of $\sigma$ means a typical daily move of about $\sigma/\sqrt{250}$.

The conversion runs in both directions. A stock with $\sigma = 25\%$ has a daily standard deviation of $25\%/\sqrt{250} = 1.58\%$. Under the normal model, a return lies within one standard deviation of its mean with probability about $0.68$. So on about two days in three, the daily return of this stock lies within $1.58\%$ of its small daily mean. The same stock has a one-month standard deviation of $25\% \times \sqrt{1/12} = 7.22\%$.

#### *(Dividing by $n - 1$)* The factor $n - 1$ makes $s^2$ an unbiased estimator of the variance of one return.

The deviations $R_i - \bar R$ are measured from the sample mean. The sample mean minimizes the sum of squared deviations, so these deviations are smaller on average than deviations from the true mean. Dividing by $n - 1$ instead of $n$ corrects the shortfall exactly, which makes $s^2$ unbiased ([[1.1 Estimators]]). For a year of daily data the correction is tiny. With $n = 250$, it raises $\sigma$ by the factor $\sqrt{250/249} = 1.002$. The mean $\bar R$ also matters little for daily data, since a stock's average daily return is usually much smaller than its daily standard deviation.

#### *(Sampling Frequency)* Daily, weekly, and monthly data estimate the same $\sigma$ under the model, with different precision.

Under the model, the annualization factor removes the effect of the sampling interval. The frequencies differ in how many returns a fixed calendar window provides. One year gives about $250$ daily returns but only $52$ weekly ones and $12$ monthly ones. The variance of $s^2$ shrinks as $n$ grows, so daily data give the most precise estimate from a given window. A longer window also adds returns, but it reaches further into the past.

#### *(Constant Volatility Is an Assumption)* Real return series have calm periods and turbulent periods, so the estimate depends on the window.

Plots of daily returns for Microsoft and Intel since 1996 show clusters of large moves. The clusters appear around the dot-com crash of 2000 to 2002, the financial crisis of 2008, and the COVID-19 crash of 2020. Long calmer stretches lie between them. A window inside a crisis and a window inside a calm year can give very different values of $\sigma$. The model keeps one constant $\sigma$ because that makes trees and the Black-Scholes-Merton formula workable. The (Hard) example shows how large the gap between windows can be.

#### *(Log Returns)* Many analysts compute volatility from log returns $\ln(S_i/S_{i-1})$ instead of simple returns.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules compute volatility from simple returns only. Log returns are a common alternative in practice.

Log returns add across periods: $\ln(S_n/S_0) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln(S_i/S_{i-1})$. So the variance of a multi-period log return is exactly the sum of the one-period variances when the periods are independent. For simple returns this holds only approximately. For small moves $\ln(1 + R) \approx R - R^2/2$, so both kinds of return give nearly the same $\sigma$ on daily data. The prices in the (Easy) example give $28.92\%$ with simple returns and $28.82\%$ with log returns. Normal log returns make the price [[2.2c.11 Lognormal Distribution|lognormal]], which is the price model behind the Black-Scholes-Merton formula.

#### *(Input to Option Models)* In a binomial tree, $\sigma$ sets the size of each up and down move.

The Cox-Ross-Rubinstein tree multiplies the price by $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ or $d = e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ at each step of length $\Delta t$ ([[2.3.3c Binomial Tree]]). With $\sigma = 30\%$ and monthly steps, $u = 1.0905$ and $d = 0.9170$. A larger $\sigma$ spreads the possible prices at expiry further apart. That raises the value of calls and puts, because an option holder gains from large moves in one direction and cannot lose more than the premium in the other.

# Derivation

We want to show that $\sigma = s\sqrt{250}$ estimates the annual volatility from daily returns.

Let $\Delta t = 1/250$ be the length of one trading day in years. Under the model, each daily return $R_i$ has the same variance, and the returns are independent. A year contains $250$ daily returns. For small daily moves, the annual return is close to the sum of the daily returns. Independence makes the variance of a sum equal to the sum of the variances ([[2.5b Second-Order Moments]]). So the annual variance is $250$ times the daily variance:
$$\sigma^2 = 250 \operatorname{Var}(R_i), \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad \operatorname{Var}(R_i) = \sigma^2 \Delta t.$$
The left form defines $\sigma^2$ as the variance per year. The right form is the same statement for a period of length $\Delta t$.

The sample variance $s^2$ estimates $\operatorname{Var}(R_i)$ without bias. Replacing $\operatorname{Var}(R_i)$ by $s^2$ and taking the square root gives
$$\sigma = \sqrt{250\, s^2} = \sqrt{\frac{250}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(R_i - \bar R\big)^2}.$$
This is the formula in the callout. The same steps with weekly returns replace $250$ by $52$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Five Days of Prices
>A stock closes at $\$42.00$, $\$42.84$, $\$42.15$, $\$43.02$, $\$42.60$, and $\$43.45$ on six consecutive trading days.
>
>>[!example]- Estimate the annualized volatility of the stock from these prices.
>>Six prices give five daily returns:
>>$$R_1 = \frac{42.84}{42.00} - 1 = 2.00\%, \quad R_2 = -1.61\%, \quad R_3 = 2.06\%, \quad R_4 = -0.98\%, \quad R_5 = 2.00\%.$$
>>Their average is $\bar R = 0.69\%$. The squared deviations from $\bar R$ sum to $0.0013377$. Divide by $n - 1 = 4$ to get the sample variance $s^2 = 0.00033443$, so the daily standard deviation is $s = 1.829\%$. Multiply by $\sqrt{250} = 15.811$ to annualize:
>>$$\sigma = 1.829\% \times 15.811 = 28.92\%.$$
>>Five returns are far too few for a reliable estimate. The example shows the steps, and a usable estimate needs many more returns.

>[!example] (Medium) Weekly Data and Other Horizons
>A stock closes at $\$120.00$, $\$122.90$, $\$119.70$, $\$124.10$, $\$121.60$, $\$125.00$, $\$121.80$, and $\$126.20$ on eight consecutive Fridays.
>
>>[!example]- Estimate the annualized volatility, convert it to a one-day and a one-month standard deviation, and give the price range that covers about two thirds of the outcomes one month from the last close.
>>The seven weekly returns are $2.42\%$, $-2.60\%$, $3.68\%$, $-2.01\%$, $2.80\%$, $-2.56\%$, and $3.61\%$. Their average is $0.76\%$. The squared deviations sum to $0.0053564$, so $s^2 = 0.0053564/6 = 0.00089273$ and $s = 2.988\%$ per week. Weekly data use the factor $52$:
>>$$\sigma = 2.988\% \times \sqrt{52} = 21.55\%.$$
>>The one-day standard deviation is $21.55\%/\sqrt{250} = 1.36\%$. The one-month standard deviation is $21.55\% \times \sqrt{1/12} = 6.22\%$.
>>
>>Under the normal model, a return falls within one standard deviation of its mean with probability about $0.68$. Ignoring the small mean, the one-month return lies between $-6.22\%$ and $+6.22\%$ with that probability. From $\$126.20$, the price range is $\$118.35$ to $\$134.05$.

>[!example] (Hard) Calm Window, Turbulent Window
>An analyst has two windows of ten daily returns for the same stock. In a calm month the stock closes at $\$80.00$, $\$80.40$, $\$79.90$, $\$80.30$, $\$80.80$, $\$80.50$, $\$80.90$, $\$80.60$, $\$81.10$, $\$80.70$, and $\$81.00$. In a turbulent month that follows, it closes at $\$81.00$, $\$78.60$, $\$80.90$, $\$77.20$, $\$79.70$, $\$76.10$, $\$78.80$, $\$75.30$, $\$77.90$, $\$74.60$, and $\$76.40$.
>
>>[!example]- Estimate $\sigma$ from each window and from all twenty returns, explain how the pooled estimate relates to the other two, and say what the gap means for the constant-volatility model.
>>In the calm window the daily returns stay within $0.63\%$ of zero. The sample standard deviation is $0.5171\%$ per day, so $\sigma = 0.5171\% \times \sqrt{250} = 8.18\%$. In the turbulent window the daily moves reach $4.57\%$. The sample standard deviation is $3.8658\%$ per day, so $\sigma = 61.12\%$. Pooling all twenty returns gives $s = 2.7044\%$ per day and $\sigma = 42.76\%$.
>>
>>The pooled $\sigma$ is well above the average of the two volatilities, which is $34.65\%$. Variances average across windows, and standard deviations do not. The average of the two annual variances is $(0.0818^2 + 0.6112^2)/2 = 0.1901$, and its square root is $43.61\%$. The pooled estimate is close to that value. It differs a little because the two windows have different means and because of the $n - 1$ divisors.
>>
>>The constant-volatility model says one $\sigma$ describes both months. These data give values that differ by a factor of about $7.5$. A one-month option priced with $8.18\%$ would assume a one-month standard deviation of $2.36\%$. With $61.12\%$ it would assume $17.64\%$. The model gives no rule for choosing the window. That choice is a judgment the analyst makes outside the model.
