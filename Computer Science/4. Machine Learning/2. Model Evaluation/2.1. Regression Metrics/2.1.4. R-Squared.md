>[!note] R-Squared
>The **coefficient of determination** $R^2$ is the fraction of the variation in $y$ that a model's predictions account for. It uses three sums of squares over $n$ points:
>$$\mathrm{SST} = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar y)^2, \qquad \mathrm{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat y_i)^2, \qquad \mathrm{SSR} = \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2 .$$
>Here $\bar y$ is the mean of the $y_i$. SST is the total sum of squares and SSE the error sum of squares. SSR is the regression sum of squares. The definition is
>$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{\mathrm{SST}} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{MSE}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2}.$$
>It compares the model's MSE with the MSE of always predicting $\bar y$. The **adjusted** $R^2$ divides each sum of squares by its degrees of freedom. With $p$ predictors plus an intercept,
>$$R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{SSE}/(n - p - 1)}{\mathrm{SST}/(n - 1)} = 1 - (1 - R^2)\,\frac{n - 1}{n - p - 1}.$$
>It falls when a new predictor reduces SSE too little to make up for the lost degree of freedom. The headline facts are these:
>
>- **Decomposition**: for least squares with an intercept, on its training data, $\mathrm{SST} = \mathrm{SSR} + \mathrm{SSE}$. Then $R^2 = \mathrm{SSR}/\mathrm{SST}$ lies in $[0, 1]$.
>- **Correlation**: in simple regression, $R^2 = r_{xy}^2$, the squared sample correlation of $x$ and $y$. With several predictors and an intercept, $R^2$ is the squared correlation between $y$ and $\hat y$.
>- **Never decreasing**: adding a predictor to a least squares fit never lowers the training $R^2$, which motivates the adjusted version.
>- **Negative values**: on test data, or for a fit without an intercept, SSE can exceed SST and make $R^2 < 0$. The model then predicts worse than the constant $\bar y$.
>- **No units**: $R^2$ is a ratio, so it compares the quality of fit across targets on different scales.
>
>$R^2$ is the scale-free companion of [[2.1.1. Mean Squared Error|MSE]]. On one dataset it ranks models in the same order as MSE, and it adds a comparison across targets.

>[!warning] Names of the Sums of Squares
>Texts disagree on the abbreviations. This vault writes SSE for the error sum of squares and SSR for the regression sum of squares. Many texts write RSS for the residual sum of squares, and some use SSR for it. STAT 153 writes $Q$ for SSE. Some texts also let $p$ count every coefficient, including the intercept, and then write $n - p$ in place of $n - p - 1$.

# Intuition

The baseline for any regression model is the constant prediction $\bar y$, which ignores the inputs. Its squared error is SST. A model that uses the inputs lowers the squared error to SSE. $R^2$ reports the fraction of SST that the model removed. An $R^2$ of $0.8$ means the model's squared error is $20\%$ of the baseline's. The name comes from simple regression, where $R^2$ equals the square of the correlation coefficient $r$.

For least squares the removed part has a geometric meaning. After centering, the fitted vector $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ and the residual vector are orthogonal. Their squared lengths therefore add like the sides of a right triangle. SST is the squared hypotenuse, and SSR and SSE are the squared legs. $R^2$ is the squared cosine of the angle between the centered $\mathbf{y}$ and the centered $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$. The triangle gives the bound $R^2 \le 1$ and the correlation formula at once.

The animation compares the spread around the mean (SST) with the spread around the fit (SSE). On new test data, a poor model misses more than the mean does, so its $R^2$ is negative.

# Properties

#### *(Training $R^2$ Grows With Size)* Pure-noise predictors raise the training $R^2$ by about $p/(n - 1)$ on average.

Suppose $y$ is unrelated to $p$ predictors and the noise is Gaussian. The training $R^2$ then follows a $\mathrm{Beta}\big(p/2, (n - p - 1)/2\big)$ distribution with mean $p/(n - 1)$. With $n = 21$ houses and $p = 10$ random predictors, the expected $R^2$ is $0.5$, although the predictors carry no information. A least squares fit with $p = n - 1$ predictors interpolates the data and reaches $R^2 = 1$. In the same setting the adjusted $R^2$ has expected value $0$. An analyst therefore cannot pick the number of predictors from the training $R^2$ alone. Adjusted $R^2$, [[2.3.2. Cross-Validation|cross-validation]] or a test set should guide that choice.

#### *(Reading Adjusted $R^2$)* Adjusted $R^2$ rises when a new predictor has $|t| > 1$ and falls otherwise.

Here $t$ is the new predictor's $t$-statistic in the larger least squares fit. A predictor with $|t|$ between $1$ and $2$ raises $R^2_{\text{adj}}$. A test at the $5\%$ level would not call that predictor significant. Adjusted $R^2$ is therefore a lenient filter for predictors. It is negative when $R^2 < p/(n - 1)$, that is, when the predictors explain less than pure noise would on average. In the STAT 153 finger-tapping regression, $n = 65$ observations and $p = 3$ predictors gave $R^2 = 0.326$. The adjusted value was $1 - 0.674 \cdot 64/61 = 0.293$.

#### *(Negative Out of Sample)* On new data $R^2$ has no lower bound, and a negative value means the model loses to a constant.

On a test set, $R^2 = 1 - \mathrm{SSE}_{\text{test}}/\mathrm{SST}_{\text{test}}$, with $\bar y$ taken over the test set. This is the convention of scikit-learn's `r2_score`. Nothing ties the model's errors to SST on new data, so $R^2$ can fall below $0$. A model that predicts a constant $c$, such as the training mean, gets
$$R^2 = -\frac{n(\bar y_{\text{test}} - c)^2}{\mathrm{SST}_{\text{test}}} \le 0.$$
Overfit models and models applied after a distribution shift often give negative test $R^2$. Return forecasting in finance computes out-of-sample $R^2$ against the training mean instead (Campbell and Thompson, 2008). A report should state which mean defines the baseline.

#### *(Without an Intercept)* A fit through the origin breaks the decomposition, and software then reports a different $R^2$.

Without an intercept the residuals need not sum to zero, and $\mathrm{SST} = \mathrm{SSR} + \mathrm{SSE}$ fails. The ordinary $R^2$ can then be negative even on the training data. Statsmodels switches to the **uncentered** $R^2 = 1 - \mathrm{SSE}/\sum_i y_i^2$, which compares the model with predicting $0$. In STAT 153 lecture 5, a line through the origin for monthly chicken prices reported an uncentered $R^2$ of $0.885$. Adding the intercept gave an ordinary $R^2$ of $0.917$. The two numbers use different baselines and cannot be compared. When $y$ is far from zero, the uncentered version is close to $1$ for almost any model.

#### *(What $R^2$ Does Not Measure)* A high $R^2$ does not certify the model, and a low $R^2$ does not make it useless.

Anscombe (1973) built four datasets with the same fitted line and $R^2 \approx 0.67$. One is linear with noise, one is a clean curve, and two are driven by single outliers. Only a plot of the data or the residuals separates them. In the other direction, a slope can be precise and important while $R^2$ is small, because the noise is large. $R^2$ also depends on the spread of the inputs. For $y = \beta_1 x + \varepsilon$ with noise variance $\sigma^2$, the population value is
$$R^2 = \frac{\beta_1^2\,\mathrm{Var}(x)}{\beta_1^2\,\mathrm{Var}(x) + \sigma^2}.$$
With $\beta_1 = 2$ and $\sigma = 4$, it is $0.8$ when $\mathrm{Var}(x) = 16$ and $0.2$ when $\mathrm{Var}(x) = 1$. The slope and the noise are the same in both cases. Two unrelated trending time series can also give a high $R^2$, the spurious regression described in [[3.1a. Multiple Linear Regression]].

# Derivation

We show that $\mathrm{SST} = \mathrm{SSR} + \mathrm{SSE}$ for least squares with an intercept. Write $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}$ and $\mathbf{1}$ for the vector of ones. The normal equations $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}$ say that $\mathbf{e}$ is orthogonal to every column of $\mathbf{X}$ ([[10. Projection Theorem]]). The intercept column is $\mathbf{1}$, so $\sum_i e_i = 0$. Since $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ is a combination of the columns, $\mathbf{e}^T\hat{\mathbf{y}} = 0$ as well. Split each deviation as $y_i - \bar y = (\hat y_i - \bar y) + e_i$ and square:
$$\mathrm{SST} = \sum_i (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2 + 2\sum_i (\hat y_i - \bar y)\,e_i + \sum_i e_i^2 .$$
The cross term equals $2(\mathbf{e}^T\hat{\mathbf{y}} - \bar y\,\mathbf{e}^T\mathbf{1}) = 0$, which leaves $\mathrm{SST} = \mathrm{SSR} + \mathrm{SSE}$. The fact $\sum_i e_i = 0$ used the intercept column, and the identity fails without it.

For simple regression, write $S_{xx} = \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2$, $S_{xy} = \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)$ and $S_{yy} = \mathrm{SST}$. The fitted values satisfy $\hat y_i - \bar y = \hat\beta_1(x_i - \bar x)$ with $\hat\beta_1 = S_{xy}/S_{xx}$. Then
$$R^2 = \frac{\mathrm{SSR}}{\mathrm{SST}} = \frac{\hat\beta_1^2 S_{xx}}{S_{yy}} = \frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}S_{yy}} = r_{xy}^2 .$$
The last expression is the square of the sample [[2.5b Second-Order Moments|correlation coefficient]].

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Price Against Floor Area
>Five houses have floor areas $x = [10, 14, 18, 22, 26]$ hundred square feet and prices $y = [300, 360, 420, 500, 520]$ thousand dollars. The least squares line is $\hat y = 159 + 14.5x$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute SST, SSE, SSR and $R^2$. Check the decomposition and the correlation formula.
>>The mean price is $\bar y = 420$, and the deviations are $[-120, -60, 0, 80, 100]$, so $\mathrm{SST} = 34{,}400$. The fitted values are $[304, 362, 420, 478, 536]$, and the residuals are $[-4, -2, 0, 22, -16]$. Hence $\mathrm{SSE} = 16 + 4 + 0 + 484 + 256 = 760$ and $\mathrm{SSR} = 2(116^2 + 58^2) = 33{,}640$. The sum $33{,}640 + 760$ equals SST. The fit gives $R^2 = 1 - 760/34{,}400 = 0.978$. The correlation is $r = S_{xy}/\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}} = 2320/\sqrt{160 \cdot 34{,}400} = 0.989$, and $r^2 = 0.978$. The RMSE is $\sqrt{760/5} = 12.3$, so a typical prediction still misses by about $12$ thousand dollars.

>[!example] (Medium) Choosing Between Two Price Models
>An analyst fits two models to $n = 30$ apartment rents. Model A has $3$ predictors and $R^2 = 0.70$. Model B adds five more predictors and reaches $R^2 = 0.74$.
>
>>[!example]- Which model has the higher adjusted $R^2$? What $R^2$ would model B need to tie?
>>Model A gives $R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - 0.30 \cdot 29/26 = 0.665$. Model B gives $1 - 0.26 \cdot 29/21 = 0.641$. Model A wins, because five predictors cost five degrees of freedom and bought only $0.04$ of $R^2$. To tie, model B needs $1 - R^2 = 0.335 \cdot 21/29 = 0.242$, that is, $R^2 = 0.758$. A cross-validated error would settle the choice more reliably.

>[!example] (Hard) A Model Moved to a New City
>A delivery-time model trained in one city is tested in another. The five test deliveries took $y = [30, 34, 28, 40, 38]$ minutes, and the model predicted $\hat y = [36, 33, 35, 33, 41]$. The training mean was $30$ minutes.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the test $R^2$ of the model and of the constant training mean. Does the squared correlation between $y$ and $\hat y$ agree?
>>The test mean is $34$, so $\mathrm{SST} = 16 + 0 + 36 + 36 + 16 = 104$. The residuals are $[-6, 1, -7, 7, -3]$, so $\mathrm{SSE} = 36 + 1 + 49 + 49 + 9 = 144$. The test $R^2$ is $1 - 144/104 = -0.385$. The model's test MSE is $28.8$, against $20.8$ for predicting the test mean. The constant $30$ gives $\mathrm{SSE} = 104 + 5 \cdot 4^2 = 184$ and $R^2 = -0.769$. The squared correlation between $y$ and $\hat y$ is $0.014$, so it does not match the negative $R^2$. The identity $R^2 = \mathrm{corr}(y, \hat y)^2$ holds only for a least squares fit on its own training data.
