>[!note] Isovolumetric Process
>An **isovolumetric process** (also called isochoric) changes the state of a gas while its volume stays fixed. On a $P$-$V$ diagram it is a vertical line. With $V$ constant, the [[2.2.3 Ideal Gas Law|ideal gas law]] gives $P \propto T$, so pressure and temperature rise and fall together. The gas moves no boundary, so it does no [[2.5.1 Thermodynamic Work|work]],
>$$W = 0.$$
>The [[2.5.2 First Law of Thermodynamics|first law]] then puts every joule of heat into internal energy,
>$$Q = \Delta E_{\text{int}} = \tfrac{d}{2}\, Nk\, \Delta T = \tfrac{d}{2}\, V\, \Delta P,$$
>where $d$ is the number of accessible [[2.3.4 Equipartition of Energy|degrees of freedom]]. Written per mole, $Q = n C_V\, \Delta T$ with the [[2.5.4 Molar Specific Heats|molar specific heat at constant volume]] $C_V = \tfrac{d}{2} R$. Every $\Delta$ is final minus initial, so heating gives $Q > 0$ and cooling gives $Q < 0$. An isovolumetric process is realized by sealing the gas in a rigid container.

# Intuition

Seal a gas in a steel tank and warm it. The molecules speed up and hit the walls harder and more often, so the pressure climbs, but the walls do not move. No work is done, and all the heat stays inside as kinetic energy of the molecules. This is the one process where the heat added can be read off from the temperature change alone. The container fixes the path, so nothing more about it needs to be known. The sketch shows the setup.

# Properties

#### *(No Work, Ever)* A vertical path has no area under it, so $W = 0$ in both directions.

The result does not depend on the gas being ideal. Any substance held at fixed volume does no pressure work, because work needs a moving boundary. For an ideal gas the further statement $Q = \tfrac{d}{2} Nk\, \Delta T$ needs the [[2.4.1 Internal Energy|internal energy formula]]. For a solid or liquid at fixed volume the corresponding statement is $Q = mc\,\Delta T$, and since solids and liquids barely change volume when heated, their ordinary [[2.4.3 Specific Heat|specific heat]] is close to a constant-volume value.

#### *(Pressure Tracks Temperature)* With $V$ fixed, $P_2 / P_1 = T_2 / T_1$ in kelvins.

The ratio form needs absolute temperatures. A sealed container warmed from $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to $40\ ^\circ\text{C}$ has its pressure rise by the factor $313/293 = 1.07$, not by a factor of two. Read the change in internal energy from the diagram as $\tfrac{d}{2} V \Delta P$, or from the thermometer as $\tfrac{d}{2} Nk\, \Delta T$. The two agree because $V \Delta P = Nk\, \Delta T$ at fixed volume.

#### *(The Cheapest Way to Warm a Gas)* Heating at constant volume needs less heat per kelvin than any process in which the gas expands.

Every joule goes into temperature, and none leaks out as work. The [[2.5.3b Isobaric Process|isobaric]] process needs $\tfrac{d}{2} + 1$ shares of heat per kelvin instead of $\tfrac{d}{2}$, and the extra share is the work of pushing the piston. This is why $C_V$ is the smallest of the molar specific heats, and why the difference $C_P - C_V$ is exactly $R$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Helium in a Rigid Flask
>A sealed $2.00\ \text{L}$ flask holds helium at $1.00\ \text{atm}$ and $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$. It is warmed to $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the final pressure and the heat added.
>>The pressure scales with absolute temperature,
>>$$P_2 = P_1 \frac{T_2}{T_1} = (1.00\ \text{atm})\frac{373.15}{293.15} = 1.27\ \text{atm}.$$
>>Helium is monatomic, $d = 3$, and no work is done, so
>>$$Q = \tfrac{3}{2}\, V \Delta P = \tfrac{3}{2}\,(2.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3)(0.273 \times 1.013 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}) = 83\ \text{J}.$$
>>As a check, $Nk = P_1 V_1 / T_1 = 0.691\ \text{J/K}$ and $\tfrac{3}{2}(0.691)(80) = 83\ \text{J}$.

>[!example] (Medium) Cooling a Tire
>A rigid tire holds air at a gauge pressure of $2.20\ \text{atm}$ on a $30\ ^\circ\text{C}$ afternoon. Overnight the temperature falls to $5\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Treat the volume as fixed.
>
>>[!example]- Find the gauge pressure in the morning, and the sign of $Q$.
>>Gauge pressure is pressure above atmospheric, so the absolute pressure starts at $3.20\ \text{atm}$. At fixed volume,
>>$$P_2 = (3.20\ \text{atm})\frac{278.15}{303.15} = 2.94\ \text{atm},$$
>>a gauge reading of $1.94\ \text{atm}$. The air lost internal energy as it cooled, so $Q = \tfrac{5}{2} V \Delta P$ is negative. Heat flowed out of the tire, and none of the loss was work, because the tire did not shrink.
