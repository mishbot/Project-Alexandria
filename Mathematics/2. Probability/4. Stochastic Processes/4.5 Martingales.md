>[!note] Martingale
>A **martingale** is a process whose best forecast of any future value, given everything seen so far, is its current value. In discrete time, a sequence $X_0, X_1, X_2, \dots$ is a martingale when, for every $n$,
>$$\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1} \mid X_0, \dots, X_n] = X_n.$$
>The left side is the conditional [[2.5a Expectation|expectation]] of the next value given the path up to step $n$. The same forecast then holds for every later step, $\mathbb{E}[X_m \mid X_0, \dots, X_n] = X_n$ for all $m > n$. In continuous time, a process $X_t$ is a martingale when
>$$\mathbb{E}[X_t \mid \text{history up to } s] = X_s \qquad \text{for all } s < t.$$
>The history up to $s$ is everything observed until time $s$. It may include other processes, such as the Brownian motion $W_t$ that drives $X_t$. Two technical conditions come with the definition. The value $X_t$ must be known from the history up to $t$, and its average size must be finite, $\mathbb{E}[|X_t|] < \infty$, so that every average above exists.
>
>A **submartingale** satisfies $\mathbb{E}[X_t \mid \text{history up to } s] \ge X_s$, so on average it rises. A **supermartingale** satisfies the same with $\le$, so on average it falls. A martingale is the standard model of a fair game. A player's wealth in a game with a house edge is a supermartingale, and the casino's wealth is a submartingale. The basic martingales are a random walk with mean-zero steps, a gambler's wealth in a fair game, $W_t$, $W_t^2 - t$, and $e^{\sigma W_t - \sigma^2 t/2}$ for a constant $\sigma$.
>
>In finance, let $S_t$ be the price of a stock that pays no dividends, $f_t$ the price of a derivative on it, and $r$ the continuously compounded risk-free rate. Under the risk-neutral probabilities of [[2.3.3b Risk-Neutral Valuation]], the discounted prices $e^{-rt}S_t$ and $e^{-rt}f_t$ are both martingales. Write $\hat{\mathbb{E}}$ for an expectation under those probabilities. The pricing rule $f_0 = e^{-rT}\hat{\mathbb{E}}[f_T]$ for a payoff at time $T$ is the martingale property between today and $T$. A mean-zero [[4.4 Random Walks|random walk]] is the simplest martingale in discrete time, and [[4.6 Brownian Motion|Brownian motion]] is the simplest in continuous time.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>This whole note goes beyond IEOR 221, which never uses the word martingale. The modules use the idea without the name. Under risk-neutral probabilities, the expected discounted price of the stock or of a derivative equals its value today.

# Intuition

A trader watching a stock wants to know whether the price path so far says anything about the direction of the next move. A gambler at a casino table asks a similar question about her wealth. She wants a rule for sizing her bets, based on past wins and losses, that gives her a positive expected gain. Both questions concern the average of the next value, taken over all the ways the future can unfold from where things stand now.

That average is the conditional expectation. Fix the path observed so far. Consider all future paths that agree with it up to now. Average the next value over those paths, with each path weighted by its [[1.3 Conditional Probability|conditional probability]]. The result depends on the observed path. [[2.5a Expectation]] gives the formula for one variable given another.

Take a gambler who starts with $\$10$ and bets on two tosses of a fair coin. She stakes $\$1$ on the first toss. After a win she stakes $\$1$ again, and after a loss she stakes $\$2$. Each branch in the figure has probability $1/2$.

Each circle shows her wealth $X_n$ after $n$ tosses. The blue label next to each of the first three circles is the average of the two wealths one toss later. At every node that average equals the current wealth. This holds at $\$9$ too, where she doubled her stake. A bet on a fair coin has mean gain zero whatever its size, so no rule for choosing stakes from the past changes the forecast. Over both tosses her final wealth averages $(12 + 10 + 11 + 7)/4 = \$10$, her starting wealth.

The name comes from a betting system. The gambler doubles her stake after each loss and stops at the first win. That win recovers every earlier loss plus her first stake, so she ends $\$1$ ahead. The system would guarantee a profit only with unlimited wealth and unlimited time. With limits, a long run of losses uses up all her money, and that rare large loss cancels the frequent small gains on average. The word martingale now means any process that behaves like the wealth of a fair game, where no betting rule changes the expected outcome.

The average over agreeing paths is the left side of the discrete form in the callout, $\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1} \mid X_0, \dots, X_n]$. The fair coin makes it equal to the current wealth $X_n$ on the right. A coin that favored her would push the average above $X_n$, which is a submartingale. A house edge would push it below, which is a supermartingale. Many small tosses in a short time lead to Brownian motion and the continuous form.

# Properties

#### *(Constant Expected Value)* The unconditional mean of a martingale never changes, so $\mathbb{E}[X_t] = X_0$ for every $t$.

Take the expectation of both sides of $\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1} \mid X_0, \dots, X_n] = X_n$. By the law of iterated expectation in [[2.5a Expectation]], the average of a conditional average is the plain average. So $\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1}] = \mathbb{E}[X_n]$, and repeating from step $0$ gives $\mathbb{E}[X_n] = X_0$ when the start is known. In continuous time the argument is identical for any two times. The law of iterated expectation also gives the forecast two steps ahead. At step $n$, the forecast of $X_{n+2}$ equals the forecast of its step-$(n+1)$ forecast. That inner forecast is $X_{n+1}$, and the forecast of $X_{n+1}$ is $X_n$. The converse fails. Let $X_0 = 0$, let $X_1$ be $+1$ or $-1$ with equal probability, and let $X_2 = 2X_1$. Every mean is $0$, but $\mathbb{E}[X_2 \mid X_1] = 2X_1 \ne X_1$. So a changing mean proves that a process is not a martingale, and a constant mean proves nothing.

#### *(Uncorrelated Steps)* The steps of a martingale have mean zero and are uncorrelated, but they need not be independent.

Write the steps, called martingale differences, as $D_n = X_n - X_{n-1}$. The definition says $\mathbb{E}[D_{n+1} \mid X_0, \dots, X_n] = 0$, so each step has mean zero. An earlier step $D_m$ with $m \le n$ is known at step $n$. So $\mathbb{E}[D_m D_{n+1}] = \mathbb{E}\big[D_m\,\mathbb{E}[D_{n+1} \mid X_0, \dots, X_n]\big] = 0$, and the two steps are uncorrelated. As a result variances add, $\operatorname{Var}(X_n) = \sum_{k=1}^n \operatorname{Var}(D_k)$, as for a random walk. Independence can fail. In the coin example the second step is $\pm 1$ after a win and $\pm 2$ after a loss, so its size depends on the first step. Its variance is $2.5$, and $\operatorname{Var}(X_2) = 1 + 2.5 = 3.5$. A price can behave the same way. In a market where calm and volatile periods alternate, the direction of the next change can be unpredictable while its likely size depends on recent moves.

#### *(Doubling Fails With Limits)* With a cap on wealth, the doubling system wins a small amount often and loses a large amount rarely, and its expected gain is zero.

>[!warning] Optional Stopping Theorem
>This property and the (Medium) example use the optional stopping theorem without proof. Let $\tau$ be a stopping time, a rule for when to stop that uses only the past. The theorem says $\mathbb{E}[X_\tau] = X_0$ for a martingale if $\tau$ is bounded, or if $\tau$ is finite and the stopped process stays bounded, or if $\tau$ has a finite mean and the steps are bounded.

A gambler with $\$63$ who starts with a $\$1$ stake can afford six losses in a row, since $1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 16 + 32 = 63$. She ends $\$1$ ahead unless she loses all six tosses, which has probability $(1/2)^6 = 1/64$. Then she loses all $\$63$. Her expected gain is
$$\frac{63}{64} \times 1 - \frac{1}{64} \times 63 = 0.$$
The frequent $\$1$ gain and the rare $\$63$ loss cancel exactly. The same holds for a cap of $k$ tosses. She wins $\$1$ with probability $1 - 2^{-k}$ and loses $2^k - 1$ dollars with probability $2^{-k}$. She stops after at most six tosses, so the theorem applies and gives $\mathbb{E}[X_\tau] = X_0$. Without a cap she stops after two tosses on average and always wins $\$1$. That case breaks all three conditions. Her stopping time has no bound, and neither do her stakes or her losses before stopping. The expected loss she must carry right before her first win is $\sum_{k \ge 1}(2^{k-1} - 1)2^{-k}$, and this sum is infinite.

#### *(Martingales From Brownian Motion)* $W_t$, $W_t^2 - t$, and $e^{\sigma W_t - \sigma^2 t/2}$ are martingales, and each check uses only independent normal increments.

Fix $s < t$ and write $\mathbb{E}_s[\,\cdot\,]$ for the conditional expectation given the history up to $s$. Split $W_t = W_s + \Delta W$, where the increment $\Delta W = W_t - W_s$ is normal with mean $0$ and variance $t - s$, independent of that history. Given the history, $W_s$ is a known constant and $\Delta W$ keeps its usual distribution. The three forecasts are
$$\mathbb{E}_s[W_t] = W_s, \qquad \mathbb{E}_s[W_t^2] = W_s^2 + (t - s), \qquad \mathbb{E}_s[e^{\sigma W_t}] = e^{\sigma W_s}e^{\sigma^2(t - s)/2}.$$
The middle one uses $\mathbb{E}_s[2W_s\,\Delta W] = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[(\Delta W)^2] = t - s$. The last uses $\mathbb{E}[e^{\sigma\Delta W}] = e^{\sigma^2(t-s)/2}$, the moment generating function of a normal variable from [[2.2c.3 Gaussian Distribution]]. Subtracting $t$ from $W_t^2$, or multiplying $e^{\sigma W_t}$ by $e^{-\sigma^2 t/2}$, removes the growth in the forecast and leaves $W_s^2 - s$ and $e^{\sigma W_s - \sigma^2 s/2}$. So $W_t^2$ alone is a submartingale. [[4.6 Brownian Motion]] writes the same process as $B_t$. The third martingale is the discounted stock price of [[7.6 Geometric Brownian Motion]] under risk-neutral probabilities, $e^{-rt}S_t = S_0 e^{\sigma W_t - \sigma^2 t/2}$, with $W_t$ a Brownian motion under those probabilities. [[7.4 Ito Integral|Itô integrals]] $\int_0^t f\,dW_s$ are martingales too, which is why they have mean zero.

#### *(Removing a Drift)* A process with a nonzero drift is not a martingale, and subtracting the accumulated drift turns it into one.

A [[4.4 Random Walks|random walk]] with drift $\delta$ has steps $X_{n+1} - X_n = \delta + w_{n+1}$, where the shocks $w_n$ are independent with mean zero. Its forecast is $\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1} \mid X_0, \dots, X_n] = X_n + \delta$. So it is a submartingale when $\delta > 0$ and a supermartingale when $\delta < 0$, and $X_n - n\delta$ is a martingale. In continuous time, $x_t = x_0 + at + bW_t$ from [[7.1 Generalized Wiener Process]] gives the martingale $x_t - at$. The $-t$ in $W_t^2 - t$ removes the drift of $W_t^2$, which rises on average at rate $1$. Any process splits this way. Subtract from $X_n$ the sum of its one-step forecast changes, $\sum_{k=1}^n \mathbb{E}[X_k - X_{k-1} \mid X_0, \dots, X_{k-1}]$, and a martingale remains. This split is called the Doob decomposition. For a stock with expected return rate $\mu$, discounting at $\mu$ removes the drift under real-world probabilities. Discounting at $r$ removes it only under probabilities that make the stock grow at $r$.

#### *(Discounted Prices Under $p$)* In a binomial step, the discounted stock price is a martingale under the risk-neutral $p$ and not under the real-world $p^*$.

Take a stock at $\$60$ that moves in one year to $\$72$ or $\$54$, so $u = 1.2$ and $d = 0.9$. Let $r = 4\%$, and let the real expected return rate be $\mu = 10\%$, both with continuous compounding. The risk-neutral probability is $p = (e^{0.04} - 0.9)/0.3 = 0.4694$, and the real-world probability is $p^* = (e^{0.10} - 0.9)/0.3 = 0.6839$. Under $p$, the discounted expected price after one year is
$$e^{-0.04}\big(0.4694 \times 72 + 0.5306 \times 54\big) = e^{-0.04} \times 62.45 = \$60.00.$$
This is today's price, so the martingale property holds. Under $p^*$ the same calculation gives $e^{-0.04} \times 66.31 = \$63.71$, which equals $60e^{\mu - r}$. The discounted price drifts up at rate $\mu - r$, so it is a submartingale under $p^*$. The one-step rule $f = e^{-r\Delta t}[p f_u + (1 - p) f_d]$ says the same about the discounted derivative price. So backward induction in a [[2.3.3c Binomial Tree|binomial tree]] applies the martingale property one step at a time ([[2.3.3b Risk-Neutral Valuation]]).

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Checking a Two-Round Game
>A player starts with $\$20$ and plays two rounds. In the first round she wins $\$6$ with probability $1/3$ and loses $\$3$ with probability $2/3$. If she won, the second round is a coin toss for $\$6$, which she wins or loses with probability $1/2$ each. If she lost, the second round pays $\$6$ with probability $1/4$ and costs $\$3$ with probability $3/4$.
>
>>[!example]- Decide whether her wealth is a martingale, a submartingale, or a supermartingale, and find the win probability in the second round after a loss that would make it a martingale.
>>Check the forecast at each node. At the start, her wealth after one round is $\$26$ or $\$17$, and its average is
>>$$\frac{1}{3} \times 26 + \frac{2}{3} \times 17 = 20.$$
>>The forecast equals her current wealth of $\$20$. After a win, her wealth becomes $\$32$ or $\$20$, with average $\$26$, again her current wealth. After a loss, her wealth becomes $\$23$ or $\$14$, with average $\frac{1}{4} \times 23 + \frac{3}{4} \times 14 = \$16.25$. That is below her current $\$17$. The forecast never exceeds her wealth and falls short once, so the process is a supermartingale and not a martingale. Her expected final wealth is $\frac{1}{3} \times 26 + \frac{2}{3} \times 16.25 = \$19.50$, below her start.
>>
>>For a martingale, the second round after a loss must have mean gain zero. With win probability $q$, this requires $6q - 3(1 - q) = 0$, so $q = 1/3$. Then the average is $\frac{1}{3} \times 23 + \frac{2}{3} \times 14 = \$17$, her current wealth.

>[!warning] Optional Stopping Theorem
>The next example uses the optional stopping theorem stated under the Doubling Fails With Limits property, without proof. Its third condition applies: the stopping time has a finite mean, and the steps are bounded before it.

>[!example] (Medium) Waiting for an Exit
>A trader buys a stock and sets two exit orders. She sells if the price rises $5$ ticks above her entry or falls $5$ ticks below it. Each price change is one tick up or one tick down, with probability $1/2$ each, independent of the past. Let $S_n$ be the price minus her entry, in ticks, after $n$ changes, so $S_0 = 0$.
>
>>[!example]- Show that $S_n^2 - n$ is a martingale, use it to find the expected number of price changes before she sells, and find that number when both exits move to $10$ ticks.
>>Write $S_{n+1} = S_n + \xi$, where $\xi = \pm 1$ with probability $1/2$ each, independent of the past. Then $\mathbb{E}[\xi] = 0$ and $\xi^2 = 1$, so
>>$$\mathbb{E}[S_{n+1}^2 \mid S_0, \dots, S_n] = S_n^2 + 2S_n\,\mathbb{E}[\xi] + \mathbb{E}[\xi^2] = S_n^2 + 1.$$
>>The forecast of the square rises by $1$ per step. Subtracting $n + 1$ gives $\mathbb{E}[S_{n+1}^2 - (n + 1) \mid S_0, \dots, S_n] = S_n^2 - n$, so $M_n = S_n^2 - n$ is a martingale.
>>
>>Let $\tau$ be the first $n$ with $|S_n| = 5$. The step of $M$ from $n$ to $n + 1$ equals $2S_n\xi$, and before $\tau$ it is at most $8$ in size. The time $\tau$ has a finite mean. From any price inside the band, $9$ up moves in a row reach an exit, so each block of $9$ changes ends the trade with probability at least $2^{-9}$. The optional stopping theorem then gives $\mathbb{E}[M_\tau] = M_0 = 0$, that is $\mathbb{E}[S_\tau^2] = \mathbb{E}[\tau]$. At $\tau$ the price is $5$ ticks from her entry, so $S_\tau^2 = 25$ on every path. Hence $\mathbb{E}[\tau] = 25$ price changes.
>>
>>The same argument with exits at $\pm a$ gives $\mathbb{E}[\tau] = a^2$. With exits at $10$ ticks she waits $100$ price changes on average, four times as long. A band twice as wide takes four times as long to leave, because a random walk spreads like $\sqrt{n}$. The walk $S_n$ is itself a martingale, so $\mathbb{E}[S_\tau] = 0$, and she reaches the upper exit with probability $1/2$.

>[!example] (Hard) A Two-Step Tree as a Martingale
>A stock trades at $\$50$ and pays no dividends. Every six months its price rises by $15\%$ or falls by $10\%$, so $u = 1.15$ and $d = 0.90$. The risk-free rate is $6\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A one-year European call on the stock has strike $\$50$.
>
>>[!example]- Show that the discounted stock price is a martingale under the risk-neutral probability, find the call price and its discounted value at every node, and check that the discounted call price is a martingale too.
>>Each step has $\Delta t = 0.5$, so $e^{r\Delta t} = e^{0.03} = 1.03045$. The risk-neutral probability of an up move is
>>$$p = \frac{1.03045 - 0.90}{1.15 - 0.90} = 0.5218.$$
>>So $1 - p = 0.4782$. The stock is worth $\$57.50$ or $\$45.00$ after six months, and $\$66.125$, $\$51.75$, or $\$40.50$ after one year. Discounting by $e^{-0.03}$ and $e^{-0.06}$ gives $55.80$ and $43.67$ after six months, and $62.27$, $48.74$, and $38.14$ after one year. The forecasts below are for today, after an up move, and after a down move, in that order:
>>$$\begin{aligned} 0.5218 \times 55.80 + 0.4782 \times 43.67 &= 50.00, \\ 0.5218 \times 62.27 + 0.4782 \times 48.74 &= 55.80, \\ 0.5218 \times 48.74 + 0.4782 \times 38.14 &= 43.67. \end{aligned}$$
>>Each forecast equals the discounted price at its node, so the discounted stock price is a martingale under $p$.
>>
>>The call pays $\$16.125$, $\$1.75$, and $0$ at the three final nodes. One step of backward induction gives its prices after six months,
>>$$f_u = e^{-0.03}(0.5218 \times 16.125 + 0.4782 \times 1.75) = \$8.98, \qquad f_d = e^{-0.03}(0.5218 \times 1.75) = \$0.89.$$
>>Here $f_u$ and $f_d$ are the call prices after an up move and after a down move. Another step gives $f = e^{-0.03}(0.5218 \times 8.98 + 0.4782 \times 0.89) = \$4.96$ today. The discounted call values are $4.96$ today, $8.71$ and $0.86$ after six months, and $15.186$, $1.648$, and $0$ after one year. The forecasts at the same three nodes are
>>$$\begin{aligned} 0.5218 \times 8.71 + 0.4782 \times 0.86 &= 4.96, \\ 0.5218 \times 15.186 + 0.4782 \times 1.648 &= 8.71, \\ 0.5218 \times 1.648 + 0.4782 \times 0 &= 0.86. \end{aligned}$$
>>Each one matches, so the discounted call price is a martingale under $p$ too. Each backward-induction step is this martingale check solved for the unknown value. Under a real-world probability $p^* = 0.6473$, which corresponds to an expected return rate $\mu = 12\%$, the forecast of the discounted call from today is $0.6473 \times 8.71 + 0.3527 \times 0.86 = 5.94$. That exceeds $4.96$, so the discounted call is not a martingale under $p^*$.
