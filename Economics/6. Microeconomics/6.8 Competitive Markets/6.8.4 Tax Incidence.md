>[!note] Tax Incidence
>**Tax incidence** describes who bears a per-unit tax $t$. Let $p$ be the price buyers pay, including the tax. Sellers receive $p - t$. The equilibrium price $p^*$ solves
>$$Y^S(p^* - t, w, r) = X^D(p^*, M).$$
>- The [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] gives the effect of the tax on both prices, with the signed elasticities of [[6.8.3 Elasticities]]:
>$$\frac{\partial p^*}{\partial t} = \frac{\varepsilon_{S,p}}{\varepsilon_{S,p} - \varepsilon_{D,p}}, \qquad \frac{\partial (p^* - t)}{\partial t} = \frac{\varepsilon_{D,p}}{\varepsilon_{S,p} - \varepsilon_{D,p}}.$$
>- With $\varepsilon_{D,p} \le 0 \le \varepsilon_{S,p}$, the first ratio lies in $[0, 1]$. It is the buyers' share of each extra dollar of tax. The second lies in $[-1, 0]$, and minus it is the sellers' share. The two shares add to one.
>- The least elastic side bears the larger part of the tax. Buyers' share over sellers' share equals $\varepsilon_{S,p}/|\varepsilon_{D,p}|$.
>- *Inflexible supply* (fixed capacity, vertical supply, $\varepsilon_{S,p} = 0$): sellers bear the whole tax. *Flexible supply* (constant returns to scale, horizontal supply, $\varepsilon_{S,p} \to \infty$): buyers bear it. *Inflexible demand* (vertical demand, $\varepsilon_{D,p} = 0$): buyers bear it.
>- Quantity falls: $\partial X^{D*}/\partial t = (\partial X^D/\partial p)(\partial p^*/\partial t) \le 0$, with equality only when one side is perfectly inelastic. In percent terms, $\%\Delta X \approx \frac{\varepsilon_{S,p}\,\varepsilon_{D,p}}{\varepsilon_{S,p} - \varepsilon_{D,p}}\cdot\frac{\Delta t}{p}$.
>- A subsidy is a tax with $t < 0$. The same formulas apply: the buyers' price falls, the sellers' price rises, and the quantity rises.
>- Taxing buyers instead of sellers gives the same buyers' price, sellers' price, and quantity. Only the elasticities decide the split.
>- The formulas are derivatives at the current tax. For linear supply and demand the shares are constant, $\partial p^*/\partial t = Y^{S\prime}/(Y^{S\prime} - X^{D\prime})$, so they hold for any $t$. The welfare cost of the tax is in [[6.8.6 Welfare Applications]].

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture puts the tax on sellers and computes the quantity effect in levels, $\partial X^{D*}/\partial t = (\partial X^D/\partial p)(\partial p^*/\partial t)$. The equivalence of taxing buyers, the percent formula for the quantity, and the constant shares under linear curves are extensions.

# Intuition

A per-unit tax drives a **wedge** between the price buyers pay and the price sellers keep. The number of units bought must equal the number sold. The market settles at the quantity where the demand price and the supply price differ by exactly $t$. The question is how far each price moves. The side that can easily walk away from the market forces the other side to absorb the tax. Drivers who can switch to trains push a fuel tax onto refiners. Smokers who cannot quit pay a cigarette tax themselves.

The animation uses the market $Y^S = 25(p - t)$ and $X^D = 100 - 50p$. In the $(Y, p)$ plane the tax shifts the supply curve up by $t$, dashed. The purple wedge has height $t$, and the shaded rectangle is the tax revenue $t \cdot Y$. As $t$ rises from $0$ to $1.5$, buyers' price rises by $1/3$ of each step and sellers' price falls by $2/3$. Demand, with $\varepsilon_{D,p} = -2$ at $t = 0$, is more elastic than supply, with $\varepsilon_{S,p} = 1$. Revenue is $0$, $12.5$, $16.7$, then $12.5$. It falls at high taxes because the quantity shrinks.

# Properties

#### *(The Rigid Side Pays)* A side whose quantity barely responds to price keeps trading at almost any price, so it absorbs most of the tax.

When supply is vertical, sellers offer the same quantity at any price. Buyers' price is pinned by the demand curve at that quantity, so it cannot rise, and sellers lose the full $t$. Land and fixed seating capacity behave this way. When supply is horizontal, as under constant returns to scale with $p = AC$, sellers leave the market if their net price falls even slightly. Buyers' price then rises by the full $t$. The two panels show these limits with $t = 2$. In general the shares are $\varepsilon_{S,p}$ and $|\varepsilon_{D,p}|$ divided by their sum, so the less responsive side pays more.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture puts the tax on sellers only. The result below is an extension.

#### *(Legal Incidence Is Irrelevant)* A tax collected from buyers produces the same prices and quantity as a tax collected from sellers.

If buyers pay the tax, sellers receive $p_S$ and buyers pay $p_S + t$, so the equilibrium solves $Y^S(p_S) = X^D(p_S + t)$. Write $p = p_S + t$ and this is $Y^S(p - t) = X^D(p)$, the same equation as before. The legal duty to send the money to the government changes the label on the posted price and nothing else. Payroll taxes split between worker and employer, and sales taxes added at the register, are split by elasticities in the same way.

#### *(Effect on Quantity)* The tax lowers the quantity traded by an amount that depends on both elasticities.

From the derivation, $\partial X^{D*}/\partial t = Y^{S\prime}X^{D\prime}/(Y^{S\prime} - X^{D\prime})$, which is negative when both slopes are nonzero. If supply or demand is vertical, the quantity does not move. Lost trades are the source of the deadweight loss in [[6.8.6 Welfare Applications]]. A vertical supply curve therefore gives a tax with no deadweight loss. A horizontal supply curve with sloped demand gives a loss, even though sellers bear none of the tax.

# Derivation

We want $\partial p^*/\partial t$. Define $F(p, t) = Y^S(p - t) - X^D(p)$. Then $F_p = Y^{S\prime}(p - t) - X^{D\prime}(p)$ and $F_t = -Y^{S\prime}(p - t)$. The [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] gives
$$\frac{\partial p^*}{\partial t} = -\frac{F_t}{F_p} = -\frac{-Y^{S\prime}}{Y^{S\prime} - X^{D\prime}} = \frac{Y^{S\prime}\,\frac{p}{Y}}{\left(Y^{S\prime} - X^{D\prime}\right)\frac{p}{Y}} = \frac{\varepsilon_{S,p}}{\varepsilon_{S,p} - \varepsilon_{D,p}}.$$
The third step multiplies the numerator and the denominator by $p/Y$, using $Y = Y^{S*} = X^{D*}$. For the sellers' price, $\partial(p^* - t)/\partial t = \partial p^*/\partial t - 1 = \varepsilon_{D,p}/(\varepsilon_{S,p} - \varepsilon_{D,p})$. For the quantity, the chain rule gives $\partial X^{D*}/\partial t = X^{D\prime}\,\partial p^*/\partial t$. Both elasticities use the buyers' price $p$, with $Y^{S\prime}$ evaluated at $p - t$. At $t = 0$ this is the ordinary supply elasticity. At a positive tax, the supply elasticity measured at the sellers' price $p - t$ equals $\varepsilon_{S,p}\,(p - t)/p$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Tax in a Linear Market
>The market has $AY = 25p$ and $AD = 100 - 50p$, with $p^* = 4/3$. The government adds a tax of $t = 1$ per unit, paid by sellers.
>
>>[!example]- Find the buyers' price, the sellers' price, the quantity, and the revenue. Check the split with elasticities.
>>Sellers receive $p - 1$, so $25(p - 1) = 100 - 50p$, which gives $75p = 125$ and $p^* = 5/3$. Sellers receive $2/3$, and the quantity is $25 \cdot 2/3 = 50/3 \approx 16.7$, half the untaxed $100/3$. Revenue is $1 \cdot 50/3 \approx 16.7$. Buyers pay $1/3$ more and sellers get $2/3$ less. At the untaxed equilibrium $\varepsilon_{S,p} = 1$ and $\varepsilon_{D,p} = -2$, so the buyers' share is $1/(1 + 2) = 1/3$. Both curves are linear, so the derivative rule holds for the whole tax.

>[!example] (Medium) A Gasoline Tax
>Gasoline sells at $\$4.00$ per gallon. Suppose the supply elasticity is $1.5$, the short-run demand elasticity is $-0.25$, and the long-run demand elasticity is $-0.75$. A tax of $\$0.20$ per gallon is added.
>
>>[!example]- Estimate the change in the pump price, the refiners' price, and the quantity in each run.
>>In the short run the buyers' share is $1.5/(1.5 + 0.25) = 0.857$. The pump price rises by about $0.857 \times 0.20 = \$0.171$, and refiners lose $\$0.029$ per gallon. The quantity falls by about $\frac{1.5 \times (-0.25)}{1.75} \times \frac{0.20}{4} = -0.214 \times 0.05 = -1.07\%$. In the long run the buyers' share is $1.5/2.25 = 0.667$. The pump price rises by $\$0.133$, refiners lose $\$0.067$, and the quantity falls by $\frac{1.5 \times (-0.75)}{2.25} \times 0.05 = -2.5\%$. Drivers adjust over time, by buying smaller cars or moving closer to work, so their demand becomes more elastic and more of the tax shifts to refiners.

>[!example] (Hard) An Electric Car Subsidy
>Demand for electric cars is $X^D = 300 - 5p_B$ and supply is $Y^S = 5p_S - 100$, in thousands of cars, with prices in thousands of dollars. The government pays $\$4$ thousand per car.
>
>>[!example]- (a) Find prices, quantity, and cost when the subsidy goes to buyers, and again when it goes to sellers. (b) Redo (a) with a fixed capacity of $100$ thousand cars.
>>(a) Without the subsidy, $300 - 5p = 5p - 100$ gives $p = 40$ and $Q = 100$. Paid to buyers, $p_B = p_S - 4$, so $300 - 5(p_S - 4) = 5p_S - 100$ and $p_S = 42$, $p_B = 38$, $Q = 110$. Paid to sellers, sellers get $p_B + 4$, so $5(p_B + 4) - 100 = 300 - 5p_B$ and $p_B = 38$, $p_S = 42$, $Q = 110$. The outcome is the same. At the start $\varepsilon_{S,p} = 5 \cdot 40/100 = 2$ and $\varepsilon_{D,p} = -2$, so with $t = -4$ the buyers' price falls by $\frac{2}{4} \times 4 = 2$. The program costs $4 \times 110 = 440$, or $\$440$ million.
>>
>>(b) With supply fixed at $100$, $\varepsilon_{S,p} = 0$. Demand must still equal $100$, so $p_B = 40$ as before, and sellers receive $44$. Buyers gain nothing. The whole $\$400$ million goes to sellers, and no extra car is sold.
