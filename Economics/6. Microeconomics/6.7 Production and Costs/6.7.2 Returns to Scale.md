>[!note] Returns to Scale
>- **Returns to scale** describe how output responds when every input is multiplied by the same factor $t > 1$. For all input bundles $\mathbf{z}$ and all $t > 1$,
>$$\text{DRS: } f(t\mathbf{z}) < t f(\mathbf{z}), \qquad \text{CRS: } f(t\mathbf{z}) = t f(\mathbf{z}), \qquad \text{IRS: } f(t\mathbf{z}) > t f(\mathbf{z}).$$
>- Each definition must hold at every bundle and every $t > 1$, so a technology can belong to none of the three classes.
>- **Cobb-Douglas** $f(L, K) = AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta}$ gives $f(tL, tK) = t^{\alpha+\beta}f(L, K)$, so the class is decided by $\alpha + \beta < 1$, $= 1$, or $> 1$. With one input, $y = L^{\alpha}$ has DRS, CRS, or IRS as $\alpha < 1$, $= 1$, or $> 1$.
>- Returns to scale and [[6.7.1 Production Functions|diminishing marginal product]] are separate. For Cobb-Douglas, $f'_L$ diminishes exactly when $\beta < 1$ and $f'_K$ exactly when $\alpha < 1$, while the scale class depends on $\alpha + \beta$. The function $K^{0.6}L^{0.6}$ has diminishing marginal products and IRS.
>- The Cobb-Douglas cost function from [[6.7.3 Cost Minimization]] is $c(w, r, y) = B\,(y/A)^{1/(\alpha+\beta)}$, with $B$ depending only on $w, r, \alpha, \beta$. With one input, $c(w, y) = wy^{1/\alpha}$, $\text{MC} = \frac{1}{\alpha}wy^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}$, and $\text{AC} = wy^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}$.
>- For these two cost functions: under DRS, cost is convex in $y$, and marginal cost rises and lies above average cost. Under CRS, $\text{AC} = \text{MC}$ is constant ($B/A$ for Cobb-Douglas, $w$ for one input). Under IRS, cost is concave, and both curves fall with marginal cost below average cost.
>- A price taker maximizes $py - c(w, r, y)$, which needs $c''_{yy} > 0$ ([[6.7.5 Profit Maximization and Supply]]). For Cobb-Douglas under DRS there is an interior $y^*$ with $p = c'_y$ and an upward-sloping supply function, the only well-behaved case under perfect competition. Under CRS, $y^* = 0$ if $p < B/A$, any $y^* \ge 0$ if $p = B/A$, and $y^* \to \infty$ if $p > B/A$. Under IRS the first-order condition gives a profit minimum and $y^* \to \infty$ for every $p > 0$.

# Intuition

A firm that can build an exact copy of its plant, with the same machines and the same number of workers, should double its output. This replication argument makes constant returns the natural benchmark. Increasing returns appear when size itself helps. Large teams can specialize, and a pipeline that is twice as wide carries four times the oil with only twice the steel in its walls. Decreasing returns appear when some input cannot be copied, such as the attention of the founder or the best piece of land, so doubling the measured inputs less than doubles output.

# Properties

#### *(Scale Versus One Input)* Diminishing marginal product holds the other inputs fixed, and returns to scale move all inputs together.

A new worker in a fixed factory crowds the existing machines, so her marginal product falls. If the firm adds machines in proportion, the crowding disappears. For Cobb-Douglas, $f''_{LL} < 0$ needs $\beta < 1$ and $f''_{KK} < 0$ needs $\alpha < 1$, yet $\alpha + \beta$ can exceed $1$. The function $KL$ has constant marginal products and IRS. The Medium example works through $K^{0.6}L^{0.6}$.

#### *(Why IRS Breaks Price Taking)* Under IRS, the output where price equals marginal cost minimizes profit.

Profit $\pi(y) = py - c(y)$ has $\pi'' = -c''$. With Cobb-Douglas technology and $\alpha + \beta > 1$, the cost $B(y/A)^{1/(\alpha+\beta)}$ is concave in $y$, so $\pi'' > 0$ and the first-order condition picks a minimum. Average cost is proportional to $y^{1/(\alpha+\beta) - 1}$, which falls toward zero as $y$ grows. For any $p > 0$ there is an output beyond which $p > \text{AC}$, and profit $(p - \text{AC})\,y$ grows without bound. No supply function exists, and the firm cannot stay small relative to its market. Under CRS the firm accepts any size only at $p = B/A$. A market of such firms supplies any quantity at that price, a horizontal supply curve, which is the flexible-supply case of [[6.8.4 Tax Incidence]]. The figure plots profit for $y = L^{\alpha}$ with $w = 1$ and $p = 1.2$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture showed $f(tK, tL) = t^{\alpha+\beta}f(K, L)$ and derived costs only for Cobb-Douglas and for $y = L^{\alpha}$. The three properties below extend these results to any homogeneous technology and to technologies that fit none of the three classes.

#### *(Homogeneous Technologies)* Cobb-Douglas is homogeneous of degree $s = \alpha + \beta$, so its class is the same at every bundle and every scale.

A function is homogeneous of degree $s$ when $f(t\mathbf{z}) = t^s f(\mathbf{z})$, the notion used in [[6.3.2 Homogeneity of Demand]]. If $\mathbf{z}$ produces $y$, then $t\mathbf{z}$ produces $t^s y$. To multiply output by $m$, the firm multiplies all inputs by $m^{1/s}$. In the first figure, $(LK)^{3/4}$ has $s = 1.5$, so output $3$ needs inputs $3^{1/1.5} = 2.08$ along the ray. A homogeneous technology has DRS, CRS, or IRS exactly when $s < 1$, $s = 1$, or $s > 1$. Non-homogeneous technologies can switch class from one scale to another.

#### *(Scale Shapes Cost)* For a technology homogeneous of degree $s$, $c(w, r, y) = c(w, r, 1)\,y^{1/s}$, so $\text{MC} = \text{AC}/s$.

Let $\mathbf{z}^*$ be the cheapest bundle that makes one unit. Then $y^{1/s}\mathbf{z}^*$ makes $y$ units. No bundle makes $y$ more cheaply, because scaling it down by $y^{-1/s}$ would make one unit more cheaply than $\mathbf{z}^*$. Cost is linear in inputs, which gives the formula. Then $\text{AC} = c(w, r, 1)\,y^{1/s - 1}$ and $\text{MC} = \text{AC}/s$. Average cost rises under DRS, stays flat under CRS, and falls under IRS. The Cobb-Douglas and one-input formulas fit this pattern. Cobb-Douglas has $c(w, r, 1) = BA^{-1/(\alpha+\beta)}$, and one input has $\text{MC} = \text{AC}/\alpha$.

#### *(None of the Three)* A technology with IRS at small scale and DRS at large scale fits no class, and its average cost falls and then rises.

The definitions quantify over every bundle, so a technology that switches class fails all three. Such technologies are common. A setup requirement or an indivisible machine gives increasing returns at first, and a hard-to-copy input gives decreasing returns later. The resulting U-shaped average cost is the shape behind [[6.7.4 Cost Curves]] and the long-run price $p = \min \text{AC}$. The Hard example builds one.

# Derivation

We want the scale class of Cobb-Douglas and the sign of the profit second-order condition. Multiplying both inputs by $t$ gives
$$f(tL, tK) = A(tK)^{\alpha}(tL)^{\beta} = t^{\alpha+\beta}AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta} = t^{\alpha+\beta}f(L, K).$$
For $t > 1$, $t^{\alpha+\beta}$ is below, equal to, or above $t$ exactly when $\alpha + \beta < 1$, $= 1$, or $> 1$. With $c(y) = B(y/A)^{1/(\alpha+\beta)}$, profit is $py - B(y/A)^{1/(\alpha+\beta)}$. Its first and second derivatives are
$$p - \frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}\,\frac{B}{A}\left(\frac{y}{A}\right)^{\frac{1-(\alpha+\beta)}{\alpha+\beta}}, \qquad -\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}\,\frac{1-(\alpha+\beta)}{\alpha+\beta}\,\frac{B}{A^2}\left(\frac{y}{A}\right)^{\frac{1-2(\alpha+\beta)}{\alpha+\beta}}.$$
All factors of the second derivative are positive except $-(1 - (\alpha+\beta))$. It is negative under DRS, so the first-order condition gives a maximum by [[7.1 Unconstrained Optimality Conditions]]. It is positive under IRS, so it gives a minimum. Under CRS it is zero and profit is $(p - B/A)\,y$, a straight line through the origin. Its slope decides between $y^* = 0$, any $y^*$, and no finite $y^*$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) One Input, Two Exponents
>A firm uses only labor, at wage $w = 1$, with $y = L^{\alpha}$. It sells at price $p$.
>
>>[!example]- Classify returns to scale for $\alpha = \frac12$ and $\alpha = 2$, and describe the supply function in each case.
>>For $\alpha = \frac12$, $(tL)^{1/2} = t^{1/2}L^{1/2} < tL^{1/2}$, so DRS. Cost is $c = y^2$, with $\text{MC} = 2y$ and $\text{AC} = y$. Setting $p = 2y$ gives $y^* = p/2$, and profit $p^2/4 > 0$, so the firm produces at every positive price. Supply is increasing and given by $y = (c')^{-1}(p)$ at every $p > 0$, with no shutdown threshold.
>>
>>For $\alpha = 2$, $(tL)^2 = t^2L^2 > tL^2$, so IRS. Cost is $c = \sqrt{y}$, with $\text{MC} = 1/(2\sqrt{y})$ falling. The condition $p = \text{MC}$ gives $y = 1/(4p^2)$, which is a profit minimum. Profit $py - \sqrt{y}$ grows without bound, so $y^* \to \infty$ at every $p > 0$ and no supply function exists.

>[!example] (Medium) Diminishing Returns to Each Input, Increasing Returns to Scale
>A workshop has $f(L, K) = K^{0.6}L^{0.6}$. The owner sees that each extra worker adds less output and concludes that the workshop is too big.
>
>>[!example]- Compute output at $(L, K) = (1, 1), (2, 1), (3, 1), (2, 2)$. With $w = r = 1$, find the cost of $y = 1$ and $y = 2$. Is the owner right?
>>Output is $1$, $2^{0.6} \approx 1.516$, $3^{0.6} \approx 1.933$, and $2^{1.2} \approx 2.297$. With capital fixed at $1$, the second worker adds $0.516$ and the third adds $0.417$, so the marginal product of labor diminishes. Doubling both inputs more than doubles output, since $2.297 > 2$, so the technology has IRS with $\alpha + \beta = 1.2$.
>>
>>With $\alpha = \beta$ and $w = r$, the cheapest bundle has $L = K$, and $B = 2$ in the Cobb-Douglas formula. So $c(y) = 2y^{1/1.2}$. One unit costs $2$. Two units need inputs scaled by $2^{1/1.2} \approx 1.78$ and cost $3.56$, so average cost falls from $2$ to $1.78$. The owner confuses the two properties. Hiring workers alone runs into diminishing returns, while growing both inputs together lowers average cost.

>[!example] (Hard) A Setup Requirement
>A software firm needs one unit of labor to set up before it produces anything: $f(L) = \sqrt{L - 1}$ for $L \ge 1$ and $f(L) = 0$ for $L < 1$. The wage is $w = 10$.
>
>>[!example]- Show that the technology has none of the three scale properties. Find the cost function, the minimum of average cost, and the supply function. How much does the firm produce at $p = 30$ and at $p = 16$?
>>At $L = 1.25$ and $t = 2$, $f(2.5) = \sqrt{1.5} \approx 1.22 > 2f(1.25) = 1$, an IRS comparison. At $L = 2$ and $t = 2$, $f(4) = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.73 < 2f(2) = 2$, a DRS comparison. Since both inequalities occur, none of the definitions holds.
>>
>>Producing $y > 0$ needs $L = 1 + y^2$, so $c(y) = 10(1 + y^2)$ for $y > 0$ and $c(0) = 0$. Average cost $10(1/y + y)$ is U-shaped with minimum $20$ at $y = 1$. Marginal cost $20y$ also equals $20$ there, so it crosses average cost at its minimum. The condition $p = 20y$ gives $y = p/20$, with profit $p^2/40 - 10$. This is non-negative only when $p \ge 20$. Supply is $y = p/20$ above the threshold $\bar p = 20$ and $0$ below it. At $p = 30$ the firm makes $1.5$ units for a profit of $12.5$. At $p = 16$ the first-order condition suggests $0.8$ units, which would lose $3.6$, so the firm produces nothing.
