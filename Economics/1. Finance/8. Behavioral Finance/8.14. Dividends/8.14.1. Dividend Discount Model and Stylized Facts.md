>[!note] Dividend Discount Model and Stylized Facts
>**The dividend discount model (DDM)** is the foundational valuation model in finance: the value of a stock equals the present value of its future expected dividends, discounted at the equity cost of capital. It comes from applying the standard discounted-cash-flow rule to equities and identifying $D_t$ (dividend per share) as the equity-holder's cash flow:
>$$V_0 = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\mathbb{E}_0[D_k]}{(1+r_s)^k}.$$
>
>**Special cases — closed-form valuations.**
>- **Constant dividend.** If $D_t = D_0$ for all $t$ (preferred-stock-style perpetuity):
>$$V_0 = \frac{D_0}{r_s}.$$
>Example: a preferred stock paying $\$2$/year at an $8\%$ discount rate is worth $\$25$/share.
>- **Constant growth (Gordon Growth Model).** If $D_t = D_0(1+g)^t$ with $g < r_s$:
>$$V_0 = \frac{D_1}{r_s - g} = \frac{D_0(1+g)}{r_s - g}.$$
>The Gordon formula is the workhorse of valuation; it isolates the three drivers of equity value (expected near-term dividend, long-run growth rate, equity cost of capital).
>
>**Why the DDM matters for the dividend puzzle.** The DDM expresses share value entirely in terms of dividends, which makes it look as though dividends are the unique driver of equity value. The Miller-Modigliani irrelevance theorem shows the appearance is misleading: in a frictionless world, the *path* of dividends is irrelevant; only the present value of total free cash flow to equity matters. A firm that pays no dividends but reinvests profitably or repurchases shares delivers value to equity holders just as effectively as a dividend-paying firm. The DDM is a valuation framework, not a normative argument that firms *must* pay dividends.
>
>**Seven stylized facts.**
>1. **Centuries-old practice.** Dividends have been the primary corporate payout vehicle for four centuries, since the founding of the Dutch East India Company in $1602$.
>2. **Concentrated among mature firms.** Dividend-payers are typically large, well-established firms with stable cash flows and low idiosyncratic risk. Young firms and high-growth firms generally do not pay.
>3. **Repurchases are growing.** Since the early $1980$s (after SEC Rule $10b$-$18$ provided a safe harbor for open-market repurchases), share repurchases have grown substantially. Repurchases now exceed dividends in aggregate U.S. corporate payouts.
>4. **Dividends are sticky.** Firms smooth dividends over time and treat dividend cuts as costly. Annual changes in dividends are far smaller than annual changes in earnings.
>5. **Dividends are tax-inefficient.** Before the U.S. Jobs and Growth Tax Relief Reconciliation Act ($\text{JGTRRA}$) of $2003$, dividends were taxed at ordinary income rates ($35\%$+ for high earners) while long-term capital gains were taxed at preferential rates ($15\%$–$20\%$). $\text{JGTRRA}$ created the "qualified dividend" category, taxing most dividends at the long-term capital-gains rate. Even with this reform, repurchases remain tax-advantaged because they let shareholders defer taxation entirely (the gain is taxed only at sale).
>6. **Initiations are good news; omissions are bad news.** Stock prices react positively to dividend initiations and increases (typically $1$–$3\%$ on announcement) and negatively (often $5$–$10\%$+) to dividend cuts and omissions. The asymmetric magnitude reflects the stickiness: cutting a dividend signals that the firm has run out of good options.
>7. **Cash-dividend premium.** Investors demand a premium for cash-paying shares. The Citizens Utility Company case (the firm had two share classes, one paying cash dividends and one paying stock dividends; the cash-paying class consistently traded at a premium despite higher tax exposure) is the cleanest demonstration.

# Intuition

The DDM is a near-tautology: any dollar that the equity-holder ever receives must come through some dividend, repurchase, or liquidation, so the present value of the equity is the present value of those future payments. Where the model becomes interesting is in its application: under what assumptions can we replace the infinite sum with a closed form, and what determines the inputs (next-period dividend, growth rate, discount rate)?

The Gordon Growth Model is the cleanest closed form. It assumes the firm pays a dividend that grows at a constant rate $g$ forever, with $g$ strictly less than the discount rate $r_s$ (or the geometric series diverges). The formula $V_0 = D_1/(r_s - g)$ has three inputs, each of which captures a distinct economic content: $D_1$ is what the firm currently pays; $g$ is the firm's long-run sustainable growth rate; $r_s$ is the equity cost of capital, which depends on the firm's systematic risk via CAPM. The formula tells you how to think about each driver separately and how their combination produces the share price.

The seven stylized facts about dividends are organized to motivate the puzzle. The longevity of the practice (Fact 1), the cross-sectional concentration in mature firms (Fact 2), the recent rise of repurchases (Fact 3), the stickiness (Fact 4), and the tax inefficiency (Fact 5) are all data points that any candidate dividend theory must accommodate. The asymmetric market reactions (Fact 6) and the Citizens Utility cash-dividend premium (Fact 7) provide additional empirical hooks that distinguish among the candidate theories.

# Properties

#### *(DDM Definition)* Stock value is the present value of expected future dividends.

$$V_0 = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\mathbb{E}_0[D_k]}{(1+r_s)^k}.$$
The DDM follows from applying the standard present-value rule to the equity-holder's cash flow, which is the dividend stream plus the eventual liquidation value. By the iterated-expectations / law-of-iterated-expectations argument, the latter cancels out for an infinitely-lived firm and the present value reduces to the dividend stream alone.

#### *(Constant-Dividend Special Case)* Perpetual constant dividend gives $V_0 = D_0/r_s$.

If $D_t = D_0$ for all $t$, the geometric sum closes to $V_0 = D_0/r_s$. The formula values preferred stock and any equity claim with a level perpetual cash flow. Setting $D_0 = \$2$ and $r_s = 8\%$ gives $V_0 = \$25$.

#### *(Gordon Growth Special Case)* Constant growth gives $V_0 = D_1/(r_s - g)$.

If $D_t = D_0(1+g)^t$ with $g < r_s$:
$$V_0 = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{D_0(1+g)^k}{(1+r_s)^k} = D_0 \cdot \frac{(1+g)/(1+r_s)}{1 - (1+g)/(1+r_s)} = \frac{D_1}{r_s - g}.$$
The Gordon formula is the most-used closed-form valuation tool in equity analysis. It collapses the entire infinite stream into three numbers: $D_1$, $r_s$, and $g$. The condition $g < r_s$ is essential; if $g \geq r_s$, the present value is infinite, which is economically incoherent.

#### *(Stickiness)* Dividend changes are far smaller than earnings changes.

Lintner ($1956$) documented that dividend changes are smoothed: when earnings rise (or fall) sharply, dividends adjust only partially and slowly toward a target payout ratio. The empirical regularity is that the *standard deviation* of annual dividend growth is less than half the standard deviation of annual earnings growth at the firm level. Managers actively smooth, and the smoothing is itself a stylized fact requiring explanation.

#### *(Tax Inefficiency)* Pre-$2003$ dividends were taxed more heavily than capital gains.

For most of the post-WWII period, U.S. dividend income was taxed at ordinary income rates (top rate $35$–$50\%$) while long-term capital gains were taxed at preferential rates (typically $15$–$28\%$). The Jobs and Growth Tax Relief Reconciliation Act of $2003$ created the *qualified dividend* category, taxing most dividends at the long-term capital-gains rate ($15$–$20\%$). Even after $2003$, repurchases retain a deferral advantage: the equity-holder is taxed on a repurchase only if she chooses to sell, while a dividend forces immediate taxation regardless of consumption needs.

#### *(Asymmetric Announcement Returns)* Initiations are $+1$–$3\%$; omissions are $-5$–$10\%$+.

Stock-price reactions to dividend changes show an asymmetric magnitude pattern. Initiations (a firm starting to pay a dividend after never having paid one) typically produce positive announcement-day abnormal returns of $1$–$3\%$. Increases produce $0.5$–$1.5\%$. Omissions (a firm stopping a previously-existing dividend) produce negative reactions of $5$–$10\%$+, sometimes much larger. The asymmetry reflects the stickiness: investors interpret a cut as bad news because cuts only happen when management has run out of good options.

#### *(Citizens Utility Case)* Cash-dividend shares trade at a premium despite tax disadvantage.

Citizens Utility Company maintained two share classes that differed only in the form of dividend payment: one class paid cash dividends, the other paid stock dividends. Under standard tax accounting, the cash-paying class was strictly worse because cash dividends were taxed immediately while stock dividends were not. Yet the cash-paying class consistently traded at a premium of $5$–$10\%$ over the stock-paying class. The case is one of the cleanest violations of tax-rationality in equity markets and a key motivating fact for the [[8.14.4. Behavioral Theories of Dividends|behavioral dividend theories]].

# Examples

>[!example] Valuing AT&T's preferred stock
>Suppose AT&T issues a perpetual preferred stock paying $\$5$ annual dividend per share. The market discount rate for preferred-stock-style perpetuities is $6\%$. The DDM gives $V_0 = \$5/0.06 = \$83.33$. If interest rates rise to $8\%$, the same preferred stock should fall to $\$5/0.08 = \$62.50$. Sensitivity to the discount rate makes preferred stock behave more like long-duration bonds than like common stock; the firm's underlying business operations affect the preferred only through the survival of the dividend stream.

>[!example]- Gordon Growth applied to a mature dividend-payer
>Coca-Cola pays an annual dividend of $\$1.84$ per share with a long-term growth rate of approximately $5\%$. The equity cost of capital for a stable mature firm with KO's beta is approximately $8\%$. Gordon Growth gives $V_0 = D_1/(r_s - g) = (\$1.84 \times 1.05)/(0.08 - 0.05) = \$1.93/0.03 = \$64.43$. Actual KO stock has traded in a range of approximately $\$50$–$\$70$ over recent years, which is consistent with this valuation given normal variation in the input assumptions.

>[!example]- Why the Gordon Growth Model breaks down
>The Gordon formula requires $g < r_s$. For young high-growth firms, the perceived growth rate $g$ may approach or exceed $r_s$ — Amazon in $1999$ had a long-term growth rate that many investors estimated at $20\%$+, against an equity cost of capital under $10\%$. The formula then implies infinite valuation, which is incoherent. Practitioners handle this by using two-stage models: a high-growth period of $5$–$10$ years followed by a Gordon-style stable-growth perpetuity. The transition point is a judgment call, and the resulting valuations are sensitive to the assumed long-run growth rate.
