>[!note] Autocovariance Function
>The **autocovariance function** of a [[2.1. Time Series|time series]] $\{X_t\}$ measures the linear dependence between observations at two time points $s$ and $t$, defined as
>$$\gamma_X(s, t) = \text{Cov}(X_s, X_t) = \mathbb{E}[(X_s - \mu_s)(X_t - \mu_t)],$$
>where $\mu_s = \mathbb{E}[X_s]$ and $\mu_t = \mathbb{E}[X_t]$ are the values of the [[2.2.1 Mean Function|mean function]] at times $s$ and $t$. This is the time series analogue of [[2.5b Second-Order Moments|covariance]] between two random variables.
>
>When the process is **(weakly) stationary**, the autocovariance depends only on the **lag** $h = |s - t|$, not on the absolute times, and is written
>$$\gamma_X(h) = \text{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+h}) \quad \text{for all } t.$$
>
>Note that $\gamma_X(t, t) = \gamma_{X}(0) = \text{Var}(X_t)$ is the variance of the process.
>
>>[!warning] Autocovariance is Not Scale-Free
>>The autocovariance $\gamma_X(h)$ has units equal to the square of the units of $X_t$, making it difficult to compare across series measured on different scales. The [[2.2.3 Autocorrelation Function|autocorrelation function]] $\rho_X(h) = \gamma_X(h)/\gamma_X(0)$ normalizes this to a unitless quantity in $[-1, 1]$.

# Intuition
The autocovariance function captures how the **memory** of a process decays over time. A large $|\gamma_X(h)|$ at lag $h$ means that knowing the value at time $t$ gives useful information about the value at time $t + h$. If $\gamma_X(h) > 0$, values tend to stay on the same side of the mean (persistence); if $\gamma_X(h) < 0$, they tend to alternate (mean-reversion). If $\gamma_X(h) \approx 0$ for all $h \neq 0$, the series has no linear memory: it behaves like white noise.

The rate at which $\gamma_X(h) \to 0$ as $h$ grows characterizes how quickly the process "forgets" its past. Short-memory processes (e.g., ARMA) have autocovariances that decay exponentially; long-memory processes have autocovariances that decay polynomially.

# Properties
#### (*Symmetry*) $\gamma_X(h) = \gamma_X(-h)$ for all $h$. Dependence between $X_t$ and $X_{t+h}$ is the same regardless of direction in time.
#### (*Positive Semi-Definiteness*) For any $n$, any times $t_1, \dots, t_n$, and any constants $a_1, \dots, a_n$,
$$\sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n a_i a_j \gamma_X(t_i, t_j) \geq 0.$$
This ensures that the variance of any linear combination $\sum a_i X_{t_i}$ is nonnegative.

#### (*Variance at Lag Zero*) $\gamma_X(0) = \text{Var}(X_t) \geq 0$.
#### (*Cauchy–Schwarz Bound*) $|\gamma_X(h)| \leq \gamma_X(0)$ for all $h$. The autocovariance at any lag cannot exceed the variance.
#### (*White Noise*) If $\{W_t\} \sim \text{WN}(0, \sigma^2)$, then $\gamma_W(h) = \begin{cases} \sigma^2 & h = 0, \\ 0 & h \neq 0. \end{cases}$
#### (*Linearity*) If $Y_t = aX_t + b$, then $\gamma_Y(h) = a^2\,\gamma_X(h)$.

# Examples
>[!example] MA(1) Process
>Let $X_t = W_t + \theta W_{t-1}$, where $\{W_t\} \sim \text{WN}(0, \sigma^2)$ and $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$.
>>[!example]- Derive the autocovariance function.
>>For lag $h = 0$:
>>$$\gamma_X(0) = \text{Var}(W_t + \theta W_{t-1}) = \sigma^2 + \theta^2\sigma^2 = (1 + \theta^2)\sigma^2.$$
>>For lag $h = 1$:
>>$$\gamma_X(1) = \text{Cov}(W_t + \theta W_{t-1},\; W_{t+1} + \theta W_t) = \theta\sigma^2,$$
>>since the only shared term is $W_t$ appearing as $\theta W_t$ in both expressions.
>>For $|h| \geq 2$: $\gamma_X(h) = 0$, because $X_t$ and $X_{t+h}$ share no common white noise terms. So
>>$$\gamma_X(h) = \begin{cases} (1+\theta^2)\sigma^2 & h = 0, \\ \theta\sigma^2 & |h| = 1, \\ 0 & |h| \geq 2. \end{cases}$$
>>The MA(1) has memory of exactly **one lag**: it forgets its past after a single step.

>[!example] Random Walk
>Let $X_t = \sum_{j=1}^t W_j$ with $\{W_j\} \sim \text{WN}(0, \sigma^2)$.
>>[!example]- Show that the autocovariance depends on absolute time, not just the lag.
>>For $s \leq t$:
>>$$\gamma_X(s, t) = \text{Cov}\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^s W_i,\; \sum_{j=1}^t W_j\right) = \sum_{i=1}^s \text{Var}(W_i) = s\sigma^2 = \min(s,t)\,\sigma^2,$$
>>since only the first $s$ terms overlap. Because $\gamma_X(s,t) = \min(s,t)\,\sigma^2$ depends on $s$ and $t$ individually, not just on $|s - t|$, the random walk is **not stationary**.
