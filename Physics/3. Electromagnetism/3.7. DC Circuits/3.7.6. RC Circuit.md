>[!note] RC Circuit
>An **RC circuit** holds a resistor $R$ and a capacitor $C$ in series. To charge the capacitor, connect the pair to an ideal source of emf $\mathcal{E}$ through a switch. The capacitor starts uncharged, and the switch closes at $t = 0$. The charge on the positive plate then grows as
>$$Q(t) = C\mathcal{E}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad \tau = RC.$$
>The constant $\tau$ is the **time constant**. It comes out in seconds when $R$ is in ohms and $C$ in farads. The current in the circuit is the rate at which the charge grows:
>$$I(t) = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R}\,e^{-t/\tau}.$$
>Here $\mathcal{E}/R$ is the current at the instant the switch closes. The current decays toward zero, and the charge rises toward $C\mathcal{E}$ without ever reaching it. The time constant sets how fast this happens:
>- At $t = \tau$, the charge is 63% of $C\mathcal{E}$, and the current is 37% of $\mathcal{E}/R$.
>- At $t = 2\tau$, they are 86% and 14%.
>- At $t = 3\tau$, they are 95% and 5%.
>
>An uncharged capacitor acts as a plain wire at the instant the switch closes. After many time constants it carries no current and acts as an open circuit. The EE note [[2.2i First-Order Circuits]] treats the RC circuit as one case of a first-order circuit.

# Intuition

The name lists the two elements, R for the resistor and C for the capacitor. At first the capacitor holds no charge and has no potential difference. The whole emf then sits across the resistor, and the current is as large as it will ever be. As charge builds on the plates, the capacitor's potential difference $Q/C$ grows. Less of the emf is left for the resistor, so the current falls. The current is proportional to the charge still missing, and that makes the approach exponential.

A larger $R$ lets less current through, so the capacitor takes longer to charge. A larger $C$ needs more charge to reach the same potential difference, which also takes longer. The product $RC$ combines both effects into one time.

The capacitor of the Easy example charges after the switch closes. The graphs trace $Q(t)$ and $I(t)$, with markers at $\tau$, $2\tau$, and $3\tau$. The capacitor acts as a wire at first and as a gap at long times.

# Derivation

Connect the source, the switch, the resistor, and the capacitor in one loop. Draw the current arrow so that it leaves the source's $+$ terminal. The capacitor plate that this current reaches becomes positive. Start at the source's $-$ terminal and travel along the arrow. With the [[3.7.5. Kirchhoff's Loop Rule|loop rule]] signs, the source gives $+\mathcal{E}$. The resistor, crossed along the current, gives $-IR$. The capacitor, crossed from its $+$ plate to its $-$ plate, gives $-Q/C$. The loop rule reads
$$\mathcal{E} - IR - \frac{Q}{C} = 0.$$
This is one equation with two unknowns, the charge $Q$ and the current $I$.

The second equation links them. The current brings charge to the positive plate, so the charge grows at the rate of the current, $I = +dQ/dt$. Substituting gives
$$\mathcal{E} = R\,\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{Q}{C}.$$
Here $R\,dQ/dt$ is the potential difference across the resistor, and $Q/C$ is the potential difference across the capacitor.

Separate the variables by moving every $Q$ to one side and $dt$ to the other:
$$\frac{dQ}{C\mathcal{E} - Q} = \frac{dt}{RC}.$$
The left side now holds only the charge, and the right side only the time. Integrating between limits builds in the starting condition $Q(0) = 0$, so no unknown constant appears. The lower limits are time $0$ and charge $0$, and the upper limits are time $t$ and charge $Q$. Tildes mark the integration variables, to keep them apart from the limits:
$$\int_0^{Q} \frac{d\tilde Q}{C\mathcal{E} - \tilde Q} = \int_0^{t} \frac{d\tilde t}{RC}.$$
The left side is $-\ln\!\big[(C\mathcal{E} - Q)/(C\mathcal{E})\big]$, and the right side is $t/RC$. Multiplying by $-1$ and taking the exponential of both sides gives $1 - Q/(C\mathcal{E}) = e^{-t/RC}$. Solving for $Q$ gives the charging curve in the callout. Differentiating it gives the current, $I = dQ/dt = (\mathcal{E}/R)\,e^{-t/RC}$.

# Properties

#### *(Timescale)* The capacitor never finishes charging, so $\tau$ measures how fast it approaches $C\mathcal{E}$.

The charge still missing at time $t$ is $C\mathcal{E}\,e^{-t/\tau}$. Each time constant multiplies it by $e^{-1} \approx 0.37$. After $\tau$, $2\tau$, and $3\tau$, the missing part is 37%, 14%, and 5%, which gives the percentages in the callout. After $5\tau$, less than 1% is missing. A "long time" means $t \gg \tau$, and "fully charged" means charged to within a small fraction. The current follows the same factors, since it is proportional to the missing charge.

#### *(Why $RC$ Is a Time)* An ohm times a farad is a second, and $\tau$ is the charging time at the starting current.

An ohm is a volt per ampere, and a farad is a coulomb per volt. Their product is a coulomb per ampere, which is a second. The time constant can also be read as $\tau = C\mathcal{E}/(\mathcal{E}/R)$. That is the final charge divided by the starting current. If the current stayed at its starting value, the capacitor would fill in exactly $\tau$. A $1.0\ \text{k}\Omega$ resistor with a $1.0\ \text{nF}$ capacitor gives $\tau = 1.0\ \mu\text{s}$. A $10\ \text{k}\Omega$ resistor with a $470\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor gives $4.7\ \text{s}$.

#### *(Wire First, Gap Later)* At $t = 0$ an uncharged capacitor can be replaced by a wire, and at long times by a gap.

At $t = 0$ the charge is zero, so the capacitor has no potential difference, like a wire. The current $\mathcal{E}/R$ is what the circuit would carry with the capacitor replaced by a wire. At long times the current is zero, like a gap, and the capacitor holds the full $\mathcal{E}$. This gives the start and the end of any network of resistors and uncharged capacitors. Replace every capacitor by a wire for $t = 0$, or by a gap for long times. Then solve the resulting resistor network with Kirchhoff's rules. The times in between need more work, done in EE with the methods of [[2.2i First-Order Circuits]].

#### *(The Jump Is Unphysical)* The model's current jumps from zero to $\mathcal{E}/R$ the instant the switch closes, which no real current does.

Before the switch closes, no current flows. A current is made of moving electrons, and they speed up over a short but nonzero time. The model leaves out the magnetic field that the current produces, and the induction that comes with it. [[3.11.4. LR Circuit]] adds induction to the model. A result that jumps or grows without limit, with no physical cause, means the model has left out some physics.

#### *(Discharging)* A charged capacitor connected across a resistor loses its charge as $Q_0\,e^{-t/\tau}$, with the same time constant.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture derived only the charging curve. Discharging follows from the same loop rule and comes from Giancoli section 26.5.

Let the capacitor start with charge $Q_0$ on its positive plate, with no source in the loop. The current leaves the positive plate through the resistor. Start at the negative plate and travel along the current. Crossing the capacitor from $-$ to $+$ gives $+Q/C$, and crossing the resistor along the current gives $-IR$. The current now drains the positive plate, so $I = -dQ/dt$. The loop rule and its solution are
$$\frac{Q}{C} + R\,\frac{dQ}{dt} = 0, \qquad Q(t) = Q_0\,e^{-t/RC}.$$
The first equation is the loop rule, and the second is its solution with $Q(0) = Q_0$. The current is $I = (Q_0/RC)\,e^{-t/RC}$, which starts at $V_0/R$ with $V_0 = Q_0/C$. The stored energy $Q_0^2/(2C)$ from [[3.5.3. Energy Stored in an Electric Field]] ends up as heat in the resistor.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Charge and Current at Each Time Constant
>A $20\ \text{k}\Omega$ resistor and an uncharged $150\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor connect in series to an ideal $9.0\ \text{V}$ source at $t = 0$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the time constant, the final charge, and the initial current. Then find the charge and the current at $t = \tau$ and $t = 3\tau$.
>>The time constant is
>>$$\tau = RC = (2.0 \times 10^{4}\ \Omega)(1.5 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{F}) = 3.0\ \text{s}.$$
>>This is the scale for every later time. The charge approaches $C\mathcal{E} = (150\ \mu\text{F})(9.0\ \text{V}) = 1.35\ \text{mC}$. The current starts at $\mathcal{E}/R = 9.0/(2.0 \times 10^{4}) = 0.45\ \text{mA}$.
>>
>>At $t = \tau = 3.0\ \text{s}$, the charge is $(0.632)(1.35) = 0.853\ \text{mC}$, and the current is $(0.368)(0.45) = 0.166\ \text{mA}$. At $t = 3\tau = 9.0\ \text{s}$, the charge is $(0.950)(1.35) = 1.28\ \text{mC}$, and the current is $(0.0498)(0.45) = 0.0224\ \text{mA}$.

>[!example] (Medium) Time to Reach 75% Charge
>A $5.0\ \text{k}\Omega$ resistor and an uncharged $400\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor connect in series to an ideal $12\ \text{V}$ source at $t = 0$.
>
>>[!example]- How long does the charge take to reach 75% of its final value, and what is the current then?
>>The time constant is $\tau = (5.0 \times 10^{3})(4.0 \times 10^{-4}) = 2.0\ \text{s}$. The final charge is $C\mathcal{E} = 4.8\ \text{mC}$, so the target is $3.6\ \text{mC}$. The charge is the fraction $1 - e^{-t/\tau}$ of its final value. Setting that fraction to $0.75$ gives $e^{-t/\tau} = 0.25$. Taking the logarithm of both sides gives
>>$$t = \tau\ln 4 = (2.0\ \text{s})(1.386) = 2.77\ \text{s}.$$
>>This is a little under $1.4$ time constants. The current starts at $\mathcal{E}/R = 2.4\ \text{mA}$ and decays by the same factor $e^{-t/\tau}$. At this time it is $I = (2.4\ \text{mA})(0.25) = 0.60\ \text{mA}$. The current has fallen to a quarter of its starting value, the same fraction as the missing charge.

>[!example] (Hard) Start and End of a Network
>An ideal $12\ \text{V}$ source has its $+$ terminal at $A$ and its $-$ terminal at $B$. A switch and a resistor $R_1 = 4.0\ \text{k}\Omega$ run in series from $A$ to point $P$. From $P$ to $B$ there are two branches. One is $R_2 = 8.0\ \text{k}\Omega$. The other is $R_3 = 4.0\ \text{k}\Omega$ from $P$ to point $D$, followed by an uncharged $50\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor from $D$ to $B$. The switch closes at $t = 0$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the source current and the current in each branch right after the switch closes. Then find them after a long time, together with the capacitor's final charge.
>>Right after closing, the uncharged capacitor acts as a wire. $R_3$ then runs from $P$ to $B$, in parallel with $R_2$:
>>$$R_{23} = \frac{(8.0)(4.0)}{8.0 + 4.0} = 2.67\ \text{k}\Omega.$$
>>This pair is in series with $R_1$, so the total is $6.67\ \text{k}\Omega$, and the source current is $12/6.67 = 1.80\ \text{mA}$. The pair has $(1.80\ \text{mA})(2.67\ \text{k}\Omega) = 4.8\ \text{V}$ across it. $R_2$ carries $4.8/8.0 = 0.60\ \text{mA}$, and $R_3$ carries $4.8/4.0 = 1.20\ \text{mA}$ onto the capacitor.
>>
>>After a long time, the capacitor acts as a gap, and $R_3$ carries no current. $R_1$ and $R_2$ are then in series, so the source current is $12/12 = 1.0\ \text{mA}$. $R_2$ has $(1.0\ \text{mA})(8.0\ \text{k}\Omega) = 8.0\ \text{V}$ across it. With no current in $R_3$, point $D$ is at the potential of $P$. The capacitor therefore has $8.0\ \text{V}$ and holds $(50\ \mu\text{F})(8.0\ \text{V}) = 400\ \mu\text{C}$. The source current falls from $1.80$ to $1.0\ \text{mA}$ as the capacitor charges.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not track where the source's energy goes while a capacitor charges. The next example combines the charging curve with the energy formulas of [[3.5.3. Energy Stored in an Electric Field]], as in Giancoli chapter 26.

>[!example] (Hard) Where the Energy Goes
>An ideal source of emf $\mathcal{E}$ charges an uncharged capacitor $C$ through a resistor $R$, until the charge is $C\mathcal{E}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the work the source does, the energy the capacitor stores, and the heat the resistor produces. Evaluate them for $\mathcal{E} = 12\ \text{V}$ and $C = 400\ \mu\text{F}$.
>>The source moves the charge $C\mathcal{E}$ from its $-$ terminal to its $+$ terminal. It does work $\mathcal{E}$ on each coulomb, so
>>$$W_{\text{source}} = \mathcal{E}\,(C\mathcal{E}) = C\mathcal{E}^2.$$
>>The capacitor ends with $U = \tfrac12 C\mathcal{E}^2$, half of that work. The resistor turns energy into heat at the rate $I^2R$. Integrating over the whole charging gives
>>$$\int_0^\infty I^2R\,dt = \frac{\mathcal{E}^2}{R}\int_0^\infty e^{-2t/RC}\,dt = \frac{\mathcal{E}^2}{R}\cdot\frac{RC}{2} = \frac{1}{2}C\mathcal{E}^2.$$
>>The integrand is the power in the resistor at time $t$. The heat equals the stored energy, and the two halves account for all of the source's work. The resistance cancels. A smaller $R$ charges the capacitor faster but still turns half of the work into heat.
>>
>>For $\mathcal{E} = 12\ \text{V}$ and $C = 400\ \mu\text{F}$, the source does $(4.0 \times 10^{-4})(144) = 0.0576\ \text{J}$ of work. The capacitor stores $0.0288\ \text{J}$, and the resistor produces $0.0288\ \text{J}$ of heat.
