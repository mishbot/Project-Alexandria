>[!note] Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)
>The **discrete Fourier transform** decomposes a finite-length time series $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ into a sum of complex sinusoids at $n$ equally spaced frequencies. Each coefficient $d(\omega_j)$ in the decomposition tells how strongly the data oscillates at the corresponding frequency, and is computed as the inner product of the data with the sinusoid:
>$$d(\omega_j) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{t=1}^{n} x_t\, e^{-2\pi i\, t \omega_j}, \qquad j = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1.$$
>The frequencies $\omega_j = j/n$ are called the **Fourier frequencies**: they are the only frequencies the DFT can resolve from $n$ equally spaced samples. Using Euler's formula $e^{-i\theta} = \cos\theta - i\sin\theta$, the same coefficient splits into a real and imaginary part:
>$$d(\omega_j) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigl(\sum_{t=1}^n x_t\cos(2\pi t\omega_j) \;-\; i\sum_{t=1}^n x_t\sin(2\pi t\omega_j)\Bigr),$$
>so each $d(\omega_j)$ encodes both the amplitude (through its modulus $|d(\omega_j)|$) and the phase (through its argument $\arg d(\omega_j)$) of the frequency-$\omega_j$ component. The squared modulus $|d(\omega_j)|^2$ keeps only the amplitude information and gives the [[2.3.2 Periodogram|periodogram]].
>
>For real-valued data, the coefficients satisfy the symmetry $d(\omega_j) = \overline{d(1 - \omega_j)}$, so $|d(\omega_j)|^2 = |d(1 - \omega_j)|^2$ and only the frequencies $\omega \in [0, 1/2]$ carry independent information; values above $1/2$ are mirror images of values below. The transform is also fully invertible: $x_t = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} d(\omega_j)\, e^{+2\pi i\, t \omega_j}$ recovers the original data exactly, so no information is lost in passing to the frequency domain.

# Intuition

The DFT projects a finite time series onto a basis of complex sinusoids and reads off how much of each one is present. Each coefficient $d(\omega_j)$ answers two questions about the frequency $\omega_j$ at once: *how much* of the data oscillates at this rate (the magnitude), and *how the oscillations are aligned in time* (the phase). Two signals that differ only by a time shift have identical magnitudes at every frequency but different phases, which is why the periodogram, built from magnitudes alone, suffices for power-spectrum analysis but not for reconstruction.

The restriction to the Fourier grid $\omega_j = j/n$ is a finite-sample artifact. With $n$ samples, the lowest non-zero frequency the DFT can see is $1/n$ (one full cycle across the entire record), and the highest is $1/2$ (alternating values $+1, -1, +1, \ldots$, the [[2.4 Time Series Regression|Nyquist limit]]). Frequencies between Fourier grid points are not directly observed and bleed into neighboring bins, the phenomenon known as *spectral leakage*. A pure sinusoid whose frequency happens to coincide with a Fourier grid point produces a single clean spike; one that falls between points spreads its energy across nearby bins instead.

# Properties

#### *(Linearity)* The DFT distributes over linear combinations.

For any series $x_t, y_t$ and scalars $a, b$, the DFT satisfies $\widehat{a x + b y} = a\,\hat x + b\,\hat y$. This follows directly from the linearity of the sum that defines $d(\omega_j)$ and means that decomposing a signal into a known component plus a residual decomposes the DFT additively. The same property is the technical reason behind tools like periodogram regression and the additive time-frequency decomposition.

#### *(Parseval's Identity)* Total energy is preserved between the time and frequency domains.

Squaring and summing on either side gives
$$\sum_{t=1}^n x_t^2 \;=\; \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} |d(\omega_j)|^2,$$
the discrete analogue of Plancherel's theorem. The total variance of the data equals the sum of squared moduli across all Fourier frequencies, which justifies interpreting $|d(\omega_j)|^2$ as the contribution of frequency $\omega_j$ to total power. The [[2.3.2 Periodogram|periodogram]] $P(\omega_j) = (4/n)|d(\omega_j)|^2$ is just a rescaled version of this energy decomposition.

#### *(Orthogonality of Sinusoids)* Cosines and sines at distinct Fourier frequencies are orthogonal under the discrete inner product.

For $j \neq k$ in $\{1, \ldots, \lfloor n/2 \rfloor\}$,
$$\sum_{t=1}^n \cos(2\pi t\omega_j)\cos(2\pi t\omega_k) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^n \sin(2\pi t\omega_j)\sin(2\pi t\omega_k) \;=\; 0,$$
and the cross-terms $\sum_t \cos(2\pi t\omega_j)\sin(2\pi t\omega_k)$ vanish for all $j, k$. The orthogonality is what makes the DFT coefficients independent: estimating the amplitude at one Fourier frequency does not affect the estimates at the others, mirroring the behavior of an orthogonal regression design with sinusoidal predictors.

#### *(Computational Cost)* The naive DFT is $O(n^2)$; the FFT brings it down to $O(n\log n)$.

A direct computation of $d(\omega_j)$ at all $n$ Fourier frequencies requires $n^2$ complex multiplications. The **Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)** algorithm exploits redundancies in the sinusoidal basis to compute the same set of coefficients in $O(n\log n)$ when $n$ is a power of two (and in close to that for general $n$). The speedup is what makes spectral analysis practical on long records: without it, a length-$10^6$ series would require $10^{12}$ multiplications instead of about $2 \times 10^7$. All standard implementations (NumPy `fft.fft`, R `fft`, MATLAB `fft`) use FFT internally.

# Examples

>[!example] DFT of a length-4 series
>Compute $d(\omega_j)$ for $x_1 = 2, x_2 = 3, x_3 = 1, x_4 = 4$.
>>[!example]- Solution
>>The Fourier frequencies are $\omega_j = 0, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4$.
>>
>>For $j = 0$: every exponential equals $1$, so $d(0) = \tfrac{1}{2}(2 + 3 + 1 + 4) = 5$, the rescaled sample mean.
>>
>>For $j = 1$: the exponential $e^{-i\pi t/2}$ takes values $-i, -1, i, 1$ for $t = 1, 2, 3, 4$, giving
>>$$d(1/4) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(2(-i) + 3(-1) + 1(i) + 4(1)\bigr) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - i) \;=\; 0.5 - 0.5i.$$
>>
>>For $j = 2$: $e^{-i\pi t}$ takes values $-1, 1, -1, 1$, so $d(1/2) = \tfrac{1}{2}(-2 + 3 - 1 + 4) = 2$.
>>
>>By the real-data symmetry, $d(3/4) = \overline{d(1/4)} = 0.5 + 0.5i$. The squared moduli are $|d(0)|^2 = 25$, $|d(1/4)|^2 = 0.5$, $|d(1/2)|^2 = 4$, with periodogram values $P(j/n) = (4/n)|d|^2 = 25, 0.5, 4$ at $j = 0, 1, 2$.

>[!example] DFT of a pure cosine
>For $x_t = \cos(2\pi \omega_0 t)$ with $\omega_0 = k/n$ on the Fourier grid, where does the energy concentrate?
>>[!example]- Solution
>>Using $\cos\theta = \tfrac12 (e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta})$,
>>$$d(\omega_j) \;=\; \frac{1}{2\sqrt n}\sum_{t=1}^n \bigl(e^{2\pi i (\omega_0 - \omega_j)t} + e^{-2\pi i(\omega_0 + \omega_j)t}\bigr).$$
>>By the orthogonality of sinusoids on the Fourier grid, the first sum equals $n$ when $\omega_j = \omega_0$ and zero otherwise; the second contributes only at $\omega_j = -\omega_0$, which on the Fourier grid maps to $1 - \omega_0$. So $d(\omega_j) = \sqrt{n}/2$ at $\omega_j \in \{\omega_0, 1-\omega_0\}$ and zero elsewhere: a clean two-spike signature, no leakage. If $\omega_0$ is *not* on the grid (say $\omega_0 = (k + 0.5)/n$), the energy spreads across neighboring bins instead, and the spike fades into a broader peak. That is leakage.
