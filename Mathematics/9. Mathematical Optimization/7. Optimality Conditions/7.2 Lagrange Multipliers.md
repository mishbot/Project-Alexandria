>[!note] Lagrange Multipliers
>The method of **Lagrange multipliers** finds the candidates for a maximum of $f(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{p})$ over $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ subject to $m$ equality constraints $h_1(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{p}) = 0, \dots, h_m(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{p}) = 0$, with $n > m$. The vector $\mathbf{p}$ holds parameters that the optimizer takes as given.
>
>The **Lagrange multiplier theorem** gives a necessary condition. Let $\mathbf{x}^* = \mathbf{x}^*(\mathbf{p})$ be a local solution. Assume $f$ and every $h_j$ are continuously differentiable near $\mathbf{x}^*$. Assume also that the $m \times n$ Jacobian $J$ of the constraints, with $\partial h_j(\mathbf{x}^*)/\partial x_i$ in row $j$ and column $i$, has maximal rank $m$. Then there are numbers $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m$, the Lagrange multipliers, such that $(\mathbf{x}^*, \boldsymbol{\lambda})$ is a critical point of the **Lagrangian**
>$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = f(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{p}) - \sum_{j=1}^m \lambda_j\, h_j(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{p}).$$
>The first-order conditions are
>$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x_i} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} - \sum_{j=1}^m \lambda_j \frac{\partial h_j}{\partial x_i} = 0 \quad (i = 1, \dots, n), \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \lambda_j} = -h_j = 0 \quad (j = 1, \dots, m).$$
>They are $n + m$ equations in the $n + m$ unknowns $(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\lambda})$. The parameters $\mathbf{p}$ are not chosen, so there is no first-order condition for them. A constraint written as $h(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{p}) = c$ enters the Lagrangian as $-\lambda\,[h(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{p}) - c]$. With $n = 2$ and $m = 1$, dividing the two conditions for $x_1$ and $x_2$ gives the tangency condition
>$$\frac{f'_{x_1}}{f'_{x_2}} = \frac{h'_{x_1}}{h'_{x_2}}.$$
>
>For $n = 2$ and $m = 1$, the **bordered Hessian** is the Hessian of $\mathcal{L}$ in the variables $(\lambda, x_1, x_2)$:
>$$\bar H = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -h'_{x_1} & -h'_{x_2} \\ -h'_{x_1} & \mathcal{L}''_{x_1x_1} & \mathcal{L}''_{x_1x_2} \\ -h'_{x_2} & \mathcal{L}''_{x_2x_1} & \mathcal{L}''_{x_2x_2}\end{pmatrix}.$$
>At a point that satisfies the first-order conditions, $\det \bar H > 0$ is sufficient for a strict local constrained maximum, and $\det \bar H < 0$ for a strict local constrained minimum. The multiplier $\lambda$ of a constraint $h(\mathbf{x}) = c$ is the rate at which the optimal value $F$ changes with the constant, $dF/dc = \lambda$. The proof is in [[7.3 Envelope Theorem]]. Inequality constraints are treated in [[4.2. Lagrangian Duality]].

>[!warning] Critical Point of the Lagrangian
>The lecture states that $(\mathbf{x}^*, \boldsymbol{\lambda})$ maximizes the Lagrangian, and it writes the sum as starting from $j = 0$. The sum starts at $j = 1$. The precise statement is that every partial derivative of $\mathcal{L}$ is zero at $(\mathbf{x}^*, \boldsymbol{\lambda})$. For $\max xy$ subject to $x + y = 2$, the solution is $(1, 1)$ with $\lambda = 1$. The Lagrangian $xy - (x + y - 2)$ has Hessian $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ in $(x, y)$, so $(1, 1)$ is a saddle point of $\mathcal{L}$ and not its maximum.

# Intuition

A constraint restricts the choice to a curve or a surface. When the constraint can be solved for one variable, substitution turns the problem into an unconstrained one. For example, $\max x^2 y$ subject to $x + y = 3$ becomes $\max x^2(3 - x)$ over $x$ alone. Many constraints cannot be solved this way. The constraint $x e^y + y e^x = 5$ has no closed-form solution for $y$, so substitution fails for $\max xy$ subject to it.

A graph shows what a solution looks like. Take $\max xy$ subject to $h(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - 1 = 0$. The level curves $xy = K$ are hyperbolas, and a larger $K$ lies further from the origin. A level curve that crosses the circle, such as $xy = 0.2$, cannot be optimal. Moving along the circle between the two crossing points reaches higher level curves. A level curve that misses the circle, such as $xy = 0.8$, is out of reach. The best feasible level curve, $xy = 0.5$, touches the circle at $(1/\sqrt{2}, 1/\sqrt{2})$ without crossing it. By symmetry $(-1/\sqrt{2}, -1/\sqrt{2})$ is also a maximum. At the touching point the two curves have the same slope. The Lagrange conditions state this tangency with derivatives.

In consumer theory $f$ is utility and the constraint is the budget line. Tangency then says that the MRS equals the price ratio, as in [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]]. The same method with the roles of objective and constraint swapped gives [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]]. In utility maximization the multiplier equals the marginal utility of income, a fact used in [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function]].

# Properties

#### *(Reading the Multiplier)* The product $\lambda\,dc$ approximates the change in the optimal value, and the sign of $\lambda$ depends on the Lagrangian convention.

With the minus sign in $\mathcal{L} = f - \lambda(h - c)$, a positive $\lambda$ means that raising $c$ raises the maximum. Writing $f + \lambda(h - c)$ instead flips the sign of every $\lambda$ and leaves $\mathbf{x}^*$ unchanged. The units of $\lambda$ are units of $f$ per unit of $c$. For a budget constraint $p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 = M$, $\lambda$ is utility per dollar. For a firm that minimizes cost subject to an output target, $\lambda$ is the extra cost of one more unit of output. The estimate $\lambda\,dc$ is first-order, so it is accurate only for small $dc$.

#### *(Lagrangian Curvature)* The lower-right block of the bordered Hessian holds second derivatives of $\mathcal{L}$, which include the curvature of the constraint.

Each entry is $\mathcal{L}''_{x_ix_j} = f''_{x_ix_j} - \lambda h''_{x_ix_j}$. For a linear constraint, such as a budget line, $h'' = 0$ and the block equals the Hessian of $f$. For a curved constraint the term $-\lambda h''$ matters. The function $x^2 - xy + y^2$ is convex, yet on the circle $x^2 + y^2 = p$ it has constrained maxima. Expanding the determinant along its first row gives a formula for quick use:
$$\det \bar H = -(h'_{x_1})^2\,\mathcal{L}''_{x_2x_2} + 2\,h'_{x_1}h'_{x_2}\,\mathcal{L}''_{x_1x_2} - (h'_{x_2})^2\,\mathcal{L}''_{x_1x_1}.$$
The signs in the border do not matter. Replacing $-h'$ by $h'$ multiplies one row and one column by $-1$, which leaves the determinant unchanged.

#### *(Rank Condition)* If the constraint gradient vanishes at the optimum, the Lagrange conditions can miss it.

Take $\max -x$ subject to $h(x, y) = x^3 - y^2 = 0$. Feasible points satisfy $x^3 = y^2 \geq 0$, so $x \geq 0$ and the maximum is at $(0, 0)$. There $\nabla h = (3x^2, -2y) = (0, 0)$, so the rank condition fails. The condition $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial x = -1 - 3\lambda x^2 = 0$ reads $-1 = 0$ at the origin. No multiplier exists, and a search that relies on the first-order conditions never finds the solution. Points where $\nabla h = \mathbf{0}$ must be checked separately. For a budget constraint this never happens, since $\nabla h = (p_1, p_2) \neq \mathbf{0}$.

#### *(Candidates, Then Classify)* A maximization and a minimization with the same constraint have the same first-order conditions.

Solving the conditions gives all constrained critical points, maxima and minima together, and the bordered Hessian classifies each one locally. When the problem is known to have a maximum, compare the values of $f$ at all candidates and at any points where the rank condition fails. The largest value is the global maximum. A minimization problem, such as cost minimization, is set up the same way, with the objective first and the constraint term subtracted. A candidate with $\det \bar H < 0$ is a strict local minimum.

# Derivation

We want to see where the Lagrange conditions come from, for $n = 2$ and $m = 1$. Take $\max f(x, y)$ subject to $h(x, y) = 0$. Assume $h$ is continuously differentiable with $h'_y \neq 0$ at the solution. By the [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]], the constraint defines $y = g(x)$ near the solution, with
$$g'(x) = -\frac{h'_x(x, g(x))}{h'_y(x, g(x))}.$$
Substitution turns the problem into the unconstrained problem $\max_x f(x, g(x))$. By the chain rule, its FOC is
$$f'_x(x, g(x)) + f'_y(x, g(x))\,g'(x) = 0.$$
Replacing $g'$ gives $f'_x - f'_y\,h'_x/h'_y = 0$. When $f'_y \neq 0$, this rearranges to the tangency condition $f'_x/f'_y = h'_x/h'_y$. Now define $\lambda = f'_y/h'_y$ at the solution. Then $f'_y = \lambda h'_y$ holds by definition, and the previous equation gives $f'_x = \lambda h'_x$. These are the conditions $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial x = 0$ and $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial y = 0$. The third condition, $h = 0$, is the constraint itself. The rank condition of the theorem is $\nabla h \neq \mathbf{0}$ here. It lets the implicit function theorem solve the constraint for one of the variables.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Fencing a Garden Against a Wall
>A gardener has $40$ meters of fence for a rectangular plot along a wall. The wall forms one side of the rectangle. The fence covers the opposite side, of length $x$, and the two sides of length $y$. The area is $xy$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the largest area with a Lagrangian. What does the multiplier say about a $41$st meter of fence?
>>The constraint is $x + 2y = 40$, so the Lagrangian is $\mathcal{L} = xy - \lambda(x + 2y - 40)$. The FOCs are
>>$$y - \lambda = 0, \qquad x - 2\lambda = 0, \qquad x + 2y - 40 = 0.$$
>>The first two give $x = 2y$. The constraint then gives $4y = 40$, so $y^* = 10$, $x^* = 20$, and $\lambda = 10$. The area is $200$ square meters. For the second-order check, $h'_x = 1$, $h'_y = 2$, $\mathcal{L}''_{xx} = \mathcal{L}''_{yy} = 0$, and $\mathcal{L}''_{xy} = 1$. Only the middle term of the determinant formula is nonzero, so $\det \bar H = 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 4 > 0$ and the point is a maximum.
>>
>>The multiplier predicts that one more meter of fence adds about $10$ square meters. With $c$ meters of fence the optimal area is $c^2/8$, whose derivative at $c = 40$ is $c/4 = 10$. With $41$ meters the area is $210.125$, a gain of $10.125$.

>[!example] (Medium) Cheapest Way to Make 20 Deliveries
>A delivery firm makes $q = L^{1/2}K^{1/2}$ deliveries per day from $L$ driver hours and $K$ van hours. A driver hour costs $16$ and a van hour costs $4$. The firm wants $20$ deliveries at the lowest cost. The problem has the same structure as [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization|expenditure minimization]].
>
>>[!example]- Find the cost-minimizing inputs, check the second-order condition, and interpret $\lambda$.
>>The problem is $\min 16L + 4K$ subject to $L^{1/2}K^{1/2} = 20$. With the same sign convention, $\mathcal{L} = 16L + 4K - \lambda(L^{1/2}K^{1/2} - 20)$. The FOCs are
>>$$16 = \frac{\lambda}{2}\left(\frac{K}{L}\right)^{1/2}, \qquad 4 = \frac{\lambda}{2}\left(\frac{L}{K}\right)^{1/2}, \qquad L^{1/2}K^{1/2} = 20.$$
>>Dividing the first by the second gives $K/L = 4$. The constraint then gives $2L = 20$, so $L^* = 10$ and $K^* = 40$. The cost is $160 + 160 = 320$. The first condition gives $\lambda = 32/(K/L)^{1/2} = 16$.
>>
>>For the second-order check, $h'_L = \frac{1}{2}(K/L)^{1/2} = 1$ and $h'_K = \frac{1}{2}(L/K)^{1/2} = \frac{1}{4}$. The cost is linear, so the second derivatives of $\mathcal{L}$ come only from $-\lambda h$. At the solution they are $\mathcal{L}''_{LL} = 0.8$, $\mathcal{L}''_{KK} = 0.05$, and $\mathcal{L}''_{LK} = -0.2$. The formula gives
>>$$\det \bar H = -(1)^2(0.05) + 2(1)\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)(-0.2) - \left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^2(0.8) = -0.2 < 0,$$
>>which confirms a constrained minimum.
>>
>>The multiplier is the cost of one more delivery. For this technology the minimum cost of $q$ deliveries is $16q$. A $21$st delivery therefore costs exactly $16 = \lambda$ more.

>[!example] (Hard) A Quadratic on a Circle
>Solve $\max x^2 - xy + y^2$ subject to $x^2 + y^2 = p$, where the parameter $p > 0$ is the squared radius of the circle.
>
>>[!example]- Find all candidates, decide which are maxima and which are minima, and compute the bordered Hessian at each.
>>The Lagrangian is $\mathcal{L} = x^2 - xy + y^2 - \lambda(x^2 + y^2 - p)$. The FOCs are
>>$$2x - y - 2\lambda x = 0, \qquad -x + 2y - 2\lambda y = 0, \qquad x^2 + y^2 = p.$$
>>The first two give $y = (2 - 2\lambda)x$ and $x = (2 - 2\lambda)y$. Combining them gives $x = (2 - 2\lambda)^2 x$. The case $x = 0$ forces $y = 0$, which violates the constraint. So $(2 - 2\lambda)^2 = 1$, and there are two values of $\lambda$.
>>
>>If $2 - 2\lambda = 1$, then $\lambda = 1/2$ and $y = x$. The constraint gives $x = \pm\sqrt{p/2}$. The candidates are $(\sqrt{p/2}, \sqrt{p/2})$ and $(-\sqrt{p/2}, -\sqrt{p/2})$, both with $f = p/2$.
>>
>>If $2 - 2\lambda = -1$, then $\lambda = 3/2$ and $y = -x$. The candidates are $(\sqrt{p/2}, -\sqrt{p/2})$ and $(-\sqrt{p/2}, \sqrt{p/2})$, both with $f = 3p/2$.
>>
>>For the bordered Hessian, $h'_x = 2x$, $h'_y = 2y$, $\mathcal{L}''_{xx} = \mathcal{L}''_{yy} = 2 - 2\lambda$, and $\mathcal{L}''_{xy} = -1$. Using $x^2 + y^2 = p$, the formula gives
>>$$\det \bar H = -4x^2(2 - 2\lambda) - 8xy - 4y^2(2 - 2\lambda) = -4p\,(2 - 2\lambda) - 8xy.$$
>>At the two points with $y = x$, $2 - 2\lambda = 1$ and $xy = p/2$, so $\det \bar H = -4p - 4p = -8p < 0$. Both are constrained minima. At the two points with $y = -x$, $2 - 2\lambda = -1$ and $xy = -p/2$, so $\det \bar H = 4p + 4p = 8p > 0$. Both are constrained maxima. The maximum value is $3p/2$ with $\lambda = 3/2$, and the minimum value is $p/2$ with $\lambda = 1/2$. [[7.3 Envelope Theorem]] checks that the derivative of each value with respect to $p$ equals its $\lambda$.
