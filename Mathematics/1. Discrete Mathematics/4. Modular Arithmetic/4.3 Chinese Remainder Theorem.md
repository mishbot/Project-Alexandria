>[!note] Chinese Remainder Theorem
>Given pair-wise **coprime moduli**  $n_{1},\dots ,n_{k}$ and their product $N=\prod_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}$, the **Chinese Remainder Theorem** states that there exists a **unique integer** $0 \leq x \leq N - 1$ that simultaneously satisfies $x\equiv a_{i}\pmod{n_{i}}$ for all $i=1, 2, \ldots, n$. 
>
>Moreover, writing $N_{i}=N/n_{i}$ and letting $N_{i}^{-1}$ denote the [[4.2 Modular Inverse|modular inverse]] of $N_{i} \pmod{n_{i}}$, the unique solution for $x$ can be explicitly constructed as
>$$x\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\,\bigl(N_{i}\,N_{i}^{-1}\bigr)\pmod{N},$$
>where $N_{i}N_{i}^{-1}$ are the **basis residues**, such that $N_{i}N_{i}^{-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{n_{i}}$ and $N_{i}N_{i}^{-1} \equiv 0 \pmod{n_{j}}$ for all $n_{j} \neq n_{i}$.

# Intuition
Although difficult in theory, the CRT is based on the following claim:
- For $m, n$ with $\gcd(m, n) = 1$ that there is exactly one $x \pmod{mn}$ that satisfies the equations $x \equiv a \pmod{n}$ and $x \equiv b \pmod{m}$.

The proof for this claim is as follows. Let $u \equiv m(m^{-1} \pmod{n})$ and $v \equiv n(n^{-1} \pmod{m})$. Then, $u \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$ and $u \equiv 0 \pmod{m}$, whereas $v \equiv 0 \pmod{n}$ and $v \equiv 1 \pmod{m}$. This means that $x = ua+vb \equiv (1)a+(0)b \equiv a \pmod{n}$, and similarly $x \equiv b \pmod{m}$, meaning we have an $x$ satisfying the equation.
# Examples
>[!example] How Many Solutions?
>Consider the equation $ax \equiv b \pmod{pq}$ for distinct primes $p, q$. In the below three parts, all values $a, b, x$ are defined as values in in the range $\{ 0, 1, \ldots, pq-1 \}$.
>>[!example]- If $\gcd(a, pq) = p$, show that there exists a solution if and only if $b = 0 \pmod{p}$.
>>We proceed in the following way:
>>- **Sufficiency ($\implies$):** Since $b = 0 \pmod{p}$, this means that $b$ is also a multiple of $p$. As such, we can express $\frac{a}{p}x=\frac{b}{p} + kq$, which reduces to $\frac{a}{p}x \equiv \frac{b}{p}+mpq \equiv \frac{b}{p} \pmod{q}$. This congruence has a solution as long as $\gcd(a/p, q)=1$ (i.e., $a/p$ is invertible). We know that this requirement is satisfied since $\gcd(a, pq)=p$, meaning that $p \mid a$, but $q \not\mid a$.
>>- **Necessity ($\impliedby$):** Let $ax=b+kpq$. Since $\gcd(a, pq)=p$, then $0 \equiv b + 0 \pmod{p}$.
>
>>[!example]- If $\gcd(a, pq) = p$, and there is a solution $x$, show that there are exactly $p$ solutions.

>
>>[!example]- For how many pairs $(a, b)$ are there exactly $p$ solutions?

Since $\gcd(a, pq) = p$, $a \equiv b \equiv 0$ as shown in the last step. We know that all solutions are of the form $\frac{a}{p}x=\frac{b}{p}+kq$. Therefore, it must be that $q \mid \left( \frac{a}{p}x-\frac{b}{p} \right)$. Since $\gcd(a, q)=1$, then $a/p$ is invertible, and we get $x \equiv (a/p)^{-1}b$. >[!example] Celebrate and Remember Textiles
>You’ve decided to knit a 70-themed baby blanket as a gift for your cousin and want to incorporate rows from three different stitch patterns with the following requirements on the row lengths of each of the stitch patterns:
>- Alternating Link: Multiple of 8, plus 3
>- Double Helix: Multiple of 3, plus 1
>- Crossover: Multiple of 7, plus 6
>
>You want to be able to switch between knitting these different patterns without changing the number of stitches on the needle, so you must use a number of stitches that simultaneously meets the requirements of all three patterns.
>
>>[!example]- Find the smallest number of stitches you need to cast on in order to incorporate all three patterns in your baby blanket.
>>We must solve using CRT for the following system of equations:
>>$$\begin{cases}
x \equiv 3 \pmod{8}, \\
x \equiv 1 \pmod{3}, \\
x \equiv 6 \pmod{7}.
\end{cases}$$
>>We construct $N=168$, and therefore $N_{1}=21$, $N_{2}=56$, $N_{3}=24$. A quick application of EGCD finds that the inverses are $N_{1}^{-1}=5$, $N_{2}^{-1}=2$, and $N_{3}^{-1}=5$. Therefore, $$x \equiv 3 \cdot (21 \cdot 5) + 1 \cdot (56 \cdot 2) + 6 \cdot (24 \cdot 5) \pmod{168},$$which simplifies to $x \equiv 139 \pmod{168}$.
