>[!note] Indirect Utility Function
>The **indirect utility function** gives the highest utility a consumer can reach at prices $\mathbf{p} = (p_1, p_2)$ and income $M$:
>$$v(\mathbf{p}, M) = u\big(x_1^*(\mathbf{p}, M),\, x_2^*(\mathbf{p}, M)\big) = \max_{x_1, x_2 \ge 0}\ \{u(x_1, x_2) : p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 \le M\}.$$
>Here $x_i^*$ is the [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|Marshallian demand]]. The function measures well-being through the prices and income the consumer faces, without naming the bundle. The [[7.3 Envelope Theorem|envelope theorem]] applied to the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = u - \lambda(p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 - M)$ gives
>$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial M} = \lambda > 0, \qquad \frac{\partial v}{\partial p_i} = -\lambda\, x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M) \le 0 ,$$
>where $\lambda = u'_{x_i}/p_i$ is the multiplier at the optimum. By the first result, $\lambda$ is the **marginal utility of income**, the extra utility from one more dollar. By the second, a price rise hurts in proportion to the quantity bought. So $v$ is increasing in $M$ and decreasing in each $p_i$, strictly whenever $x_i^* > 0$. It is homogeneous of degree zero, $v(t\mathbf{p}, tM) = v(\mathbf{p}, M)$ for $t > 0$, because scaling all prices and income leaves the [[6.2.1 Budget Constraint|budget set]] unchanged. The same argument makes demand homogeneous of degree zero, as in [[6.3.2 Homogeneity of Demand]].
>
>We can also go in the other direction. Dividing the second result by the first cancels $\lambda$ and recovers the Marshallian demand from the indirect utility function:
>$$x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M) = -\frac{\partial v/\partial p_i}{\partial v/\partial M}.$$
>This is **Roy's identity**, which the lecture did not state. Shephard's lemma, $h_i = \partial e/\partial p_i$ in [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]], does the same for the expenditure function and Hicksian demand.
>
>For Cobb-Douglas $u = x_1^{\alpha}x_2^{1-\alpha}$, with $x_1^* = \alpha M/p_1$ and $x_2^* = (1-\alpha)M/p_2$,
>$$v(\mathbf{p}, M) = \frac{\alpha^{\alpha}(1-\alpha)^{1-\alpha}}{p_1^{\alpha}\,p_2^{1-\alpha}}\, M, \qquad \lambda = \frac{\alpha^{\alpha}(1-\alpha)^{1-\alpha}}{p_1^{\alpha}\,p_2^{1-\alpha}} .$$
>The values of $v$ and $\lambda$ depend on which utility function represents the preferences. The sign of $\lambda$, the direction in which $v$ moves, and the demand do not. The reverse question, the least income that reaches a given utility, is [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization|expenditure minimization]].

# Intuition

Two consumers with the same tastes face different prices and incomes. We can compare their indirect utilities to see which one is better off, without computing either bundle. The indirect utility function is the value of the consumer's optimization problem. Maximum profit plays the same role for a firm.

The diagram shows a rise in the price of good $1$. The budget line tilts inward around its $x_2$ intercept, and the best reachable indifference curve moves down from $A$ to $A'$. The consumer's utility falls from $v(p_1, p_2, M)$ to $v(p_1', p_2, M)$. A rise in income would do the opposite and move the consumer to a higher curve.

When income or a price changes, the consumer reoptimizes and the whole bundle moves. By the envelope theorem, the reallocation does not matter to first order. At the optimum every good returns $\lambda$ utility per dollar, so shifting money between goods neither gains nor loses. Only the direct effect on the budget counts.

# Properties

#### *(Chain Rule View)* The envelope results follow from the FOCs and the budget identity, without the envelope theorem.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture derived these results only with the envelope theorem, as in the Derivation below.

Differentiate $v = u(x_1^*, x_2^*)$ with respect to $M$ and use $u'_{x_i} = \lambda p_i$:
$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial M} = u'_{x_1}\frac{\partial x_1^*}{\partial M} + u'_{x_2}\frac{\partial x_2^*}{\partial M} = \lambda\left(p_1\frac{\partial x_1^*}{\partial M} + p_2\frac{\partial x_2^*}{\partial M}\right).$$
The budget identity $p_1x_1^* + p_2x_2^* = M$ holds at every $M$, so its derivative gives $p_1\,\partial x_1^*/\partial M + p_2\,\partial x_2^*/\partial M = 1$. Hence $\partial v/\partial M = \lambda$. For a price, the same steps give $\lambda(p_1\,\partial x_1^*/\partial p_1 + p_2\,\partial x_2^*/\partial p_1)$. Differentiating the budget identity with respect to $p_1$ gives $x_1^* + p_1\,\partial x_1^*/\partial p_1 + p_2\,\partial x_2^*/\partial p_1 = 0$, so the bracket equals $-x_1^*$. The budget absorbs every change in the bundle, and each dollar of that change is worth $\lambda$.

#### *(Dollar Cost of a Price Rise)* A small rise $dp_i$ hurts as much as losing $x_i^*\,dp_i$ dollars of income.

A price rise lowers utility by $\lambda x_i^*\,dp_i$. A loss of income $dM$ lowers utility by $\lambda\, dM$. The two are equal when $dM = x_i^*\,dp_i$, the extra cost of the old bundle. The dollar figure $x_i^*\,dp_i$ does not depend on the utility function chosen, so every representation of the preferences gives the same dollar figure. For a large price rise the true dollar cost is smaller, because the consumer buys less of the good that became expensive. The Medium example below measures the gap. The term $x_i^*\,\partial x_i^*/\partial M$ in the [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]] comes from this same loss of purchasing power.

>[!warning] The Size of $\lambda$ Depends on the Utility Function
>Utility is ordinal. If $w = f(u)$ with $f' > 0$, then $w$ represents the same preferences and gives the same demand. Its indirect utility is $f(v)$ and its multiplier is $f'(v)\,\lambda$. So the number $\lambda$ can be made as large or as small as we like. It cannot compare how much a dollar matters to two different people. Its sign is positive in every representation, so "more income is better" and "higher prices are worse" hold for all of them.

#### *(Why Roy's Identity Works)* The ratio converts the utility cost of a price rise into dollars, and that dollar cost is the quantity bought.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture derived $\partial v/\partial M = \lambda$ and $\partial v/\partial p_i = -\lambda x_i^*$ but did not divide them.

The numerator $-\partial v/\partial p_i$ is the utility lost per dollar of price increase. The denominator $\partial v/\partial M$ is the utility gained per dollar of income. Their ratio is the income that makes up for a one-dollar price rise. By the Dollar Cost property above, that income is $x_i^*$, the extra cost of the current bundle. The multiplier $\lambda$ cancels, so Roy's identity gives the same demand for every utility function that represents the preferences. For Cobb-Douglas, $v = kM/(p_1^{\alpha}p_2^{1-\alpha})$ with a constant $k$, so $\partial v/\partial p_1 = -\alpha v/p_1$ and $\partial v/\partial M = v/M$. The ratio gives $x_1^* = \alpha M/p_1$, the demand we started from. For roommate A in the Hard example below, $v_A = M^2/(4p_1p_2)$ gives $\partial v_A/\partial p_1 = -M^2/(4p_1^2p_2)$ and $\partial v_A/\partial M = M/(2p_1p_2)$, so $x_1^* = M/(2p_1) = 10$ slices.

#### *(Corners)* A rise in the price of a good the consumer does not buy leaves utility unchanged to first order.

At a corner with $x_i^* = 0$ the formula gives $\partial v/\partial p_i = -\lambda \cdot 0 = 0$. The consumer does not buy the good, so a small rise in its price costs nothing. Hence $v$ is weakly decreasing in each price, and strictly decreasing only in the prices of goods the consumer buys. A fall in that price can still help, because it may make the good worth buying. In $M$, $v$ is strictly increasing whenever the consumer has positive marginal utility for some good, since $\lambda > 0$.

>[!warning] Lecture Wording: Always Decreasing
>The lecture states that indirect utility is "always decreasing in the price $p_i$". This is exact when $x_i^* > 0$. At a corner with $x_i^* = 0$ the derivative is zero, so the precise statement is that $v$ never increases with $p_i$.

# Derivation

We want $\partial v/\partial M$ and $\partial v/\partial p_i$. The constrained [[7.3 Envelope Theorem|envelope theorem]] covers a value function $F(\mathbf{a}) = \max f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{a})$ subject to $h(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{a}) = 0$ with multiplier $\lambda$. At the optimum it gives
$$\frac{dF}{da_k} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial a_k} - \lambda\,\frac{\partial h}{\partial a_k},$$
with every derivative evaluated at $\mathbf{x}^*(\mathbf{a})$. In utility maximization $F = v$, $f = u(x_1, x_2)$, and $h = p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 - M$. The parameters are $p_1$, $p_2$, and $M$. The utility $u$ does not contain any of them, so $\partial f/\partial a_k = 0$. The constraint has $\partial h/\partial M = -1$ and $\partial h/\partial p_i = x_i$. Therefore
$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial M} = 0 - \lambda(-1) = \lambda, \qquad \frac{\partial v}{\partial p_i} = 0 - \lambda\, x_i^* = -\lambda\, x_i^* .$$
The sign of $\lambda$ comes from the FOC $u'_{x_i} = \lambda p_i$. With $u'_{x_i} > 0$ and $p_i > 0$, $\lambda > 0$, which gives the signs in the callout.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Dividing the second result by the first gives Roy's identity from the callout. The lecture stopped at the two results.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Checking the Marginal Utility of Income
>A student has $M = \$80$ a week for coffee at $p_1 = \$2$ a cup and bagels at $p_2 = \$3$ each. The utility is $u = x_1^{1/4}x_2^{3/4}$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute $v(\mathbf{p}, M)$ and $\lambda$, and check that one more dollar raises utility by $\lambda$.
>>Demand is $x_1^* = \frac{1}{4}\cdot 80/2 = 10$ cups and $x_2^* = \frac{3}{4}\cdot 80/3 = 20$ bagels. So $v = 10^{1/4}\cdot 20^{3/4} \approx 16.82$. The multiplier is
>>$$\lambda = \frac{u'_{x_1}}{p_1} = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{20}{10}\right)^{3/4} \approx 0.2102 .$$
>>The formula $v = \lambda M$ for Cobb-Douglas gives $0.2102 \cdot 80 \approx 16.82$, which agrees. With $M = 81$ the same prices give $v \approx 17.03$, an increase of $0.2102 = \lambda$. The match is exact here because Cobb-Douglas $v$ is linear in $M$. For other utility functions it holds for small changes.

>[!example] (Medium) The Cost of a Price Rise
>The same student faces a rise in the coffee price from $\$2$ to $\$2.50$. Income and the bagel price do not change.
>
>>[!example]- Estimate the loss with $\partial v/\partial p_1$, compute the exact loss, and state both in dollars.
>>The first-order estimate is $\lambda x_1^*\,\Delta p_1 = 0.2102 \cdot 10 \cdot 0.5 \approx 1.05$ units of utility. In dollars it equals $x_1^*\,\Delta p_1 = \$5$, the extra cost of the old $10$ cups.
>>
>>The exact new utility is $v = \frac{(1/4)^{1/4}(3/4)^{3/4}}{2.5^{1/4}\, 3^{3/4}}\cdot 80 \approx 15.91$, a loss of about $0.91$. The student now buys $x_1^* = 20/2.5 = 8$ cups. To get back to $16.82$ at the new prices the student needs $M' = 80\cdot(2.5/2)^{1/4} \approx \$84.59$, that is $\$4.59$ more. The estimate of $\$5$ is too high because it keeps the quantity of coffee fixed. The student cuts coffee from $10$ to $8$ cups and avoids part of the price rise.

>[!example] (Hard) Two Roommates With the Same Tastes
>Roommates A and B have the same preferences over pizza slices ($x_1$, $p_1 = \$2$) and drinks ($x_2$, $p_2 = \$4$). Each has $M = \$40$. Roommate A writes the utility as $u_A = x_1x_2$ and roommate B as $u_B = \sqrt{x_1x_2}$. Roommate A computes a much larger $\lambda$ and concludes that an extra dollar matters more to A.
>
>>[!example]- Compute demand, $v$, and $\lambda$ for both, and evaluate the claim.
>>Both functions give the same MRS, $-x_2/x_1$, so both roommates buy $x_1^* = 40/(2\cdot 2) = 10$ slices and $x_2^* = 40/(2\cdot 4) = 5$ drinks.
>>
>>For A, $v_A = 50$ and $\lambda_A = u'_{x_1}/p_1 = x_2/p_1 = 2.5$. For B, $v_B = \sqrt{50} \approx 7.07$ and $\lambda_B = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{x_2/x_1}/p_1 \approx 0.177$. The ratio $\lambda_A/\lambda_B \approx 14.1$ equals $f'(v_B) = 2v_B$ for $f(s) = s^2$, since $u_A = u_B^2$. The claim is wrong. The two roommates have identical preferences and make identical choices, and the gap in $\lambda$ comes only from how each wrote down utility.
>>
>>The envelope results hold in each representation. For A, $v_A = M^2/(4p_1p_2)$, so $\partial v_A/\partial p_1 = -M^2/(4p_1^2p_2) = -25$, and $-\lambda_A x_1^* = -2.5\cdot 10 = -25$. Both roommates agree on every sign. More income helps, and higher prices hurt.
