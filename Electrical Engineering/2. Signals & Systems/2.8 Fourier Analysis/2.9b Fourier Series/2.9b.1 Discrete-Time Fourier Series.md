>[!note] Discrete-Time Fourier Series (DTFS)
>The discrete-time Fourier series is a Fourier transform method that provides a frequency-domain representation of a discrete-time signal defined over all integers. It results in a continuous frequency spectrum. Any $p$-periodic discrete-time signal $x: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{C}$ can be written in the frequency domain, such that  
>$$x(n)=\sum^{p-1}_{k=0}X_{k}e^{j\omega_0kn} \iff X_{k}=\frac{1}{p}\sum^{p-1}_{n=0}x(n)e^{-j\omega_0kn}.$$
>
>The synthesis (left) equation can be transformed into matrix-vector form, such that the formula for the DTFS of a $p$-periodic discrete signal $x(n)$ with eigenvector components $\psi_{k}(n)=e^{j\omega kn}$ is
>$$
>\vec{x} = \Psi\vec{X} \iff
>\begin{bmatrix}x(0) \\ x(1) \\ \vdots \\ x(p-1)\end{bmatrix} =
>\begin{bmatrix}
>\psi_0(0) & \ldots & \psi_k(0) & \ldots & \psi_{p-1}(0) \\ 
>\psi_0(1) & \ddots & \psi_k(1) & \ddots & \psi_{p-1}(1) \\
>\vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 
>\psi_0(p-1) & \ldots & \psi_k(p-1) & \ldots & \psi_{p-1}(p-1)
>\end{bmatrix}
>\begin{bmatrix}X_0 \\ \vdots \\ X_k \\ \vdots \\ X_{p-1}\end{bmatrix}
>$$
>where $\Psi \in \mathbb{C}^{p \times p}$ is an orthogonal matrix called the **Fourier kernel** representing a [[2.8b Change of Basis|Fourier basis]] with each of its columns $\Psi_k$ being the basis vectors, $\vec{X} \in \mathbb{C}^p$ is the vector of Fourier coefficients, and $\vec{x} \in \mathbb{C}^p$ is the signal output for each $n \in \set{0, 1, \ldots, (p-1)}$.
>
>Then, the solution to the discrete-time Fourier series for $x(n)$ is the projection
>$$\vec{X}(\omega)=\frac{1}{p}\Psi\textasteriskcentered\vec{x},$$
>where $\frac{1}{p}\Psi\textasteriskcentered = \Psi^{-1}$ is known as the **Fourier matrix**.
# Intuition
We start with a very simple change of basis. Consider two discrete functions $\psi_0(n)=1$ and $\psi_1(n)=\begin{cases}1 && (n \mod{2} \equiv 0) \\ -1 && (n \mod{2} \equiv 1)\end{cases}$. 

The functions can equivalently be written as $\psi_0(n) = e^{i0n}$ and $\psi_1(n)=e^{i \pi n}$, which you can find for yourself by solving the complex exponential for the given frequency with any $n \in \mathbb{Z}$. The two functions can also be written as two vectors $\psi_0 = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ and $\psi_1 = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$.

Since the fundamental period of the function is $p=2$ (you can find this using periodicity of discrete signals), this means that any two-periodic signal $x(n)$ can be decomposed as
$$\begin{align}
x(n) &= X_0\psi_0(n) + X_1\psi_1(n) \\
&= X_0e^{i0n}+X_1e^{i \pi n}.
\end{align}$$
Additionally, since $\omega_0=\frac{2\pi}{p}=\pi$, only the frequencies $0, \pi$ may contribute to the signal. This can be generalized for a $P$-periodic signal $x(n)$, such that the only frequencies that contribute to that signal are contained in the set $\set{0, \omega_0, 2\omega_0, \ldots, (P-1)\omega_0}$.

Proceeding in the 2-period realm, we can find $X_0$ and $X_1$ by solving for their respective vectors' dot products,
$$\begin{align}
\vec{x} \cdot \psi_0 = X_0(\psi_0 \cdot \psi_0) + X_1 \times 0 \iff X_0=\frac{\vec{x} \cdot \psi_0}{\psi_0 \cdot \psi_0} \\

\vec{x} \cdot \psi_1 = X_0 \times 0 +X_1(\psi_1 \cdot \psi_1) \iff X_1=\frac{\vec{x} \cdot \psi_1}{\psi_1 \cdot \psi_1}
\end{align}$$
where $\psi_0 \cdot \psi_1 = 0$ is automatically omitted since the two vectors are orthogonal, and $x$ is the vector of the signal $x(n)$. Note that this was a very simplified example, since ==a 2-period signal is always real== (the sine component of the complex number is always 0). 

We can now continue our $p$-periodic signal generalization by saying that a $p$-periodic signal can be generalized into a $p$-element vector, such that $\vec{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x(0) & x(1) & \ldots & x(p-1)\end{bmatrix}^T$. We will also define a $p$-dimensional orthogonal basis $\set{\psi_0, \psi_1, \ldots, \psi_{p-1}}$. The vector $\vec{x}$ can then be decomposed using
$$\begin{align}
\vec{x} &= X_0\psi_0+X_1\psi_1+\ldots+X_{p-1}\psi_{p-1} \\
&= \sum^{p-1}_{k=0}X_k\psi_k \\
&= \sum^{p-1}_{k=0}X_ke^{jk\omega_0n}
\end{align}$$
Then, we can find any $X_n$ by projecting $\vec{x}$ onto the coefficient's respective basis vector $\psi_n$:
$$
\begin{align}
\langle \vec{x}, \psi_n \rangle &= \langle \sum^{p-1}_{k=0}X_k\psi_k, \psi_n \rangle  \\
&= \sum^{p-1}_{k=0}X_k\langle \psi_k, \psi_n \rangle \\
&= X_n\langle \psi_n, \psi_n \rangle \\
&= X_n \times p.
\end{align}
$$
Here, we use the fact that the inner product of two orthogonal vectors is always $0$, as well as the fact that $\langle \vec{x}, \vec{x} \rangle = p$. The latter comes from the relation of the basis vectors to the periodicity of $x(n)$.

Finally, by dividing both sides by $\langle \psi_n, \psi_n \rangle = p$ we can define any Fourier series coefficient $X_n$ as
$$X_n = \frac{1}{p} \langle \vec{x}, \psi_n \rangle.$$
In the signal realm, we can equivalently state that for $\omega_0 \in \set{0, \omega_0, 2\omega_0, \ldots, (p-1)\omega_0}$,
$$x(n)=\sum^{p-1}_{k=0}X_k\psi_k(n)=\sum^{p-1}_{k=0}X_ke^{jk\omega_0n} \iff X_k=\frac{1}{p}\sum^{p-1}_{k=0}x(n)(e^{jk\omega_0n})\textasteriskcentered =\frac{1}{p}\sum^{p-1}_{k=0}x(n)e^{-jk\omega_0n}$$
where in the second equation $(e^{jk\omega_0n})\textasteriskcentered = e^{-jk\omega_0n}$.

Therefore, we get our discrete-time Fourier series for $p$-periodic signals with frequency $\omega_0 \in \set{0, \omega_0, 2\omega_0, \ldots, (p-1)\omega_0}$,
$$x(n)=\sum^{p-1}_{k=0}X_ke^{jk\omega_0n} \iff X_k=\frac{1}{p}\sum^{p-1}_{k=0}x(n)e^{-jk\omega_0n}.$$

Let's try to solve this for a $3$-period signal. We have $p=3$, and therefore an orthogonal basis of $\set{\psi_0, \psi_1, \psi_2}$. Let's define our basis vectors to follow the rule $\psi_k(n) = e^{jk\omega_0n}$. Since $\psi_k = \begin{bmatrix}\psi_k(0) & \psi_k(1) & \psi_k(2)\end{bmatrix}^T$ and $\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{p} = \frac{2\pi}{3}$, then
$$\begin{align}
\psi_0 &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}^T \\
\psi_1 &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & e^{j\frac{2\pi}{3}} & e^{j\frac{4\pi}{3}}\end{bmatrix}^T \\
\psi_2 &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & e^{j\frac{4\pi}{3}} & e^{j\frac{8\pi}{3}}\end{bmatrix}^T = \begin{bmatrix}1 & e^{j\frac{4\pi}{3}} & e^{j\frac{2\pi}{3}}\end{bmatrix}^T.
\end{align}$$

>[!example] Discrete-Time Fourier Series by Inspection
>Consider the $p$-periodic discrete signal
>$$x(n)=1+\sin(\frac{2\pi}{p}n)+3\cos(\frac{2\pi}{p}n)+\cos(\frac{2\pi}{p}n+\frac{\pi}{2}).$$
>As a sum of complex exponentials, we can equivalently express it as
>$$x(n)=e^{j\frac{2\pi}{p}0}+\frac{1}{2j}(e^{j\frac{2\pi}{p}n}-e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{p}n})+\frac{3}{2}(e^{j\frac{2\pi}{p}n} + e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{p}n}) + \frac{1}{2}(e^{j\frac{\pi}{2}}e^{j\frac{4\pi}{p}n} - e^{-j\frac{\pi}{2}}e^{-j\frac{4\pi}{p}n}).$$
>Using the Fourier series decomposition $x(n)=\sum^{p-1}_{k=0}X_k\psi_k(n)$, we can reorder the complex exponentials by matching their frequencies, such that
>$$x(n) = 
>\underbrace{(1)}_{X_0}e^{j\frac{2\pi}{p}0} +
>\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{2j} + \frac{3}{2} \right)}_{X_1}e^{j\frac{2\pi}{p}n} +
>\underbrace{\left(\frac{3}{2} - \frac{1}{2j} \right)}_{X_{-1}}e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{p}n} +
>\underbrace{\frac{j}{2}}_{X_2}e^{j\frac{4\pi}{p}n} +
>\underbrace{-\frac{j}{2}}_{X_{-2}}e^{-j\frac{4\pi}{p}n}
>$$
>The coefficients $X_{-1}$ and $X_{-2}$ come from seeing that $-1\omega_0=-\frac{2\pi}{p}$, and $-2\omega_0=-\frac{4\pi}{p}$ for $k\omega_0$. From the above, we can infer that $X_{-1}=X_{p-1}$ and $X_{-2}=X_{p-2}$, since we are trying to keep $k \in \set{0, 1, \ldots, (p-1)}$. Therefore, we have 5 coefficients: $X_0, X_1, X_2, X_{p-2}, X_{p-1}$.

>[!example] A DTFS Puzzle
>Consider a signal $x: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{R}$ with the following properties:
>1. $x(n+4\ell)=x(n),$ $\forall\ell,n \in \mathbb{Z}.$
>2. $\sum^{2}_{n=-1}x(n)=4.$
>3. $\sum^{2}_{n=-1}x(n)(-1)^n=8.$
>4. $\sum^{2}_{n=-1}x(n)\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}n)=\sum^{2}_{n=-1}x(n)\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}n)=0.$
>
>==(1)== We begin with the first equation, which implies that $n$ is periodic with period $p=\frac{4}{k}$ and fundamental frequency $\omega_{0} = \frac{2\pi}{p}=\frac{k\pi}{2}$. It also implies that we should be looking for 4 Fourier coefficients: $X_{-1}, X_{0}, X_{1}, \text{ and }X_{2}$.
>
>==(2)== Meanwhile, the second equation can be manipulated into 
>$$\sum^{2}_{n=-1}x(n) \cdot 1= \sum^{2}_{n=-1}x(n) e^{j 0 n} = 4.$$
>Dividing the rightmost side by $p$ (assuming $k=1$ such that $\frac{p}{k}=4$), we get 
>$$\frac{1}{4}\sum^{2}_{n=-1}x(n) e^{j 0 n} = \frac{1}{4} \cdot 4 = 1.$$
>Therefore, $X_{0} = 1$.
>
>==(3)== To proceed, we observe that equation 3 can also be manipulated, such that
>$$\sum^{2}_{n=-1}x(n)(-1)^n=\sum^{2}_{n=-1}x(n)e^{j\pi n}=8.$$
>This implies that $k\omega_{0}=\pi$, or equivalently, $k=2$. As such,
>$$X_{2} = \frac{1}{4}\sum^{2}_{n=-1}x(n)e^{j\pi n}=\frac{1}{4} \cdot 8.$$
>Therefore, $X_{2}=2$.
>
>==(4)== What does the fourth equation entail? We can proceed by manipulating it just like the previous two, such that
>$$\frac{1}{4}\sum^{2}_{k=-1}x(n)\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}n) = \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{1}{2}\sum^{2}_{k=-1}[x(n)e^{j \frac{\pi}{2} n} + x(n)e^{-j \frac{\pi}{2}n}] = 0.$$
>We observe that the right side can be simplified into $\frac{1}{2}X_{1}+\frac{1}{2}X_{-1}=0$, or equivalently $X_{1}=-X_{-1}$.
>
>Similarly,
>$$\frac{1}{4}\sum^{2}_{k=-1}x(n)\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}n) = \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{1}{2j}\sum^{2}_{k=-1}[x(n)e^{j \frac{\pi}{2} n} - x(n)e^{-j \frac{\pi}{2}n}] = 0,$$
>which can be simplified into $\frac{1}{2j}X_{1}-\frac{1}{2j}X_{-1}=0$, or $X_{1}=X_{-1}$. Therefore, $X_{1}$ and $X_{-1}$ need to meet the conditions
>$$X_{1}=-X_{-1} \text{ and } X_{1} = X_{-1},$$
>which is only true for $X_{1}=X_{-1}=0$.
>
>We found the coefficients to be $X_{-1}=0$, $X_{0}=1$, $X_{1}=0$, $X_{2}=2$. We can now find $x(n)$ accordingly:
>$$\begin{align}
>x(n) &= X_{0}e^{j\frac{\pi}{2}0n}+X_{1}e^{j\frac{\pi}{2}2n} \\
>&= 1 + 2e^{j\pi n}.
>\end{align}$$
