>[!note] Thermal Radiation
>Every object with a temperature emits electromagnetic waves, and **thermal radiation** is heat transfer by those waves. It needs no material in between, which is how the sun heats the earth across empty space. An object of surface area $A$ at absolute temperature $T$ radiates energy at the rate
>$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = \varepsilon\, \sigma\, A\, T^4,$$
>the **Stefan-Boltzmann law**, with the constant
>$$\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W}/(\text{m}^2 \cdot \text{K}^4).$$
>The temperature must be in kelvins, because the law involves $T$ itself and not a difference. The **emissivity** $\varepsilon$ is a number between $0$ and $1$ that describes the surface. It is also the fraction of incoming radiation that the surface absorbs rather than reflects, because in equilibrium a surface must emit exactly what it absorbs. A surface with $\varepsilon = 1$ absorbs everything that lands on it and is called a **blackbody**, whatever its color to the eye. Shiny metal has $\varepsilon$ near $0.1$, and skin, paint, and most rough surfaces have $\varepsilon$ near $0.9$ or above at the wavelengths they emit.
>
>Radiation that lands on a surface is split by $\varepsilon$. Of an incoming power $P_{\text{inc}}$, the surface absorbs $\varepsilon P_{\text{inc}}$ and reflects $(1 - \varepsilon) P_{\text{inc}}$. Sunlight of intensity $I$, in watts per square meter, falls straight onto an area $A$. It delivers $P_{\text{inc}} = IA$, so the surface absorbs
>$$P_{\text{abs}} = \varepsilon I A.$$
>Here $A$ is only the area facing the sun, while emission uses the whole surface. Problems often write the solar intensity as $H$, the solar constant.
>
>An object also absorbs the radiation from its surroundings. If the surroundings are at $T_{\text{amb}}$, it absorbs $\varepsilon \sigma A T_{\text{amb}}^4$ from them. The net rate at which the object loses energy by radiation is
>$$\frac{dQ}{dt}\bigg|_{\text{net}} = \varepsilon\, \sigma\, A \left( T^4 - T_{\text{amb}}^4 \right),$$
>positive when the object is hotter than its surroundings and negative when it is colder. An object at the ambient temperature radiates as much as it absorbs and its temperature holds steady. The wavelengths emitted depend on temperature. Objects near room temperature radiate in the infrared, which is invisible. A heating coil at several hundred degrees glows dull red, and the sun at $5800\ \text{K}$ radiates mostly visible light.

# Intuition

A hot coil in a lamp lights a match held a meter away without touching it and without warming the air much. The energy arrives as light, mostly infrared, focused by a lens the way sunlight is focused by a magnifying glass. The coil is not special. Everything in a room, including its occupants, radiates in the same way, only at longer wavelengths because it is cooler. An infrared camera shows this directly. People glow, cold windows look dark, and a thin plastic bag that blocks visible light is transparent in the infrared, so the warm hand behind it shows through. The image uses false color: nothing is glowing red, the camera has painted the strongest infrared red.

The diagram explains the emissivity. Put an object in a room and let everything settle to one temperature. The object keeps radiating, so it would cool to absolute zero unless something fed energy back. The walls radiate too, and the object absorbs part of what reaches it. In equilibrium the two rates must match exactly. If the object absorbed a larger fraction than it emitted, it would warm without limit in a room at a fixed temperature, which never happens. So one number $\varepsilon$ governs both, and a good absorber is a good emitter. In symbols, let $a$ be the fraction the object absorbs. The walls send $\sigma A T_{\text{amb}}^4$ onto it, so at $T = T_{\text{amb}}$ the balance reads
$$a\,\sigma A T_{\text{amb}}^4 = \varepsilon\,\sigma A T_{\text{amb}}^4, \qquad a = \varepsilon.$$

# Properties

#### *(Fourth Power)* Doubling the absolute temperature multiplies the radiated power by sixteen.

Radiation is weak near room temperature and dominant at high temperature. A body at $600\ \text{K}$ radiates $16$ times as much per unit area as one at $300\ \text{K}$, and a filament at $3000\ \text{K}$ radiates $10^{4}$ times as much. Conduction and convection grow only in proportion to $\Delta T$, so at high temperature radiation is the main way heat leaves. This is why furnaces and stars are described by radiation alone. Near room temperature the net formula is close to linear in $T - T_{\text{amb}}$, because $T^4 - T_{\text{amb}}^4 \approx 4 T_{\text{amb}}^3 (T - T_{\text{amb}})$ when the difference is small.

#### *(A Blackbody Is a Trap, Not a Color)* Any surface that absorbs every wavelength that reaches it has $\varepsilon = 1$.

A small hole in a closed box is a blackbody. Light that enters bounces around inside and is absorbed before it finds the hole again, so nothing comes back out and the hole looks black even if the inside is painted white. The sun is a blackbody for the same reason. A laser beam aimed at it is absorbed long before it could scatter back out. The word black refers to absorption at all wavelengths. Many objects that look black absorb visible light and reflect infrared, and their $\varepsilon$ in the infrared is lower than their appearance suggests.

#### *(Surface, Not Bulk)* Emissivity is a property of the surface layer, and a coat of paint changes it.

A steel ball and a glass ball painted with the same white paint have the same emissivity, because radiation that cannot pass the surface never reaches the material below. Polishing a metal lowers $\varepsilon$ and painting it raises $\varepsilon$. Thermos flasks are silvered on the inside for this reason: a low-emissivity surface both radiates little and reflects most of what arrives. The emissivity also depends on wavelength in general, so a surface can be a good absorber of sunlight and a poor emitter in the infrared. Solar collectors use such coatings.

#### *(Both Directions at Once)* Radiative exchange is always two-way, and only the net matters for heating or cooling.

A person in a room at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ radiates several hundred watts and absorbs nearly as much back from the walls. The net loss is what the body must replace by metabolism, and it is much smaller. Standing near a cold window raises the net loss, because the window radiates less back than a warm wall does. This is why the window side of a room feels cold even when the air temperature is the same. A clear night sky is colder still, and objects facing it can cool below the air temperature and frost over.

#### *(Absorbing Sunlight)* Sunlight striking a surface at an angle $\theta$ from the perpendicular delivers an absorbed power of $\varepsilon I A \cos\theta$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover the tilt factor $\cos\theta$ or the split between sunlit and emitting areas. This property follows Giancoli section 19.10.

A tilted surface catches less of the beam. The beam crosses only the projection of the surface, which has area $A\cos\theta$. So the surface absorbs
$$P_{\text{abs}} = \varepsilon I A \cos\theta.$$
At $\theta = 0$ the surface faces the sun and absorbs $\varepsilon I A$. At $\theta = 90^\circ$ the light grazes the surface and delivers nothing. A flat plate absorbs sunlight on its sunlit face only. It emits from both faces, so its emitted power is $\varepsilon \sigma (2A) T^4$. The edges add a little more area. For a thin plate this is usually ignored.

#### *(Temperature Change Over Time)* The net power sets how fast the temperature changes, $mc\,dT/dt = P_{\text{in}} - P_{\text{out}}$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture said only that an object absorbing more than it emits would warm without limit. It did not write this rate equation. The homework uses it.

A power is a rate of heat flow, $dQ/dt$. By [[2.4.3 Specific Heat]], a small heat $dQ$ changes the temperature by $dT = dQ/mc$. Dividing by $dt$ gives
$$mc\,\frac{dT}{dt} = P_{\text{in}} - P_{\text{out}}.$$
Take a plate in sunlight with the surroundings ignored. Then $P_{\text{in}} = \varepsilon I A_{\text{sun}}$ and $P_{\text{out}} = \varepsilon \sigma A_{\text{emit}} T^4$. The right side depends on $T$, so the rate changes as the plate warms. To find the time from $T_1$ to $T_2$, separate the variables and integrate,
$$t = \int_{T_1}^{T_2} \frac{mc\,dT}{P_{\text{in}} - P_{\text{out}}(T)}.$$
If the net power stays nearly constant, this reduces to $t \approx mc\,(T_2 - T_1)/P_{\text{net}}$.

#### *(Steady Temperature)* The temperature stops changing when the absorbed power equals the emitted power.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture treated the balance only for an object in a room at one temperature. It did not treat an object heated by the sun. This property follows Giancoli section 19.10.

Setting $dT/dt = 0$ gives $P_{\text{in}} = P_{\text{out}}$. For the plate above, $\varepsilon I A_{\text{sun}} = \varepsilon \sigma A_{\text{emit}} T_0^4$, so
$$T_0 = \left(\frac{I A_{\text{sun}}}{\sigma A_{\text{emit}}}\right)^{1/4}.$$
The emissivity cancels, because it scales absorption and emission alike. This holds when one $\varepsilon$ describes both the sunlight and the infrared the plate emits. A plate below $T_0$ warms toward it, and a plate above $T_0$ cools toward it. The temperature approaches $T_0$ but never passes it. So a problem can only ask for a final temperature between the start and $T_0$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Radiation from a Person
>A person with skin temperature $33\ ^\circ\text{C}$, surface area $1.6\ \text{m}^2$, and emissivity $0.70$ stands in a room whose walls are at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the emitted power and the net rate of heat loss by radiation.
>>In kelvins the temperatures are $306\ \text{K}$ and $293\ \text{K}$. The emitted power is
>>$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = \varepsilon\sigma A T^4 = (0.70)(5.67 \times 10^{-8})(1.6)(306)^4 = 560\ \text{W}.$$
>>The net loss subtracts what the walls send back,
>>$$\frac{dQ}{dt}\bigg|_{\text{net}} = (0.70)(5.67 \times 10^{-8})(1.6)\left[(306)^4 - (293)^4\right] = 89\ \text{W}.$$
>>The body emits more than half a kilowatt but recovers most of it from the room. The net $89\ \text{W}$ is comparable to the resting metabolic rate, so radiation is a major route of heat loss even indoors.

>>[!example]- Find its total radiated power and the intensity of sunlight at the earth's orbit, $1.50 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m}$ away.
>>The surface area is $4\pi R^2 = 6.09 \times 10^{18}\ \text{m}^2$, so
>>$$P = \sigma A T^4 = (5.67 \times 10^{-8})(6.09 \times 10^{18})(5800)^4 = 3.9 \times 10^{26}\ \text{W}.$$
>>At the earth this power is spread over a sphere of radius $1.50 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m}$,
>>$$I = \frac{P}{4\pi r^2} = \frac{3.9 \times 10^{26}}{4\pi (1.50 \times 10^{11})^2} = 1.4 \times 10^{3}\ \text{W/m}^2,$$
>>which matches the measured solar constant of $1370\ \text{W/m}^2$ above the atmosphere. Two numbers about the sun and one about the earth's orbit reproduce the brightness of daylight.

>>[!example]- Find the steady temperature of the plate.
>>In the steady state the plate absorbs as much as it emits, net. Per unit area it absorbs $1000\ \text{W/m}^2$ from the sun plus $\sigma T_{\text{amb}}^4$ on each of two faces, and it emits $\sigma T^4$ from each of two faces,
>>$$1000 + 2\sigma T_{\text{amb}}^4 = 2\sigma T^4, \qquad T^4 = T_{\text{amb}}^4 + \frac{1000}{2\sigma}.$$
>>With $T_{\text{amb}} = 293\ \text{K}$, $T_{\text{amb}}^4 = 7.37 \times 10^{9}\ \text{K}^4$ and $1000/(2\sigma) = 8.82 \times 10^{9}\ \text{K}^4$, so $T^4 = 1.62 \times 10^{10}\ \text{K}^4$ and
>>$$T = 357\ \text{K} = 84\ ^\circ\text{C}.$$
>>A dark car roof in the sun reaches temperatures of this order. Air moving over the plate would carry heat away by convection and lower the result. A white plate would absorb less sunlight while still radiating well in the infrared, and it would run much cooler.

>[!example] (Hard) A Satellite Panel Warming Up
>A thin aluminum panel on a satellite leaves the earth's shadow at $200\ \text{K}$ and turns to face the sun. Sunlight delivers $I = 1370\ \text{W/m}^2$ to its front face. The panel is $2.0\ \text{mm}$ thick and has $\varepsilon = 0.80$ on both faces. Aluminum has $\rho = 2700\ \text{kg/m}^3$ and $c = 900\ \text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$. Space sends back almost no radiation, and the edges can be ignored.
>
>```tikz
>\usepackage{tikz}
>\begin{document}
>\definecolor{hot}{HTML}{D9483B}
>\definecolor{mid}{HTML}{8A8A8A}
>\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1cm,y=1cm,>=latex]
>\fill (3.0,0) rectangle (3.15,2.4);
>\foreach \y in {0.9,1.5,2.1} \draw[->,hot,very thick] (0.4,\y) -- (2.9,\y);
>\node[hot,above] at (1.6,2.3) {sunlight, $I$};
>\draw[->,thick] (2.95,0.4) -- (2.2,-0.2);
>\draw[->,thick] (3.2,0.4) -- (3.95,-0.2);
>\node[below] at (1.9,-0.25) {$\varepsilon\sigma T^4$};
>\node[below] at (4.25,-0.25) {$\varepsilon\sigma T^4$};
>\node[right] at (3.3,2.25) {panel, $T$};
>\node[mid,right] at (4.1,1.2) {space: nothing comes back};
>\end{tikzpicture}
>\end{document}
>```
>
>>[!example]- Find the panel's steady temperature, its initial heating rate, and the time it takes to reach $300\ \text{K}$.
>>Work per square meter of panel. A square meter of panel with thickness $a$ has mass $\rho a$, so the rate equation reads
>>$$\rho a c\,\frac{dT}{dt} = \varepsilon I - 2\varepsilon\sigma T^4.$$
>>The factor $2$ counts both faces. The steady temperature makes the right side zero,
>>$$T_0 = \left(\frac{I}{2\sigma}\right)^{1/4} = \left(\frac{1370}{2(5.67 \times 10^{-8})}\right)^{1/4} = 331.5\ \text{K}.$$
>>At the start, $\rho a c = (2700)(0.0020)(900) = 4860\ \text{J/(m}^2\cdot\text{K)}$. The net power per square meter is $0.80\,[1370 - 2(5.67 \times 10^{-8})(200)^4] = 951\ \text{W/m}^2$. The panel starts warming at
>>$$\frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{951}{4860} = 0.20\ \text{K/s}.$$
>>For the time, write $I = 2\sigma T_0^4$ and separate the variables,
>>$$t = \frac{\rho a c}{2\varepsilon\sigma}\int_{T_1}^{T_2}\frac{dT}{T_0^4 - T^4}.$$
>>Split the integrand by partial fractions,
>>$$\frac{1}{T_0^4 - T^4} = \frac{1}{2T_0^2}\left(\frac{1}{T_0^2 - T^2} + \frac{1}{T_0^2 + T^2}\right).$$
>>Each piece is a standard integral. With $\tanh^{-1} x = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1+x}{1-x}$, the result is
>>$$t = \frac{\rho a c}{4\varepsilon\sigma T_0^3}\left[\tanh^{-1}\frac{T}{T_0} + \tan^{-1}\frac{T}{T_0}\right]_{T_1}^{T_2}.$$
>>The prefactor is $4860 / [4(0.80)(5.67 \times 10^{-8})(331.5)^3] = 735\ \text{s}$. The ratios are $T_2/T_0 = 0.905$ and $T_1/T_0 = 0.603$. The bracket is $(1.4986 + 0.7355) - (0.6983 + 0.5428) = 0.9930$, so
>>$$t = 735 \times 0.9930 = 730\ \text{s} \approx 12\ \text{min}.$$
>>With no emission, the panel would absorb a constant $0.80 \times 1370\ \text{W/m}^2$ and take only $443\ \text{s}$. Emission grows as the panel warms, so the last kelvins come slowly.
