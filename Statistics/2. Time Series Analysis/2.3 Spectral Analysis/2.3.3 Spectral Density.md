>[!note] Spectral Density
>For a stationary [[2.1. Time Series|time series]] $\{X_t\}$ whose [[2.2.2 Autocovariance Function|autocovariance]] is absolutely summable ($\sum_h |\gamma(h)| < \infty$), the **spectral density** $f(\omega)$ is the discrete-time Fourier transform of the autocovariance:
>$$f(\omega) \;=\; \sum_{h = -\infty}^{\infty} \gamma(h)\, e^{-2\pi i h \omega}, \qquad \omega \in [-\tfrac12, \tfrac12].$$
>The autocovariance is recovered by the inverse transform $\gamma(h) = \int_{-1/2}^{1/2} e^{2\pi i h \omega} f(\omega)\, d\omega$. So the autocovariance and the spectral density are the time-domain and frequency-domain faces of the same information about the process; neither contains anything the other lacks.
>
>$f(\omega)$ is the population quantity that the [[2.3.2 Periodogram|periodogram]] estimates from data. It says how the variance of the process is distributed across frequencies, playing the role of a probability density for the *energy* of the process rather than for its values. At lag $h = 0$, the inverse transform recovers the total variance as the integral of the spectral density over all frequencies,
>$$\gamma(0) = \mathrm{Var}(X_t) = \int_{-1/2}^{1/2} f(\omega)\, d\omega,$$
>so reading off the proportion $\int_a^b f(\omega)\, d\omega \,/\, \gamma(0)$ tells you the fraction of the process's variance carried by frequencies in $[a, b]$.
>
>For real-valued processes, $f(\omega) = f(-\omega)$ (because $\gamma(h)$ is real and even), so plots conventionally show only $\omega \in [0, 1/2]$. The Nyquist frequency $\omega = 1/2$ is the highest oscillation a discretely sampled process can express.
>
>>[!warning] Time-Domain vs. Frequency-Domain Equivalence
>>The autocovariance and the spectral density are Fourier-pair representations of the same information about a stationary process. The right choice between them is pragmatic: lag-based diagnostics (ACF/PACF for ARMA model identification) are easier in the time domain, while periodic structure (oscillations, hidden frequencies, harmonic peaks) is easier to read in the frequency domain.

# Intuition

If autocovariance $\gamma(h)$ tells you how strongly the process at time $t$ co-varies with the process at time $t + h$, the spectral density rearranges that same information by *frequency*: it tells you how much of the process's total variance is contributed by sinusoidal fluctuations at each rate $\omega$. A process whose energy concentrates near $\omega = 0$ moves slowly and has long memory; one whose energy concentrates near $\omega = 1/2$ alternates rapidly. White noise, with no preferred timescale, has flat spectral density: equal power at every frequency, the structural reason behind the analogy with white light.

The non-negativity of $f(\omega)$ is the frequency-domain reflection of the positive semi-definiteness of $\gamma$. Both are different statements of the same constraint: a real autocovariance function whose Fourier transform went negative could be used to construct a linear combination $\sum_i a_i X_{t_i}$ with negative variance, which is impossible. So the constraint that makes a function a valid autocovariance in the time domain is exactly the constraint that makes its Fourier transform a valid spectral density in the frequency domain.

# Properties

#### *(Non-Negativity)* $f(\omega) \geq 0$ for every $\omega$, by construction from a positive semi-definite $\gamma$.

A negative spectral density would imply a linear combination of the process with negative variance, which contradicts the positive semi-definiteness of $\gamma$. Equivalently, every valid spectral density is the squared magnitude of *some* transfer function applied to white noise, a direct consequence of the linear-filter identity below.

#### *(Symmetry)* $f(\omega) = f(-\omega)$ for real-valued processes.

This follows from $\gamma(h) = \gamma(-h)$. The frequency band $[0, 1/2]$ contains all the information; values for $\omega \in [-1/2, 0)$ are mirror images of values in $[0, 1/2]$.

#### *(Variance Decomposition)* The integral of the spectral density over all frequencies equals the process variance.

Setting $h = 0$ in the inverse transform gives $\gamma(0) = \int_{-1/2}^{1/2} f(\omega)\, d\omega$, which is the spectral analogue of the identity that variance equals the total mass of any density. Reading off proportions of the integral over a frequency band quantifies how much of the process's variability is carried by oscillations in that band: the basis of spectral-bandwidth analyses ("80% of the power is below $\omega = 0.05$").

#### *(White Noise)* For $\{W_t\} \sim \mathrm{WN}(0, \sigma^2)$, the spectral density is the constant $\sigma^2$.

Substituting the white-noise autocovariance ($\gamma(0) = \sigma^2$, $\gamma(h) = 0$ for $h \neq 0$) into the definition of $f(\omega)$ collapses the sum to its single non-zero term: $f_W(\omega) = \sigma^2$ at every $\omega$. Constant spectrum is the defining frequency-domain feature of white noise; any deviation from a flat spectrum is structure that an ARMA or other parametric model could in principle capture.

#### *(MA(1) Spectral Density)* For $X_t = W_t + \theta W_{t-1}$, the spectrum is a polynomial in $\cos$.

Plugging the MA(1) autocovariance into the definition gives
$$f_X(\omega) \;=\; \sigma^2(1 + \theta^2 + 2\theta\cos 2\pi\omega).$$
The shape decreases monotonically from $\omega = 0$ to $\omega = 1/2$ when $\theta > 0$, giving *low-pass* behavior, with power concentrated at low frequencies because positive lag-1 correlation makes realizations look smoothed. With $\theta < 0$ the shape increases, giving *high-pass* behavior with power at $\omega = 1/2$ from the alternating structure.

#### *(AR(1) Spectral Density)* For $X_t = \phi X_{t-1} + W_t$ with $|\phi| < 1$, the spectrum is the reciprocal of a polynomial.

By the linear-filter identity below applied to the AR(1) inverted into MA($\infty$) form, the spectrum becomes
$$f_X(\omega) \;=\; \frac{\sigma^2}{|1 - \phi e^{-2\pi i\omega}|^2} \;=\; \frac{\sigma^2}{1 - 2\phi\cos(2\pi\omega) + \phi^2}.$$
This is unimodal with a peak at $\omega = 0$ for $\phi > 0$ (long memory, slow drift) and at $\omega = 1/2$ for $\phi < 0$ (rapid alternation). The closer $|\phi|$ is to $1$, the sharper the peak: the AR(1) is a one-pole linear filter applied to white noise, and the pole at $z = 1/\phi$ creates the spectral concentration.

#### *(Linear Filter Identity)* Filtering a stationary process scales its spectrum by the squared magnitude of the filter's frequency response.

If $Y_t = \sum_j a_j X_{t-j}$ is the output of a [[2.3.4 Linear Filters and Frequency Response|linear filter]] with frequency response $A(\omega) = \sum_j a_j e^{-2\pi i j\omega}$ applied to a stationary input $X_t$, then
$$f_Y(\omega) \;=\; |A(\omega)|^2\, f_X(\omega).$$
This is the central computational tool of spectral analysis: convolution in the time domain becomes multiplication in the frequency domain. It is why ARMA spectra have closed-form expressions, why filter design is much easier in the frequency domain, and why audio engineering, telecommunications, and seismology all live in the spectral world.

# Examples

>[!example] Periodic process with random phase
>For $X_t = U_1 \cos(2\pi\omega_0 t) + U_2 \sin(2\pi\omega_0 t)$ with $U_1, U_2$ uncorrelated, mean zero, variance $\sigma^2$, where does the energy live?
>>[!example]- Solution
>>Computing the autocovariance,
>>$$\gamma(h) = \mathbb{E}[X_t X_{t+h}] = \sigma^2 \cos(2\pi\omega_0 h),$$
>>using $\mathbb{E}[U_i U_j] = \sigma^2 \mathbb{1}\{i = j\}$ and the trig identity $\cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta = \cos(\alpha - \beta)$. Writing the cosine in complex form,
>>$$\gamma(h) = \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\, e^{-2\pi i \omega_0 h} + \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\, e^{2\pi i \omega_0 h},$$
>>which has the form of an inverse Fourier transform with mass $\sigma^2/2$ concentrated at $\pm\omega_0$. This is a *discrete* spectral measure: the process is a single tone, all its energy lives at exactly one frequency, and there is no spectral *density*, only a spectral *measure* with two atoms. This is why the more general spectral distribution function exists alongside the density: it accommodates processes whose energy is concentrated at points rather than spread over intervals.

>[!example] Spectrum of an MA(2) low-pass filter
>What is the spectrum of $X_t = \tfrac13(W_t + W_{t-1} + W_{t-2})$, the running mean of three white-noise terms?
>>[!example]- Solution
>>The frequency response of the filter $a = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$ is
>>$$A(\omega) = \tfrac13(1 + e^{-2\pi i\omega} + e^{-4\pi i\omega}).$$
>>Factoring out $e^{-2\pi i\omega}$ gives $A(\omega) = e^{-2\pi i\omega}\cdot \tfrac13(1 + 2\cos 2\pi\omega)$, so
>>$$|A(\omega)|^2 = \tfrac19 (1 + 2\cos 2\pi\omega)^2.$$
>>At $\omega = 0$ the gain is $1$; at $\omega = 1/2$ it is $1/9$; at $\omega = 1/3$ the factor $(1 + 2\cos 2\pi\omega)$ vanishes, producing a spectral zero. Multiplying by the white-noise spectrum $\sigma^2$ gives the output spectrum $f_X(\omega) = \tfrac{\sigma^2}{9}(1 + 2\cos 2\pi\omega)^2$, preserving slow variation while killing the highest frequencies. A moving average is a low-pass filter, the simplest smoother in time-series practice.
