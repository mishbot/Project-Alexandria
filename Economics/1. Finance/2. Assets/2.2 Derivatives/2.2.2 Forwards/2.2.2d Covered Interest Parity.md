>[!note] Covered Interest Parity
>**Covered interest parity** (CIP) is a no-arbitrage rule for the forward exchange rate. It links that rate to the spot rate and the two interest rates. Let $S_0$ be the spot rate in units of domestic currency per unit of foreign currency. Let $F_0$ be the forward rate, in the same units, for an exchange at date $T$. Let $r$ and $r_f$ be the domestic and foreign risk-free rates, both continuously compounded. An investor with $S_0$ units of domestic currency can reach $T$ by two routes.
>- The **home route** invests the $S_0$ at $r$ and ends with $S_0e^{rT}$.
>- The **foreign route** converts the $S_0$ into one foreign unit and invests it at $r_f$. It then sells the $e^{r_fT}$ foreign units forward at $F_0$.
>
>Neither route bears any risk, so both must end with the same amount,
>$$S_0e^{rT} = F_0e^{r_fT}, \qquad F_0 = S_0e^{(r - r_f)T}.$$
>A foreign currency acts like a stock with dividend yield $q = r_f$ ([[2.2.2b Forward Price]]). Its holder earns the foreign rate. The parity is "covered" because the forward removes the exchange rate risk of the foreign route.
>
>The difference $F_0 - S_0$ is called the **forward points**, or forward margins.
>- The foreign currency trades at a **forward premium** when the points are positive, which happens when $r > r_f$.
>- The foreign currency trades at a **forward discount** when the points are negative, which happens when $r < r_f$.
>
>A quote away from parity allows an arbitrage in one of two directions.
>- If the quoted forward is above $S_0e^{(r - r_f)T}$, a trader borrows domestic currency and buys foreign currency at spot. The trader invests it at $r_f$ and sells it forward.
>- If the quoted forward is below $S_0e^{(r - r_f)T}$, a trader borrows foreign currency and sells it at spot. The trader invests the proceeds at $r$ and buys the foreign currency forward to repay the loan.
>
>The holder of a long FX (foreign exchange) forward buys $N$ foreign units at $T$ at the delivery price $K$. At a date $t < T$, the contract's [[2.2.2c Value of a Forward Contract|value]] in domestic currency is
>$$f = N\big(S_te^{-r_f(T-t)} - Ke^{-r(T-t)}\big) = N(F_t - K)e^{-r(T-t)}.$$
>Here $S_t$ is the spot rate at $t$ and $F_t = S_te^{(r - r_f)(T-t)}$ is the forward rate at $t$. The parity assumes free borrowing and lending in both currencies, no transaction costs, and no counterparty risk. A dealer can value a [[2.2.5b Currency Swap|currency swap]] as a series of FX forwards priced this way.

>[!warning] Lecture Notation
>The lecture notes write $FX_0$ for the spot rate, $FX_T$ for the forward rate, and $r_d$ for the domestic rate. There $FX_T$ is a rate fixed at time $0$ for an exchange at $T$. It is not the spot rate that will hold at $T$.

# Intuition

Firms that will pay or receive foreign currency later want to fix the exchange rate now. Banks quote forward rates for them. A bank that sells a currency forward does not guess the future spot rate. It hedges at once by borrowing one currency and depositing the other. The bank sets the forward rate to cover the interest it pays and earns on that hedge. A currency with a low interest rate therefore trades at a forward premium. Its holder earns less interest, and the higher forward rate makes up the difference.

# Properties

#### *(Present Value Form)* Two ways of securing one foreign unit at $T$ cost the same today.

Dividing $S_0e^{rT} = F_0e^{r_fT}$ by $e^{(r + r_f)T}$ gives
$$S_0e^{-r_fT} = e^{-rT}F_0.$$
The left side is the cost of buying $e^{-r_fT}$ foreign units today and depositing them at $r_f$. The deposit grows to exactly one unit at $T$. The right side is the present value of paying $F_0$ at $T$ under a forward. Both give one foreign unit at $T$, so they cost the same. The value of an FX forward is the difference of these two present values. The holder of a long FX forward will receive $N$ foreign units and pay $NK$ at $T$. In domestic currency at $t$, the first is worth $NS_te^{-r_f(T-t)}$ and the second $NKe^{-r(T-t)}$. This gives the value formula in the callout. Dividing that value by $S_t$ restates it in foreign currency.

#### *(Discrete Compounding)* With annual compounding, the parity uses the ratio of the two growth factors.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The parity is taught only with continuously compounded rates. The annual and simple-interest forms below are an extension.

The same two routes, with rates $r$ and $r_f$ compounded once a year, give
$$F_0 = S_0\left(\frac{1 + r}{1 + r_f}\right)^{T}.$$
For a period under a year, money markets often use simple interest, which gives $F_0 = S_0(1 + rT)/(1 + r_fT)$. All three forms give $(F_0 - S_0)/S_0 \approx (r - r_f)T$ when rates are small. Each form needs rates quoted in its own convention. Take $S_0 = \$0.7300$ per Canadian dollar, $r = 4.0\%$, $r_f = 2.8\%$, and $T = 2$ years, with annual compounding. The annual form gives $F_0 = \$0.7471$. Using these annual rates in the continuous formula gives $\$0.7477$, an error of about $\$590$ per million Canadian dollars.

#### *(Quote Direction)* A rate quoted in foreign currency per domestic unit flips the roles of the two interest rates.

Markets quote some pairs in foreign units per domestic unit. Yen against the dollar, for example, is usually quoted in yen per dollar. Write $\tilde S_0 = 1/S_0$ and $\tilde F_0 = 1/F_0$ for such quotes. Inverting the parity gives
$$\tilde F_0 = \tilde S_0e^{(r_f - r)T}.$$
The foreign rate now sits in the growth term. Swapping the two rates by mistake moves the forward by about $2(r - r_f)T$ in relative terms. One check works in any quote direction. The currency with the higher interest rate always trades at a forward discount. The (Hard) example below uses a quote in yen per dollar.

#### *(Frictions)* Trading costs turn the parity into a band, and quotes inside the band allow no arbitrage.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The trading-cost band is taught only for stock forwards. The evidence on deviations since 2008 is also beyond the lecture.

Each arbitrage trade buys at the ask price and sells at the bid price. It also borrows at a higher rate than it lends, and pays commissions. The trade that sells the forward profits only if the forward bid exceeds the domestic loan balance at $T$. That balance uses the spot ask, the domestic borrowing rate, and the foreign deposit rate. The trade that buys the forward profits only if the forward ask is below the domestic deposit balance at $T$. That balance uses the spot bid, the domestic deposit rate, and the foreign borrowing rate. Between these two limits no trade earns a profit. [[2.2.4 Cash-and-Carry Arbitrage]] derives the same band for a forward on a stock. Since the 2008 financial crisis, forward rates for major currencies have often differed from parity by more than trading costs. Du, Tepper, and Verdelhan (2018) document these deviations. They link them to post-crisis rules on bank balance sheets, which make the arbitrage costly for banks.

# Derivation

We want the forward rate $F_0$ that allows no arbitrage. Start with $S_0$ units of domestic currency at time $0$. The home route ends with $S_0e^{rT}$. The foreign route buys one foreign unit at spot, and the deposit grows to $e^{r_fT}$ units by $T$. Selling those units forward at time $0$ fixes their domestic value at $F_0e^{r_fT}$. Both amounts are known at time $0$. By the [[4.2.2a Arbitrage|law of one price]], they are equal, so $S_0e^{rT} = F_0e^{r_fT}$ and $F_0 = S_0e^{(r - r_f)T}$.

If a quoted forward $F$ differs from parity, one of the two trades below earns a risk-free profit. Each table is in domestic currency, per foreign unit delivered at $T$. The first applies when $F > S_0e^{(r - r_f)T}$.

| Position | At $0$ | At $T$ |
|---|---|---|
| Borrow $S_0e^{-r_fT}$ domestic at $r$ | $+S_0e^{-r_fT}$ | $-S_0e^{(r - r_f)T}$ |
| Buy $e^{-r_fT}$ foreign units at $S_0$, deposit at $r_f$ | $-S_0e^{-r_fT}$ | $0$ (one foreign unit, delivered into the forward) |
| Sell one foreign unit forward at $F$ | $0$ | $+F$ |
| Total | $0$ | $F - S_0e^{(r - r_f)T}$ |

The second applies when $F < S_0e^{(r - r_f)T}$.

| Position | At $0$ | At $T$ |
|---|---|---|
| Borrow $e^{-r_fT}$ foreign units at $r_f$, sell at $S_0$ | $+S_0e^{-r_fT}$ | $0$ (one foreign unit owed, paid from the forward) |
| Deposit the proceeds at $r$ | $-S_0e^{-r_fT}$ | $+S_0e^{(r - r_f)T}$ |
| Buy one foreign unit forward at $F$ | $0$ | $-F$ |
| Total | $0$ | $S_0e^{(r - r_f)T} - F$ |

Each trade starts from nothing and ends with a positive amount. Arbitrage trades of this kind move the quote back toward $S_0e^{(r - r_f)T}$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Six-Month Euro Forward
>The euro trades at $\$1.0920$ per euro. The six-month dollar rate is $4.10\%$ and the six-month euro rate is $2.35\%$, both per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Find the six-month forward rate and forward points, and say if the euro trades at a premium or discount.
>>The parity gives
>>$$F_0 = 1.0920e^{(0.0410 - 0.0235)(0.5)} = 1.0920e^{0.00875} = \$1.1016 \text{ per euro}.$$
>>The forward points are $1.1016 - 1.0920 = +0.0096$, so the euro trades at a forward premium. The dollar rate is higher, so dollar deposits earn more. The higher forward rate pays the holder of euros for the lower euro interest. A firm that buys 2,000,000 euros forward pays $2{,}000{,}000 \times F_0 = \$2{,}203{,}193.85$ in six months, using the unrounded $F_0$.

>[!example] (Medium) Valuing an Importer's Forward
>A US importer is long a forward to buy 4,000,000 Australian dollars (AUD) at $K = \$0.6650$ per AUD. Five months remain. Today the spot rate is $\$0.6810$ per AUD. The dollar rate is $4.25\%$ and the AUD rate is $3.85\%$, both per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Find today's forward rate for the same date and the contract's value to the importer and to the bank.
>>The time left is $T - t = 5/12$ year. Today's forward rate is
>>$$F_t = 0.6810e^{(0.0425 - 0.0385)(5/12)} = \$0.682136 \text{ per AUD}.$$
>>The value to the importer is
>>$$f = 4{,}000{,}000 \times (F_t - 0.6650)e^{-0.0425 \times 5/12} = \$67{,}340.67,$$
>>using the unrounded $F_t$. The present value form, $4{,}000{,}000 \times \big(0.6810e^{-0.0385 \times 5/12} - 0.6650e^{-0.0425 \times 5/12}\big)$, gives the same amount. In AUD the value is $67{,}340.67 / 0.6810 = 98{,}884.98$ AUD. The bank holds the short side, worth $-\$67{,}340.67$. The importer gains because today's forward rate, $\$0.6821$, is above the $\$0.6650$ it locked in.

>[!example] (Hard) An Arbitrage Quoted in Yen per Dollar
>A dealer quotes the yen at 149.20 yen per dollar spot. Its nine-month forward quote is 144.10 yen per dollar. The dollar rate is $4.4\%$ and the yen rate is $0.5\%$, both per year with continuous compounding. A US trader can borrow and lend at these rates in both currencies.
>
>>[!example]- Find the parity forward rate in both quote directions. Then decide which way the quote is wrong, and build an arbitrage on 1,000,000,000 yen.
>>Take the dollar as the domestic currency. The spot rate is $S_0 = 1/149.20 = \$0.0067024$ per yen. The parity forward is
>>$$F_0 = 0.0067024e^{(0.044 - 0.005)(0.75)} = 0.0067024e^{0.02925} = \$0.0069014 \text{ per yen}.$$
>>In yen per dollar this is $149.20e^{-0.02925} = 144.90$. The yen trades at a forward premium, so the yen-per-dollar number falls from 149.20 to 144.90. The quote of 144.10 yen per dollar equals $1/144.10 = \$0.0069396$ per yen, above parity. Forward yen are too expensive, so the trader sells yen forward.
>>
>>The trader buys today the yen that grow to 1,000,000,000 at $T$. That is $1{,}000{,}000{,}000\,e^{-0.00375} = 996{,}257{,}022$ yen, which cost $996{,}257{,}022 / 149.20 = \$6{,}677{,}325.89$. The trader borrows these dollars for nine months, and the loan grows by $e^{0.033} = 1.033551$.
>>
>>| Position (in \$) | Today | In nine months |
>>|---|---|---|
>>| Borrow dollars at $4.4\%$ | $+6{,}677{,}325.89$ | $-6{,}901{,}353.77$ |
>>| Buy 996,257,022 yen at 149.20, deposit at $0.5\%$ | $-6{,}677{,}325.89$ | $0$ (1,000,000,000 yen, delivered) |
>>| Sell 1,000,000,000 yen forward at 144.10 | $0$ | $+6{,}939{,}625.26$ |
>>| Total | $0$ | $+38{,}271.49$ |
>>
>>The profit equals the gap between the quoted and parity rates, $1/144.10 - F_0$ dollars per yen, times 1,000,000,000 yen. A forward quote above 144.90 yen per dollar would reverse the trade. The trader would then borrow yen, sell them at spot, deposit the dollars at $4.4\%$, and buy yen forward.
