>[!note] 1x1 and Depthwise Convolution
>A full [[5.2.1. Convolutional Layer|convolutional layer]] mixes information across space and across channels at once. Two special cases keep one kind of mixing and drop the other:
>- A **$1 \times 1$ convolution**, or pointwise convolution, has kernel size $K = 1$. It mixes the channels at each pixel and never looks at neighboring pixels.
>- A **depthwise convolution** gives each of the $C$ channels its own $K \times K$ filter. It filters each channel over space and never mixes channels.
>
>For output channel $c_{\text{out}}$ at pixel $(r, c)$, the $1 \times 1$ convolution computes
>$$h_{c_{\text{out}}}[r,c] = a\Big[\beta_{c_{\text{out}}} + \sum_{c_{\text{in}}=1}^{C_{\text{in}}} \omega_{c_{\text{out}}c_{\text{in}}} \cdot x_{c_{\text{in}}}[r,c]\Big].$$
>It is the same dense layer applied to the channel vector at every pixel. It changes the number of channels without changing the height or width. Ignoring biases, the weight counts for $C_{\text{in}}$ input and $C_{\text{out}}$ output channels are:
>- **Full $K \times K$ convolution**: $K^2 C_{\text{in}} C_{\text{out}}$.
>- **$1 \times 1$ convolution**: $C_{\text{in}} C_{\text{out}}$.
>- **Depthwise convolution**, with $C_{\text{out}} = C_{\text{in}} = C$: $K^2 C$.

# Intuition

A full convolution answers two questions at once: which patterns appear nearby, and how the channels combine. Splitting the two questions saves weights. A $1 \times 1$ convolution handles only the channels. It acts like a small dense layer that looks down through the stack of feature maps at one pixel. A depthwise convolution handles only space. It runs a separate filter over each feature map. This is like applying a blur or edge filter to each color of an image on its own. Chaining the two recovers most of what a full layer does, at a fraction of the cost.

# Properties

#### *(A Dense Layer at Every Pixel)* A $1 \times 1$ convolution applies the same small dense layer to the channel vector at every pixel.

With one input and one output channel it scales every pixel by a learned constant and adds a learned bias. With many channels it computes, at each pixel, a weighted sum of all channels for each output channel. It never looks at neighboring pixels, so it does not enlarge the [[5.2.1a. Receptive Field|receptive field]]. Networks use it in three roles:
- **Matching shapes**: it changes the channel count so that two tensors can be added across a [[5.2.5. Residual Networks|residual connection]].
- **Reducing cost**: it shrinks the channels before an expensive $3 \times 3$ layer.
- **Per-pixel outputs**: at the end of a segmentation network it turns each pixel's features into class scores.

#### *(Bottleneck Blocks)* Shrinking the channels with a $1 \times 1$ layer before a $3 \times 3$ layer, then restoring them, saves most of the weights.

A bottleneck residual block has three convolutions. The first is $1 \times 1$ and reduces the channels, for example from $256$ to $64$. The second is $3 \times 3$ and works on the $64$ channels. The third is $1 \times 1$ and restores $256$ channels so the result can be added to the block's input. The weights are $256 \cdot 64 + 9 \cdot 64^2 + 64 \cdot 256 = 69{,}632$. One full $3 \times 3$ layer at $256$ channels has $9 \cdot 256^2 = 589{,}824$, about $8.5$ times as many. The deep ResNets in Prince, such as ResNet-200, are built from bottleneck blocks.

#### *(Depthwise Filters Each Channel Separately)* A depthwise convolution divides the weight count by $C$ but removes all mixing between channels.

Each input channel $c$ is cross-correlated with its own $K \times K$ filter, $\mathbf{h}_c = \boldsymbol{\omega}_c \star \mathbf{x}_c$, and the $C$ results are stacked. A $12 \times 12 \times 3$ input filtered by three $5 \times 5 \times 1$ kernels without padding gives an $8 \times 8 \times 3$ output with $75$ weights. A full convolution with three output channels would need $225$. No output channel sees more than one input channel. The layer therefore cannot detect a pattern that spans channels, such as a red-green edge. Mixing information across channels needs a separate layer, usually a $1 \times 1$ convolution.

#### *(A Spectrum of Channel Mixing)* Grouped convolutions interpolate between a full convolution and a depthwise one.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Grouped convolutions appear only in Prince's chapter notes. Depthwise separable convolution was popularized by Xception (Chollet, 2017) and MobileNets (Howard et al., 2017).

A **grouped convolution** splits the input channels into $G$ groups. Each group is convolved only with its own share of the output channels. Its weight count is $K^2 C_{\text{in}} C_{\text{out}} / G$. Two settings of $G$ give familiar layers:
- **$G = 1$** is a full convolution.
- **$G = C_{\text{in}} = C_{\text{out}}$** is a depthwise convolution.

AlexNet used $G = 2$ to split some of its layers across two GPUs. A **depthwise separable convolution** follows a depthwise layer with a $1 \times 1$ layer. It has $K^2 C_{\text{in}} + C_{\text{in}} C_{\text{out}}$ weights, a fraction $1/C_{\text{out}} + 1/K^2$ of a full layer. With $K = 3$ and $256$ channels in and out, that is about $1/8.7$. The factorization assumes that spatial filtering and channel mixing can be done one after the other. This is a constraint, though it costs little accuracy in practice.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A 1x1 Convolution at One Pixel
>A $1 \times 1$ convolution maps $2$ input channels to $3$ output channels and uses a ReLU. Writing its weights as a $C_{\text{out}} \times C_{\text{in}}$ matrix,
>$$\boldsymbol{\Omega} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\beta} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$
>One pixel of the input has channel values $\mathbf{x}[r,c] = [1, -2]^T$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the output at this pixel, and count the parameters for a $32 \times 32$ input.
>>The pre-activation is $\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{x}[r,c] + \boldsymbol{\beta} = [1 - 2,\; 2 - 1,\; 2]^T = [-1, 1, 2]^T$. The ReLU gives $\mathbf{h}[r,c] = [0, 1, 2]^T$. Every other pixel uses the same $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ and $\boldsymbol{\beta}$. The layer therefore has $6 + 3 = 9$ parameters for a $32 \times 32$ input or any other size. The output is $32 \times 32 \times 3$.

>[!example] (Medium) Depthwise, Full and Separable
>A $12 \times 12 \times 3$ input is filtered with $5 \times 5$ kernels, no padding and stride $1$. The goal is $8$ output channels.
>
>>[!example]- Count the weights of a full convolution and of a depthwise convolution followed by a $1 \times 1$ convolution. What are the output shapes?
>>The spatial output is $12 - 5 + 1 = 8$ along each axis. A full layer has $25 \cdot 3 \cdot 8 = 600$ weights and outputs $8 \times 8 \times 8$. The depthwise layer has $25 \cdot 3 = 75$ weights and outputs $8 \times 8 \times 3$. The $1 \times 1$ layer then maps $3$ channels to $8$ with $3 \cdot 8 = 24$ weights and keeps the $8 \times 8$ size. The separable pair has $99$ weights in total, about a sixth of the full layer, and gives the same $8 \times 8 \times 8$ output shape. The full layer can learn any $5 \times 5 \times 3$ filter for each output channel. The separable pair is more limited. Each input channel gets one spatial pattern, and the output channels differ only in how they weight these patterns.

>[!example] (Medium) Where the Bottleneck Saves
>A residual block works on $128$ channels. One design uses two $3 \times 3$ layers at $128$ channels. Another uses a bottleneck: $1 \times 1$ from $128$ to $32$, $3 \times 3$ at $32$ channels, and $1 \times 1$ back to $128$.
>
>>[!example]- Compare the weight counts and the receptive fields of the two blocks.
>>The plain block has $2 \cdot 9 \cdot 128^2 = 294{,}912$ weights. The bottleneck has $128 \cdot 32 + 9 \cdot 32^2 + 32 \cdot 128 = 4096 + 9216 + 4096 = 17{,}408$, about $17$ times fewer. The $1 \times 1$ layers add nothing to the receptive field. The bottleneck therefore grows it by $2$ pixels, while the plain block grows it by $4$. The saved weights allow more blocks, which is how bottleneck ResNets reach hundreds of layers.
