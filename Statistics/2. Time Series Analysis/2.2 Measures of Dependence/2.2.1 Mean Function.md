>[!note] Mean Function
>The **mean function** of a [[2.1. Time Series|time series]] $\{X_t\}$ is the [[2.5a Expectation|expected value]] of the process at each time $t$, defined as
>$$\mu_t = \mathbb{E}[X_t] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\, f_t(x)\,dx,$$
>where $f_t(x)$ is the marginal density of $X_t$. The mean function specifies the **baseline level** around which the series fluctuates at each point in time.
>
>In general, $\mu_t$ can vary with $t$, reflecting systematic structure such as trend or seasonality. A constant mean $\mu_t = \mu$ for all $t$ is a necessary (but not sufficient) condition for [[2.1. Time Series|stationarity]].
>
>>[!warning] Sample vs. Population Mean
>>The **sample mean** $\bar X = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T X_t$ is a single number summarizing the observed series, whereas $\mu_t$ is a function of time describing the theoretical average at each index. If the series is stationary, $\bar X$ is a consistent estimator of the common $\mu$; if $\mu_t$ varies, $\bar X$ conflates different levels and may be misleading.

# Intuition
The mean function answers the question: **if you could repeat the experiment generating $\{X_t\}$ infinitely many times, what would the average value be at time $t$?** It captures deterministic structure (trend, seasonal cycles, or other systematic shifts) that persists across realizations.

For a stationary process, $\mu_t$ is a flat line: the series has no preferred direction over time. For a process with trend, $\mu_t$ drifts upward or downward. For a seasonal process, $\mu_t$ oscillates periodically. Estimating or removing $\mu_t$ is often the first step in time series analysis, because the [[2.2.2 Autocovariance Function|autocovariance]] and [[2.2.3 Autocorrelation Function|autocorrelation]] are defined in terms of deviations from the mean.

# Properties
#### (*Linearity*) If $Y_t = aX_t + b$, then $\mu_Y(t) = a\mu_X(t) + b$.
#### (*Sum*) If $Z_t = X_t + Y_t$, then $\mu_Z(t) = \mu_X(t) + \mu_Y(t)$.
#### (*Stationarity Condition*) A process is (weakly) stationary only if $\mu_t = \mu$ is constant for all $t$.
#### (*Sample Estimator*) For a stationary process, the natural estimator of $\mu$ is $\bar X = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T X_t$, which is unbiased: $\mathbb{E}[\bar X] = \mu$.

# Examples
>[!example] White Noise
>Let $\{W_t\}$ be white noise with $W_t \sim (0, \sigma^2)$ for all $t$. Then $\mu_t = \mathbb{E}[W_t] = 0$ for every $t$, so the mean function is identically zero.

>[!example] Random Walk with Drift
>Let $X_t = \delta t + \sum_{j=1}^t W_j$, where $\delta$ is the drift and $W_j \sim (0, \sigma^2)$ are i.i.d.
>>[!example]- What is the mean function?
>>By linearity of expectation,
>>$$\mu_t = \mathbb{E}[X_t] = \delta t + \sum_{j=1}^t \mathbb{E}[W_j] = \delta t.$$
>>The mean function is a straight line with slope $\delta$. This process is **not stationary** because $\mu_t$ depends on $t$.
