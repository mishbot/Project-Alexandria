>[!note] Mean Free Path
>The **mean free path** $\ell_M$ is the average distance a molecule travels between one collision and the next. For a gas of molecules of radius $r$ with number density $N/V$,
>$$\ell_M = \frac{1}{4\pi\sqrt{2}\, r^2\, (N/V)}.$$
>The formula treats the molecules as hard spheres that collide when their centers come within $2r$ of each other. The factor $\sqrt{2}$ accounts for the motion of the target molecules, which are not at rest. Using the [[2.2.3 Ideal Gas Law|ideal gas law]] $N/V = P/kT$, the same result reads
>$$\ell_M = \frac{kT}{4\pi\sqrt{2}\, r^2\, P},$$
>so the mean free path is inversely proportional to the pressure at fixed temperature.
>
>For air at standard temperature and pressure, with $r \approx 1.5 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ and $N/V = 2.69 \times 10^{25}\ \text{m}^{-3}$, the mean free path is about $9 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{m}$. That is roughly $300$ molecular diameters, or a tenth of the wavelength of visible light. The **collision frequency** is the mean speed divided by the mean free path,
>$$f = \frac{\overline{v}}{\ell_M},$$
>where $\overline{v} = \sqrt{8kT/\pi m}$ is the mean speed from the [[2.3.3 Maxwell Speed Distribution|Maxwell distribution]]. For air at STP the frequency is about $5 \times 10^{9}$ collisions per second per molecule.

# Intuition

A molecule in air moves at about $500\ \text{m/s}$. Yet a smell released in one corner of a room takes minutes to reach the other side. The mean free path explains the gap. The molecule does move fast, but it collides with a neighbor every $10^{-10}\ \text{s}$ and heads off in a new random direction. Its path is a [[4.4 Random Walks|random walk]] of tiny steps, and its net progress is slow. The mean free path is the step length of that walk.

The formula comes from a sweeping argument. Picture one molecule moving through a gas of others that are momentarily frozen. It collides with any molecule whose center lies within $2r$ of its own path, one radius for each sphere. In a time $\Delta t$ it therefore sweeps out a cylinder of radius $2r$ and length $\overline{v}\,\Delta t$, and it hits every molecule whose center is inside. The diagram shows the cylinder.

The number of centers inside the cylinder is its volume times the number density, $\pi (2r)^2\, \overline{v}\, \Delta t\, (N/V)$. That is the number of collisions in $\Delta t$. Dividing the distance traveled by the number of collisions gives the distance per collision, $\ell_M = 1 / (4\pi r^2 N/V)$. The frozen-target picture overcounts the free path by $\sqrt{2}$, because the targets also move. The average relative speed between two molecules is $\sqrt{2}\,\overline{v}$ rather than $\overline{v}$. The molecule therefore meets targets faster than the frozen picture says, and the free path is shorter by that factor.

# Properties

#### *(Pressure Sets the Path)* Lowering the pressure lengthens the mean free path in proportion.

At fixed temperature $\ell_M \propto 1/P$. Air at one atmosphere has $\ell_M \approx 10^{-7}\ \text{m}$. At $10^{-4}\ \text{Pa}$, a pressure that laboratory vacuum pumps reach, the mean free path is about $100\ \text{m}$. A molecule in such a chamber crosses it many times without meeting another molecule and collides only with the walls. Vacuum tubes, electron microscopes, and particle accelerators rely on this regime. It is also the regime in which a gas released into an evacuated tube advances at the thermal speed of its molecules. The examples in [[2.3.2 Root-Mean-Square Speed]] use this fact.

#### *(Collisions Are Brief)* The mean free path is hundreds of times the molecular size, which justifies the kinetic theory assumptions.

For air at STP the ratio $\ell_M / 2r$ is about $300$. A molecule spends nearly all of its time in free flight and only a small fraction in contact with another molecule. This is the assumption that the [[2.3.1 Molecular Interpretation of Temperature|kinetic theory]] makes when it says collisions are brief and the molecules are far apart. Both assumptions fail when the density rises until $\ell_M$ approaches $2r$, which is where the gas begins to behave like a liquid.

#### *(Random Walk)* A molecule's net displacement after $n$ collisions is about $\ell_M \sqrt{n}$, not $\ell_M n$.

Each free path points in a random direction, so the displacements partly cancel. The [[4.4 Random Walks|random walk]] result is that the typical straight-line distance from the start after $n$ steps of length $\ell_M$ is $\ell_M \sqrt{n}$. In one second an air molecule takes about $5 \times 10^{9}$ steps of $9 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{m}$. It ends up only about $6\ \text{mm}$ from where it began, despite covering $450\ \text{m}$ of path. This is the molecular origin of diffusion. It is why smells spread slowly and why stirring a liquid mixes it far faster than waiting.

#### *(Mean Speed, Not RMS)* The collision frequency uses the mean speed $\overline{v}$.

The rate of collisions is the rate at which a molecule sweeps out volume, which depends on the distance it covers per second. Distance per second is the mean speed. The rms speed appears in energy and pressure, but not here. The difference is only $8$ percent. At the level of estimate that the hard-sphere model supports, either speed gives the same order of magnitude.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Air at Standard Conditions
>Take an air molecule to have radius $r = 1.5 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ and molar mass $29\ \text{g/mol}$. At STP the number density is $2.69 \times 10^{25}\ \text{m}^{-3}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the mean free path and the number of collisions per second.
>>The mean free path is
>>$$\ell_M = \frac{1}{4\pi\sqrt{2}\,(1.5 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m})^2\,(2.69 \times 10^{25}\ \text{m}^{-3})} = 9.3 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{m}.$$
>>The mean speed at $273\ \text{K}$ is $\overline{v} = \sqrt{8RT/\pi M} = \sqrt{8(8.314)(273)/(\pi \times 0.029)} = 447\ \text{m/s}$. The collision frequency is
>>$$f = \frac{\overline{v}}{\ell_M} = \frac{447\ \text{m/s}}{9.3 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{m}} = 4.8 \times 10^{9}\ \text{s}^{-1},$$
>>about five billion collisions per second for each molecule.

>[!example] (Medium) Pressure for a One-Meter Free Path
>A vacuum chamber at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ must let molecules cross $1.0\ \text{m}$ without colliding, on average. Use $r = 1.5 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}$.
>
>>[!example]- What pressure is required?
>>Solving the pressure form of the formula for $P$ gives
>>$$P = \frac{kT}{4\pi\sqrt{2}\, r^2\, \ell_M} = \frac{(1.381 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K})(293\ \text{K})}{4\pi\sqrt{2}\,(1.5 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m})^2\,(1.0\ \text{m})} = 1.0 \times 10^{-2}\ \text{Pa}.$$
>>This is ten billionths of an atmosphere. Ordinary laboratory pumps reach it. Each cubic centimeter of the chamber still holds about $2.5 \times 10^{12}$ molecules, but they are spread thinly enough that collisions between them are rare.

>[!example] (Hard) How Far in One Second
>An air molecule at STP moves at a mean speed of $447\ \text{m/s}$ and collides $4.8 \times 10^{9}$ times per second.
>
>>[!example]- Estimate how far the molecule ends up from its starting point after one second, and compare with the distance it travels.
>>In one second the molecule travels $447\ \text{m}$ along its zigzag path. The path has $n = 4.8 \times 10^{9}$ steps of length $\ell_M = 9.3 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{m}$. A random walk of $n$ steps ends a typical distance $\ell_M \sqrt{n}$ from its start,
>>$$d \approx (9.3 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{m})\,\sqrt{4.8 \times 10^{9}} = 6.4 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}.$$
>>The molecule travels $447\ \text{m}$ to get $6\ \text{mm}$ away. The ratio $\sqrt{n}$ against $n$ is what makes diffusion in a gas at atmospheric pressure so slow compared with the speed of the molecules.
