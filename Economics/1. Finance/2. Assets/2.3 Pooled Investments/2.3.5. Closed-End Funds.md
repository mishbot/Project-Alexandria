>[!note] Closed-End Funds
>A **closed-end fund (CEF)** is an investment company that holds a portfolio of assets (often stocks) and issues a **fixed number of its own shares**, typically sold in an IPO, that then trade on an exchange like a stock. 
>- Because the share supply is inelastic and investors trade the fund shares with each other (rather than redeeming with the fund), the CEF’s market price can deviate from its **net asset value (NAV)**, which is the per-share value of the underlying portfolio, given as
>$$\text{NAV} = \frac{\text{Mkt. Val. of Holdings} - \text{Liabilities}}{\text{CEF Shares Outstanding}}.$$
>- This is contrary to an **open-end fund**, where the fund manager can issue new shares in order to fix fund share price deviations from the NAV.
>	- If new investors bring in money, the fund **issues new shares at NAV** and uses the cash to buy more of the underlying assets. Shares outstanding rises.
>	- Because creation/redemption happens at NAV, the trading price doesn't drift far from NAV, in contrast to closed-end funds.
>
>In a frictionless rational benchmark, a CEF should trade at **par** relative to NAV, but empirically CEFs often trade at **discounts** (market cap below NAV) that fluctuate over time and tend to converge back to NAV when the fund is terminated. 
>- More particularly, a CEF trades at a premium of $\approx 10\%$ after its inception (CEFs are usually created during times of high investor sentiment), but falls to a discount of $\approx 10\%$ within 120 days, and is subject to wide fluctuations thereafter.
>- A common behavioral explanation is **retail investor sentiment**, where shifts in sentiment move the fund price even though the underlying portfolio value is unchanged, which also helps explain why discounts across different CEFs are correlated.
>- **Fundamental risk** of underlying assets and **noise-trader risk** in the CEF drive its price down to compensate for the additional risk.
>
>If a CEF is shut down (i.e., liquidates its portfolio, pays off its liabilities and returns cash to shareholders, eliminating noise-trader risk) or converted to an OEF, price converges to NAV because shareholders can then effectively receive NAV.
