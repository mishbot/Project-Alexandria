>[!note] Orthogonality
>A set of vectors $S \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is **pairwise orthogonal** if and only if every *distinct* pair of vectors  $\vec{x}, \vec{y} \in S$ is pairwise orthogonal, such that
>$$\left<\vec{x},\vec{y}\right>=0.$$
>If $S$ is orthogonal, then it is linearly independent and contains at most $n$ vectors.
>
>Two sets of vectors $S_1$ and $S_2$ are **mutually orthogonal** if:
>- All $\vec{x} \in S_1$ and $\vec{y} \in S_2$ are pairwise orthogonal.
>- The bases $B_1$ and $B_2$ of $S_1$ and $S_2$ are orthogonal to each other.
>
>>[!warning] Orthogonality in Euclidean Space
>>Two nonzero vectors $\vec{x}$ and $\vec{y}$ are orthogonal if they are perpendicular in Euclidean space, such that they make an angle of $\theta=90\degree$ between them. This is critical in order to meet the dot product criterion $|\vec{x}||\vec{y}|\cos\theta=0$.
