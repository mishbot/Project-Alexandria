>[!note] Fourier Analysis
>Fourier analysis is a mathematical technique that decomposes complex functions or signals into a sum of simpler trigonometric functions, specifically sines and cosines, or complex exponentials. In the realm of linear algebra, Fourier analysis can be interpreted as a simple change of basis that compresses a signal to remove its most irrelevant components. 
>
>Any Fourier analysis method contains a synthesis and an analysis equation:
>1. The **synthesis** equation (inverse Fourier) aims to decompose a signal $x$ into a linear combination of its Fourier coefficients in the frequency domain, $X$.
>2. The analysis equation aims to decompose a signal's Fourier coefficients $X$ into a linear combination of its signal values.
>
>The following chart outlines all four Fourier analysis methods:
>
>|     | **D-T**  | **C-T**  |
| :-: | :--: | :--: |
| **D-F** | DTFS | CTFS |
| **C-F** | DTFT | CTFT |
>where the rows signify whether the Fourier analysis is discrete or continuous in the frequency domain, and the columns signify whether it is discrete or continuous in the time domain.
>>[!warning] Note (Periodicity)
>>While the **Fourier series** works with *periodic* signals in the time domain and produces *discrete* frequency coefficients, the **Fourier transform** deals with *aperiodic* signals in the time domain and produces a *continuous* frequency spectrum. Additionally, the DTFT has a *periodic* frequency response over $2\pi$, whereas the CTFT produces a *non-periodic continuous spectrum*.
>
>>[!warning] Note (Infinite Signals)
>>All of the above methods deal with signals of assumed infinite length (with the Fourier series dealing with periodic signals, therefore limiting itself to a finite interval $p$). For signals of finite length, the ***DFT (discrete Fourier transform)*** and the ***CFT (continuous Fourier transform)*** are methods of Fourier analysis that can be applied. Their formulations are very close to the **DTFS** and **CTFS**.
>
