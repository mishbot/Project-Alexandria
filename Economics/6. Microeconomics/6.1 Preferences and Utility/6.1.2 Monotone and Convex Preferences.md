>[!note] Monotone and Convex Preferences
>Monotonicity and convexity are assumptions on the shape of a [[6.1.1 Preference Relations|preference relation]] $\succsim$ over bundles in $X = \mathbb{R}^2_+$. Write $\mathbf{x} \ge \mathbf{y}$ when $x_j \ge y_j$ for every good $j$. The relation is **monotonic** if $\mathbf{x} \ge \mathbf{y}$ implies $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$. It is **strictly monotonic** if $\mathbf{x} \ge \mathbf{y}$ together with $x_j > y_j$ for at least one good $j$ implies $\mathbf{x} \succ \mathbf{y}$. For a rational relation, strict monotonicity implies monotonicity. The relation is **convex** if, whenever $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{z}$ and $\mathbf{y} \succsim \mathbf{z}$,
>$$t\mathbf{x} + (1-t)\mathbf{y} \succsim \mathbf{z} \quad \text{for all } t \in [0, 1].$$
>The bundle $t\mathbf{x} + (1-t)\mathbf{y}$ is a **mixture** of $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$, a point on the segment between them. Equivalently, $\succsim$ is convex when every upper contour set $\{\mathbf{x} \in X : \mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{z}\}$ is a [[2.1 Convex Sets|convex set]].
>
>Each assumption restricts the **indifference curves**, the sets $\{\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{x} \sim \mathbf{z}\}$ of bundles indifferent to a given bundle. Under monotonicity these sets contain no thin upward-sloping pieces, but they can be thick regions or contain horizontal and vertical pieces. Under strict monotonicity they are thin curves that slope strictly downward, and curves farther from the origin are better. Under convexity the curves bow toward the origin, and straight segments are allowed.
>
>With convex and strictly monotone preferences, a point where the budget line touches an indifference curve is a best affordable bundle. [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]] relies on this fact. Without convexity such a point can be the worst bundle on the budget line, one of the cases in [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions]]. [[6.1.4 Indifference Curves and MRS]] expresses these shapes through the slope of the curves.

# Intuition

A rational preference relation can still rank bundles in strange ways. The consumer might dislike one of the goods, or stop caring about it after some amount. Under monotonicity, more of a good is never worse. Both goods are then goods, and neither is a **bad**, such as noise or pollution, that the consumer wants less of.

A consumer with convex preferences likes balance. Suppose she is indifferent between 4 coffees with no croissants and 4 croissants with no coffee. Under convexity she likes 2 of each at least as much as either extreme. In the diagram, $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ lie on the same indifference curve. The segment of mixtures between them stays in the hatched set of bundles at least as good as both. The midpoint lies strictly above the curve, so a consumer with strictly monotone preferences ranks it strictly higher than $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$.

# Properties

#### *(Thick and Flat Pieces)* Monotone preferences never produce an upward-sloping thin curve, but they allow thick regions and flat or vertical segments.

Suppose $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathbf{y}$ with $\mathbf{y} \ge \mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y} \neq \mathbf{x}$. Take any bundle $\mathbf{w}$ with $\mathbf{x} \le \mathbf{w} \le \mathbf{y}$. Monotonicity gives $\mathbf{y} \succsim \mathbf{w}$ and $\mathbf{w} \succsim \mathbf{x}$. Since $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathbf{y}$, transitivity gives $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{w}$, so $\mathbf{w} \sim \mathbf{x}$. The whole rectangle between $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ is then one indifference region. So two indifferent bundles on an upward-sloping line always come with a thick region around them. The other shapes appear in three familiar cases. A student who stops caring after 5 slices of pizza and 5 cans of soda has one thick indifference region. It contains all bundles with $x_1 \ge 5$ and $x_2 \ge 5$. A consumer who cares only about good 1 has vertical indifference lines. A bike shop ranks deliveries of frames $x_1$ and pairs of wheels $x_2$ by the number of complete bikes it can build. Its indifference curves are L-shaped, with a horizontal and a vertical piece, the shape of [[6.4c Perfect Complements]].

#### *(Downward-Sloping Graphs)* Strict monotonicity turns each indifference curve into the graph of a decreasing function $x_2 = g(x_1)$.

If two different bundles were indifferent and one had at least as much of both goods, strict monotonicity would rank it strictly higher. So any two different bundles on one curve trade off, with more of one good and less of the other. In particular, a vertical line meets each curve at most once. So the curve is the graph of a strictly decreasing function of $x_1$. The slope of this function is the marginal rate of substitution. Under strict monotonicity the budget constraint also binds in utility maximization. A consumer with income left over can buy a little more of a good, and that bundle is strictly better.

#### *(Tangency Can Fail)* Without convexity, a point where a budget line touches an indifference curve can be the worst affordable bundle on that line.

The diagram shows a curve that bows away from the origin. The mixtures of $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ fall inside the curve, in the set of worse bundles. A straight budget line that touches such a curve at one point lies in the better set at every other point. So every other bundle on that budget line is strictly better than the touching point. With convex and strictly monotone preferences, the set of bundles at least as good as the touching point is convex. This set lies on or above the budget line. Monotonicity is needed here: a consumer who is satiated at a point inside the budget set can have convex preferences and still prefer that point to the touching point. So no affordable bundle is strictly better, and the touching point is a best choice. Without convexity, a touching point must be compared with other affordable bundles, such as the ends of the budget line.

#### *(Straight Segments)* Convexity allows mixtures that are exactly as good as the endpoints, so indifference curves may contain straight pieces.

The definition uses the weak relation $\succsim$ for the mixture. A driver who only cares about total gallons of gasoline, from either of two stations, has straight-line indifference curves $x_1 + x_2 = \text{constant}$. These preferences are convex, and every mixture of two indifferent bundles is indifferent to both. When a budget line is parallel to such a straight piece, every bundle on the piece is a best choice. This source of multiple optima appears in [[6.4b Perfect Substitutes]].

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Coffee and Croissants
>A consumer ranks bundles of coffees $x_1$ and croissants $x_2$ in $X = \mathbb{R}^2_+$. Consider three relations.
>- Relation (a): $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ if and only if $2x_1 + x_2 \ge 2y_1 + y_2$.
>- Relation (b): $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ if and only if $x_1 \ge y_1$.
>- Relation (c): $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ if and only if $x_1 - x_2 \ge y_1 - y_2$, for a consumer on a diet who treats croissants as a bad.
>
>>[!example]- Which relations are monotonic, strictly monotonic, and convex?
>>Relation (a) is strictly monotonic. If $\mathbf{x} \ge \mathbf{y}$ with one strict inequality, then $2x_1 + x_2 > 2y_1 + y_2$. It is therefore monotonic. It is convex. If $2x_1 + x_2 \ge c$ and $2y_1 + y_2 \ge c$, where $c = 2z_1 + z_2$, then every mixture also satisfies the inequality, because the left side is linear in the bundle.
>>
>>Relation (b) is monotonic but not strictly monotonic. The bundles $(1, 5)$ and $(1, 2)$ satisfy $(1, 5) \ge (1, 2)$ with more croissants, yet $(1, 5) \sim (1, 2)$. It is convex, because $x_1 \ge z_1$ and $y_1 \ge z_1$ imply $t x_1 + (1-t) y_1 \ge z_1$.
>>
>>Relation (c) is not monotonic. The bundle $(1, 2)$ has more croissants than $(1, 1)$, yet $1 - 2 < 1 - 1$, so $(1, 1) \succ (1, 2)$. It is still convex, by the same linearity argument as in (a). Convexity and monotonicity are separate assumptions, and a relation can satisfy one without the other.

>[!example] (Medium) Checking Convexity
>A consumer ranks bundles in $X = \mathbb{R}^2_+$ by the product of the two quantities, $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ if and only if $x_1 x_2 \ge y_1 y_2$. The bundles $(1, 9)$ and $(9, 1)$ are indifferent, since both products equal 9.
>
>>[!example]- Check the mixtures with $t = 1/2$ and $t = 1/4$, then prove that the relation is convex.
>>With $t = 1/2$ the mixture is $(5, 5)$, with product $25 \ge 9$. With $t = 1/4$ the mixture is $\tfrac{1}{4}(1, 9) + \tfrac{3}{4}(9, 1) = (7, 3)$, with product $21 \ge 9$. Both mixtures are at least as good as the endpoints, and in fact strictly better.
>>
>>For the general proof, take any bundle $\mathbf{z}$ and let $k = z_1 z_2$. If $k = 0$, the upper contour set of $\mathbf{z}$ is all of $X$, which is convex. If $k > 0$, the upper contour set is the set of bundles with $x_1 > 0$ and $x_2 \ge k / x_1$. This is the region above the graph of $f(x_1) = k / x_1$. The function $f$ is convex on $x_1 > 0$, because $f''(x_1) = 2k / x_1^3 > 0$, as in [[2.2 Convex Functions]]. The region above the graph of a convex function is a convex set. If $a_2 \ge f(a_1)$ and $b_2 \ge f(b_1)$, then
>>$$t a_2 + (1-t) b_2 \ge t f(a_1) + (1-t) f(b_1) \ge f(t a_1 + (1-t) b_1).$$
>>The first inequality combines the two assumptions, and the second is the definition of a convex function. So every upper contour set is convex, and the relation is convex.

>[!example] (Hard) Beach or Mountains
>A traveler splits vacation days between the beach, $x_1$, and the mountains, $x_2$. Moving between the two places wastes time, so she prefers to stay in one place. She ranks plans by $\mathbf{x} \succsim \mathbf{y}$ if and only if $x_1^2 + x_2^2 \ge y_1^2 + y_2^2$. She has 6 days, so her plans satisfy $x_1 + x_2 = 6$.
>
>>[!example]- Is the relation strictly monotonic? Is it convex? Which plan with 6 days is worst, and which is best?
>>The relation is strictly monotonic. If $\mathbf{x} \ge \mathbf{y}$ with $x_j > y_j \ge 0$ for some good $j$, then $x_j^2 > y_j^2$, and the other square does not fall.
>>
>>The relation is not convex. The plans $(6, 0)$ and $(0, 6)$ are indifferent, with value 36. Their midpoint $(3, 3)$ has value $9 + 9 = 18 < 36$, so $(6, 0) \succ (3, 3)$. The mixture is strictly worse than both endpoints, as in the diagram above.
>>
>>Along the line $x_1 + x_2 = 6$, the value of a plan is
>>$$x_1^2 + (6 - x_1)^2 = 2x_1^2 - 12x_1 + 36.$$
>>This parabola has its minimum at $x_1 = 3$, with value 18, and its maximum at the ends $x_1 = 0$ and $x_1 = 6$, with value 36. The even split $(3, 3)$ is the worst plan. It is also the only point where the line touches the indifference curve $x_1^2 + x_2^2 = 18$. Every other point of the line has a larger value. The best plans are the two extremes, $(6, 0)$ and $(0, 6)$. A rule that picks the touching point would choose the worst plan. The same shape appears in [[6.4d CES Utility]] with $\rho = 2$.
