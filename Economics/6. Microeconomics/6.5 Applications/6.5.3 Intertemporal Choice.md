>[!note] Intertemporal Choice
>The **intertemporal choice** model applies [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|utility maximization]] to consumption at two dates. A person lives for two periods, young ($t = 0$) and old ($t = 1$). She earns income $M_0$ when young and $M_1$ when old, with $M_0 < M_1$ as the leading case. She consumes $c_0$ and $c_1$, each at price $1$. A credit market lets her lend or borrow any amount at the interest rate $r$. Her savings $M_0 - c_0$, negative if she borrows, become $(M_0 - c_0)(1+r)$ when she is old. The budget constraint has two equivalent forms,
>$$c_1 \le M_1 + (M_0 - c_0)(1+r) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad c_0 + \frac{c_1}{1+r} \le M_0 + \frac{M_1}{1+r}.$$
>The second form says that the **present value** of consumption, its worth in period-0 units, cannot exceed the present value of income. One unit at $t = 1$ is worth $1/(1+r)$ at $t = 0$, because $1/(1+r)$ saved today grows to $1$. Both goods have price $1$, so $r$ is a real interest rate. With inflation, [[1.1 Fisher's Equation]] converts a nominal rate into a real one.
>
>Utility is $u(c_0, c_1) = U(c_0) + \frac{1}{1+\delta}U(c_1)$, with $U' > 0$ and $U'' < 0$. The **discount rate** $\delta \in [0, \infty)$ measures impatience. A person with discount rate $\delta$ is indifferent between one hour of TV today and $1 + \delta$ hours of TV next period. $\delta = 0$ means perfect patience, and a large $\delta$ means a myopic person. The first-order conditions of the Lagrangian give
>$$\frac{U'(c_0^*)}{U'(c_1^*)} = \frac{1+r}{1+\delta}.$$
>The left side is the ratio of marginal utilities. The right side is the market reward for waiting, $1 + r$, relative to the person's impatience, $1 + \delta$. If $r = \delta$, the person smooths consumption perfectly, for any $U$ with $U'' < 0$:
>$$c_0^* = c_1^* = c^* = \frac{1+r}{2+r}\,M_0 + \frac{1}{2+r}\,M_1.$$
>This is a weighted average of the two incomes, so $M_0 < c^* < M_1$ in the leading case. If $r > \delta$, then $c_0^* < c_1^*$. If $r < \delta$, then $c_0^* > c_1^*$.
>
>The [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] applied to the first-order condition gives $\partial c_0^*/\partial M_0 > 0$ and $\partial c_0^*/\partial M_1 > 0$. With $D = U''(c_0^*) + \frac{(1+r)^2}{1+\delta}\,U''(c_1^*) < 0$, the effect of the interest rate is
>$$\frac{\partial c_0^*}{\partial r} = \frac{U'(c_1^*)}{(1+\delta)\,D} + \frac{(1+r)\,U''(c_1^*)\,(M_0 - c_0^*)}{(1+\delta)\,D}.$$
>The first term is the substitution effect and is always negative. The second term is the income effect and equals $(M_0 - c_0^*)\,\partial c_0^*/\partial M_1$. It is positive for a saver ($M_0 > c_0^*$) and negative for a borrower ($M_0 < c_0^*$). A borrower cuts current consumption when $r$ rises, while for a saver the sign is ambiguous. The split has the same form as the [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]].

# Intuition

People often earn their income in different years from when they want to spend it. Young people usually earn less than they will later, while their needs are similar in both periods. A credit market lets a person move consumption between periods. Saving moves it into old age, and borrowing moves it into youth. The interest rate is the price of this move, since giving up one unit today yields $1 + r$ units tomorrow. The person is impatient, so she values a unit tomorrow at only $1/(1+\delta)$ of a unit today. The shape of the consumption path depends on which of the two is larger, $r$ or $\delta$.

The diagram shows the budget line in the $(c_0, c_1)$ plane. It passes through the **endowment point** $E = (M_0, M_1)$, where the person neither saves nor borrows. Its slope is $-(1+r)$, since each unit consumed today costs $1 + r$ units tomorrow. Its horizontal intercept $M_0 + M_1/(1+r)$ is the most she can consume today by borrowing against all her future income. Its vertical intercept $M_1 + (1+r)M_0$ is the most she can consume when old by saving all her current income. Points to the left of $E$ involve saving, and points to the right involve borrowing. The person in the diagram has $r > \delta$ and saves, so her optimum $A$ lies to the left of $E$.

# Properties

#### *(Reward Versus Impatience)* The ordering of $c_0^*$ and $c_1^*$ depends only on $r$ and $\delta$.

Moving one unit of consumption from youth to old age costs $U'(c_0)$ in utility today. It returns $1 + r$ units later, each worth $U'(c_1)/(1+\delta)$ in today's utility. Start from equal consumption, $c_0 = c_1$, where $U'(c_0) = U'(c_1)$. If $r > \delta$, the return $\frac{1+r}{1+\delta}\,U'(c_1)$ exceeds the cost $U'(c_0)$, so the person shifts consumption toward old age. As she does, $c_0$ falls and $U'(c_0)$ rises, while $c_1$ rises and $U'(c_1)$ falls, because $U$ is [[2.2 Convex Functions|concave]]. She stops when the first-order condition holds, with $c_0^* < c_1^*$. Her incomes set the level of consumption and whether she saves or borrows. They do not change the ordering of $c_0^*$ and $c_1^*$. A student who expects a high salary borrows today, and she still consumes more when old if $r > \delta$.

#### *(Only Present Value Matters)* A unit of future income has the same effect on $c_0^*$ as $1/(1+r)$ units of current income.

The two incomes enter the first-order condition only through $c_1 = M_1 + (M_0 - c_0)(1+r)$, that is, through $M_1 + (1+r)M_0$. This sum is $(1+r)$ times the present value $M_0 + M_1/(1+r)$. The optimal $c_0^*$ therefore depends on the incomes only through their present value, and the derivatives satisfy $\partial c_0^*/\partial M_1 = \frac{1}{1+r}\,\partial c_0^*/\partial M_0$. Two people with the same present value of income consume the same in both periods, whatever the timing of their pay. The timing changes only how much each one saves or borrows.

#### *(Saving Part of a Windfall)* An extra unit of income when young raises $c_0^*$ by less than one unit.

The derivation below gives
$$\frac{\partial c_0^*}{\partial M_0} = \frac{\frac{(1+r)^2}{1+\delta}\,U''(c_1^*)}{U''(c_0^*) + \frac{(1+r)^2}{1+\delta}\,U''(c_1^*)}.$$
The numerator and the denominator are both negative. The denominator is larger in size, so the ratio lies strictly between $0$ and $1$. The rest of the windfall is saved. Consumption when old rises too, since $c_1 = M_1 + (M_0 - c_0)(1+r)$ gives $\partial c_1^*/\partial M_0 = (1+r)\,(1 - \partial c_0^*/\partial M_0) > 0$. With a concave $U$, spreading a windfall over both periods raises utility more than consuming all of it at once.

#### *(Savers Gain, Borrowers Lose)* A rise in $r$ rotates the budget line around the endowment point, which helps savers and hurts borrowers.

A person at $E$ neither lends nor borrows, so the interest rate does not change what she can afford there. A higher rate makes the line steeper around $E$. To the left of $E$, where the person saves, the new line lies above the old one. To the right, where she borrows, it lies below. The income term $(M_0 - c_0^*)\,\partial c_0^*/\partial M_1$ measures this gain or loss. When $r$ rises by $dr$, a saver's $M_0 - c_0^*$ units of savings pay $(M_0 - c_0^*)\,dr$ more when she is old. This has the same effect as a rise in $M_1$ of that size. [[6.5.2 Labor Supply]] has the same structure, since a person who sells time gains from a higher wage. In both models the consumer starts with an endowment instead of a fixed money income. The income effect of a price change then has the sign of her net sales of the good.

#### *(Log Utility)* With $U(c) = \ln c$, consumption when young is a fixed share of the present value of income.

The first-order condition becomes $c_1/c_0 = (1+r)/(1+\delta)$. Substituting into the budget gives
$$c_0^* = \frac{1+\delta}{2+\delta}\left(M_0 + \frac{M_1}{1+r}\right), \qquad c_1^* = \frac{1+r}{2+\delta}\left(M_0 + \frac{M_1}{1+r}\right).$$
Here $M_0 + M_1/(1+r)$ is the present value of income. The utility $\ln c_0 + \frac{1}{1+\delta}\ln c_1$ is an [[6.1.3 Utility Functions|increasing transformation]] of the [[6.4a Cobb-Douglas]] utility $c_0^{(1+\delta)/(2+\delta)}\,c_1^{1/(2+\delta)}$. The fixed-share rule is therefore the Cobb-Douglas demand, with the present value as income and $1/(1+r)$ as the price of $c_1$. A more impatient person, with a higher $\delta$, consumes a larger share when young.

# Derivation

We want the first-order condition, the solution for $r = \delta$, and the comparative statics. The Lagrangian, with the sign convention of [[7.2 Lagrange Multipliers]], is
$$\mathcal{L} = U(c_0) + \frac{1}{1+\delta}\,U(c_1) - \lambda\left(c_0 + \frac{c_1}{1+r} - M_0 - \frac{M_1}{1+r}\right).$$
Setting its derivatives with respect to $c_0$ and $c_1$ to zero gives
$$U'(c_0) - \lambda = 0, \qquad \frac{U'(c_1)}{1+\delta} - \frac{\lambda}{1+r} = 0.$$
Dividing the first condition by the second eliminates $\lambda$ and gives $U'(c_0)/U'(c_1) = (1+r)/(1+\delta)$.

If $r = \delta$, the ratio equals $1$, so $U'(c_0) = U'(c_1)$. Since $U'' < 0$, the function $U'$ is strictly decreasing, and equal marginal utilities require equal consumption, $c_0^* = c_1^* = c^*$. Substituting into the budget gives
$$c^*\left(1 + \frac{1}{1+r}\right) = \frac{2+r}{1+r}\,c^* = M_0 + \frac{M_1}{1+r}.$$
Multiplying by $(1+r)/(2+r)$ gives the formula in the callout, with no functional form for $U$. The weights $(1+r)/(2+r)$ and $1/(2+r)$ are positive and sum to $1$, so $c^*$ lies strictly between $M_0$ and $M_1$ whenever $M_0 \ne M_1$. If $r > \delta$, the ratio exceeds $1$, so $U'(c_0^*) > U'(c_1^*)$, and a decreasing $U'$ requires $c_0^* < c_1^*$.

For the comparative statics, substitute the period-1 budget $c_1 = M_1 + (M_0 - c_0)(1+r)$ into the first-order condition. This leaves one equation in the one unknown $c_0$,
$$F(c_0; M_0, M_1, r) = U'(c_0) - \frac{1+r}{1+\delta}\,U'\big(M_1 + (M_0 - c_0)(1+r)\big) = 0.$$
By the [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]], the optimal $c_0^*$ is a function of the parameters, with $\partial c_0^*/\partial \theta = -F'_\theta / F'_{c_0}$ for each parameter $\theta$. This is method 1 of [[6.3.1 Comparative Statics]]. By the chain rule, with $\partial c_1/\partial c_0 = -(1+r)$, the denominator is
$$F'_{c_0} = U''(c_0) + \frac{(1+r)^2}{1+\delta}\,U''(c_1) = D < 0.$$
Both terms are negative because $U'' < 0$. The same denominator appears for every parameter.

For income when young, $\partial c_1/\partial M_0 = 1 + r$, so $F'_{M_0} = -\frac{(1+r)^2}{1+\delta}\,U''(c_1) > 0$ and
$$\frac{\partial c_0^*}{\partial M_0} = -\frac{-\frac{(1+r)^2}{1+\delta}\,U''(c_1)}{U''(c_0) + \frac{(1+r)^2}{1+\delta}\,U''(c_1)} > 0.$$
The numerator of the fraction is positive and $D$ is negative, so the minus sign in front makes the derivative positive. Consumption when young is a normal good. For income when old, $\partial c_1/\partial M_1 = 1$, so $F'_{M_1} = -\frac{1+r}{1+\delta}\,U''(c_1) > 0$ and $\partial c_0^*/\partial M_1 = -F'_{M_1}/D > 0$. This equals $\frac{1}{1+r}\,\partial c_0^*/\partial M_0$.

For the interest rate, $r$ appears twice in $F$, in the factor $(1+r)/(1+\delta)$ and inside $c_1$, with $\partial c_1/\partial r = M_0 - c_0$. The product rule gives
$$F'_r = -\frac{1}{1+\delta}\,U'(c_1) - \frac{1+r}{1+\delta}\,U''(c_1)\,(M_0 - c_0),$$
so that
$$\frac{\partial c_0^*}{\partial r} = -\frac{-\frac{1}{1+\delta}\,U'(c_1)}{D} - \frac{-\frac{1+r}{1+\delta}\,U''(c_1)\,(M_0 - c_0)}{D}.$$
The first term comes from the change in the relative price $(1+r)/(1+\delta)$ alone. Its numerator is negative because $U' > 0$. With $D < 0$ and the minus sign in front, the term is negative. Differentiating the first-order condition along a fixed indifference curve, where $dc_1 = -(1+r)\,dc_0$, gives exactly this term, so it is the substitution effect. The second term comes from the effect of $r$ on what the person can afford. Its numerator has the sign of $M_0 - c_0$, and the term has the same sign. Comparing it with $\partial c_0^*/\partial M_1 = -F'_{M_1}/D$ shows that it equals $(M_0 - c_0^*)\,\partial c_0^*/\partial M_1$, the income effect in the callout.

>[!warning] Signs Hold for Every Interest Rate
>The lecture derives $\partial c_0^*/\partial M_0$ and $\partial c_0^*/\partial r$ on the slides for the case $r > \delta$. The derivation above never uses $r > \delta$, so every sign holds for any interest rate and any discount rate.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Saver or Borrower
>Two siblings face the same interest rate. Ana is a medical student who borrows against her future salary, so $c_0^* > M_0$. Ben works when young and saves for old age, so $c_0^* < M_0$. The interest rate rises.
>
>>[!example]- For which sibling does current consumption fall for sure?
>>It falls for sure for Ana. Her substitution effect is negative, because waiting now pays more. Her income effect is $(M_0 - c_0^*)\,\partial c_0^*/\partial M_1$, where $M_0 - c_0^* < 0$ and $\partial c_0^*/\partial M_1 > 0$, so it is negative too. The higher rate raises the cost of the debt she carries, which makes her poorer. Both effects lower $c_0^*$.
>>
>>For Ben the substitution effect is still negative, but his income effect is positive. His savings earn more, which makes him richer, and he spends part of the gain in both periods. The total effect on $c_0^*$ depends on which effect is larger, so the model does not sign it without more information about $U$.

>[!example] (Medium) Smoothing With Log Utility
>A young worker earns $M_0 = 30$ now and expects $M_1 = 60$ later, in thousands of dollars. Her utility is $\ln c_0 + \frac{1}{1+\delta}\ln c_1$ with $\delta = 0.25$, so she is indifferent between one hour of TV today and $1.25$ hours next period.
>
>>[!example]- Find her consumption plan when $r = 0.25$ and when $r = 0.5$. Is she a saver or a borrower?
>>At $r = 0.25 = \delta$ she smooths perfectly. The general formula gives
>>$$c^* = \frac{1.25}{2.25}\times 30 + \frac{1}{2.25}\times 60 = 16.67 + 26.67 = 43.33.$$
>>She borrows $43.33 - 30 = 13.33$ when young and repays $13.33 \times 1.25 = 16.67$ when old, which leaves $60 - 16.67 = 43.33$. Her consumption lies between $M_0 = 30$ and $M_1 = 60$, as it must.
>>
>>At $r = 0.5$, the first-order condition with $U'(c) = 1/c$ reads $c_1/c_0 = 1.5/1.25 = 1.2$. The present value of income is $30 + 60/1.5 = 70$. The budget $c_0 + c_1/1.5 = 70$ becomes $c_0\,(1 + 1.2/1.5) = 1.8\,c_0 = 70$, so $c_0^* = 38.89$ and $c_1^* = 1.2 \times 38.89 = 46.67$. Now $r > \delta$, and $c_0^* < c_1^*$ as predicted. She still borrows $38.89 - 30 = 8.89$ and repays $8.89 \times 1.5 = 13.33$. As a borrower, she cuts current consumption when the rate rises, from $43.33$ to $38.89$.

>[!example] (Hard) A Saver and the Interest Rate
>A worker earns $M_0 = 60$ when young and nothing when old, $M_1 = 0$. Her utility is $\ln c_0 + \frac{1}{1.25}\ln c_1$, so $\delta = 0.25$, and the interest rate is $r = 0.25$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the substitution and income terms of $\partial c_0^*/\partial r$ at her optimum. What is the sign of the total, and what changes if she also earns $M_1 = 20$ when old?
>>With $r = \delta$ she smooths perfectly, so $c_0^* = c_1^* = \frac{1.25}{2.25}\times 60 = 33.33$. She saves $M_0 - c_0^* = 26.67$. For log utility $U'(c) = 1/c$ and $U''(c) = -1/c^2$, so at $c = 33.33$ we have $U' = 0.03$ and $U'' = -0.0009$. The denominator is
>>$$D = -0.0009 + \frac{1.25^2}{1.25}\times(-0.0009) = -0.0009 \times 2.25 = -0.002025.$$
>>The substitution term is $\frac{U'(c_1^*)}{(1+\delta)D} = \frac{0.03}{1.25 \times (-0.002025)} = -11.85$. For the income term, $\partial c_0^*/\partial M_1 = \frac{(1+r)\,U''(c_1^*)}{(1+\delta)\,D} = \frac{-0.0009}{-0.002025} = 0.444$, so the income term is $26.67 \times 0.444 = +11.85$.
>>
>>The two terms cancel, and $\partial c_0^*/\partial r = 0$. The log-utility formula confirms this, since $c_0^* = \frac{1+\delta}{2+\delta}\left(M_0 + \frac{M_1}{1+r}\right)$ equals $\frac{1.25}{2.25}\times 60$ for every $r$ when $M_1 = 0$. The same cancellation happens in [[6.5.2 Labor Supply]] for a person with no non-labor income.
>>
>>With $M_1 = 20$, the present value of income $M_0 + M_1/(1+r)$ falls when $r$ rises, and the log formula gives
>>$$\frac{\partial c_0^*}{\partial r} = -\frac{1+\delta}{2+\delta}\,\frac{M_1}{(1+r)^2} = -\frac{1.25}{2.25}\times\frac{20}{1.5625} = -7.11.$$
>>At $r = 0.25$ she now consumes $c_0^* = 42.22$ and saves $17.78$. Her substitution term is $-15.01$ and her income term is $+7.90$, so the substitution effect is larger. With log utility the sign depends only on future income. It is zero when $M_1 = 0$ and negative when $M_1 > 0$, whether she saves or borrows.
