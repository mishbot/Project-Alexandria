>[!note] Second Law of Thermodynamics
>The [[2.5.2 First Law of Thermodynamics|first law]] says energy is conserved. It allows a cold cup of coffee to warm itself by cooling the room, since energy would still balance. That never happens. The **second law of thermodynamics** is the rule that says which of the energy-conserving processes occur. It was found by experiment a century before atoms were accepted, so it exists in several equivalent forms. The oldest is the **Clausius statement**:
>
>Heat flows spontaneously from a body at higher temperature to a body at lower temperature, and never spontaneously in the reverse direction.
>
>A second form, the **Kelvin-Planck statement**, is about [[2.7.1 Heat Engine|heat engines]]: no cyclic device can take heat from a single reservoir and convert all of it to work. Some heat must always be rejected to a colder reservoir. Every engine has $Q_L > 0$ and $e < 1$. The two statements are equivalent. A device that violated one could be used to build a device that violates the other, and the last example below shows how.
>
>There is a third, modern form. It uses a state function called entropy, which measures the disorder of a system, and it says that the total entropy of a system together with everything it interacts with never decreases. That form needs entropy to be defined first, which happens later in this section, so it is stated and shown equivalent to the other two in [[2.7.7 Entropy Statement of the Second Law]]. The rest of this section applies the law: it sets the maximum efficiency of every heat engine and the minimum work for every refrigerator, and one particular engine reaches the limit.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not use the names Clausius or Kelvin-Planck, and it did not show that the statements are equivalent. It stated that heat flows from hot to cold on its own and never the reverse. It also explained why no engine can have $e = 1$. The names and the equivalence follow Giancoli sections 20.1 and 20.4.

# Intuition

Put heat $Q_H$ into a cylinder of gas and let the piston move out. The piston moves because molecules hit it, and only the part of their motion along the piston's axis does work. Molecules also move sideways and up and down. That motion pushes on walls that do not move and does nothing. The sketch shows the problem.

To turn all of $Q_H$ into work, every one of $10^{22}$ molecules would have to put all of its added energy into motion along one axis. They would all have to do it at the same time, for the whole stroke. With one molecule that could be arranged. With $10^{22}$ it cannot. Energy that has spread into random motion in three dimensions does not gather itself back into one direction. The second law rests on this fact. Thermodynamics states it without mentioning molecules, as a rule about heat, work, and temperature. The rule was found by experiment long before atoms were accepted, and experiment has never contradicted it.

# Properties

#### *(Direction, Not Amount)* The second law adds a direction to the first law's bookkeeping.

The first law is an equation, and equations run both ways. The second law is an inequality. It says which way a process runs when left alone: heat downhill in temperature, gases into empty space, a stirred liquid toward rest and never the reverse. Every process that occurs spontaneously is one the law permits, and every process it forbids fails to occur, however carefully the energy would balance.

#### *(Reverse Flow Costs Work)* Heat can be moved from cold to hot, but only by spending work.

The Clausius statement forbids the reverse flow when it happens *spontaneously*. A [[2.7.5a Refrigerator|refrigerator]] moves heat from its cold interior to the warm kitchen every day, and pays for it with electrical work. The second law fixes the minimum price: the work can never be zero, and the least work that any device can manage between two given temperatures is worked out in the [[2.7.5a Refrigerator|refrigerator]] note.

#### *(A Limit on Engines)* No engine can turn heat into work completely, and the fraction it can convert depends only on the temperatures it works between.

A heat engine takes in $Q_H$ at $T_H$ and must reject some $Q_L$ at a lower $T_L$. Its efficiency $1 - Q_L / Q_H$ is therefore below one. The second law goes further and caps the efficiency at a value set by $T_H$ and $T_L$ alone, whatever the working substance and however the engine is built. The [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot engine]] note derives that value. The internal energy of the air in a room, some $10^{7}\ \text{J}$, cannot run a machine, because there is nothing colder to reject heat into.

#### *(Statistical Origin)* The law is a statement about large numbers, and it can fail for a handful of molecules.

For $10^{22}$ molecules the spontaneous unmixing of a gas or the flow of heat from cold to hot has a probability so small that it never happens in the lifetime of the universe. For five molecules in a box the same events happen all the time. The law is exact for macroscopic bodies because the number of particles is enormous, not because the reverse is impossible in principle. This is the sense in which [[2.7.6 Entropy|entropy]], when it is defined later in the section, is a measure of disorder or of the number of ways the molecules can be arranged.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Coffee Cup and a Room
>A cup of coffee at $60\ ^\circ\text{C}$ sits in a room at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- Which of the following are forbidden by the first law, by the second law, or by neither: the coffee cools to $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$; the coffee warms to $80\ ^\circ\text{C}$ while the room cools slightly; the coffee stays at $60\ ^\circ\text{C}$ while the room warms?
>>Cooling to room temperature conserves energy and moves heat from hot to cold. Neither law forbids it, and it is what happens. Warming to $80\ ^\circ\text{C}$ by taking heat from the cooler room conserves energy, so the first law allows it, but it moves heat from cold to hot on its own, which the Clausius statement forbids. The third option creates energy in the room from nothing, and the first law forbids it. The second law is the one that decides between processes that both conserve energy.

>[!example] (Medium) A Ship Powered by the Sea
>The oceans hold about $10^{24}\ \text{J}$ of internal energy above the freezing point. An inventor proposes a ship that draws seawater in, extracts heat from it, turns the heat into propulsion, and returns the water a little colder.
>

>\end{tikzpicture}

>
>>[!example]- Does the proposal violate the first law, the second law, or neither?
>>The first law is satisfied: the energy for the propulsion comes out of the water's internal energy, and the water cools by the corresponding amount. The proposal is nonetheless impossible. The ship is a cyclic device that takes heat from a single reservoir, the sea, and converts all of it to work, which the Kelvin-Planck statement forbids. To run, it would also need a colder reservoir to reject heat into, and at sea there is none. The energy is there, but energy alone is not enough. What an engine needs is a temperature difference, and the ocean surface has only one temperature. The same objection rules out running a car on the heat of the surrounding air.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not show that the two statements are equivalent. This argument is standard textbook material.

>[!example] (Hard) Equivalence of Two Statements
>Suppose a device existed that took heat from a single reservoir and turned all of it into work, in violation of the Kelvin-Planck statement.
>
>>[!example]- Show that it could be used to move heat from cold to hot with no net work, in violation of the Clausius statement.
>>Let the device take $Q$ from the cold reservoir at $T_L$ and produce work $W = Q$. Use that work to drive an ordinary refrigerator between the same two reservoirs, a device that moves heat from cold to hot when supplied with work, as a kitchen refrigerator does and as the [[2.7.5a Refrigerator|refrigerator]] note describes. The refrigerator takes some $Q'$ from the cold reservoir and delivers $Q' + W$ to the hot reservoir. Over a full cycle of both machines, the cold reservoir has lost $Q + Q'$ and the hot reservoir has gained $Q' + W = Q' + Q$. No work entered from outside, since the refrigerator ran on the device's own output. The combination has moved heat $Q + Q'$ from cold to hot spontaneously, which the Clausius statement forbids. So a violation of one statement produces a violation of the other. The argument runs the other way too, and the two statements stand or fall together.
