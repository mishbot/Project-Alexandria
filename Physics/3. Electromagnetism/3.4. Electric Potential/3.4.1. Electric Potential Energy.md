>[!note] Electric Potential Energy
>The electric force does work on a charge that moves through a field. Take a uniform field $\vec E = E\,\hat x$, like the field between two large [[3.3.3b Parallel Conducting Plates|parallel plates]]. A small test charge $q_0$ moves from $x_1$ to $x_2$. It is small enough to leave the charges on the plates undisturbed. Along any path $C$ from $x_1$ to $x_2$, the force $\vec F = q_0E\,\hat x$ does the [[1.3a Work|work]]
>$$W = \int_C \vec F\cdot d\vec s = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} q_0E\,dx = q_0E\,(x_2 - x_1).$$
>Here $d\vec s$ is a small step along the path. Only the displacement along the field enters the result.
>- The work is the same for every path between the two points. It is **path independent**.
>- A force with path-independent work is **conservative**. The electric force of charges at rest is always conservative.
>
>A conservative force has a [[1.3d.1 Potential Energy|potential energy]]. The **electric potential energy** $U$ is defined by
>$$\Delta U = U_2 - U_1 = -W.$$
>For the field $\vec E = E\,\hat x$ above, this gives
>$$U(x) = -q_0Ex + C,$$
>where $C$ is a constant. Only changes in $U$ are physical, so $C$ is a free choice. The choice is made by picking a **zero of potential energy**, a point where $U = 0$. Any point works unless $U$ is infinite there.
>
>Put the positive plate at $x = 0$ and the negative plate at $x = d$, so $\vec E$ points along $+x$. There are four graphs of $U$ against $x$, all straight lines:
>- $q_0 > 0$, zero at the positive plate: $U = -q_0Ex$ falls from $0$ to $-q_0Ed$.
>- $q_0 > 0$, zero at the negative plate: $U = q_0E(d - x)$ falls from $q_0Ed$ to $0$.
>- $q_0 < 0$, zero at the positive plate: $U = -q_0Ex$ rises from $0$ to $|q_0|Ed$.
>- $q_0 < 0$, zero at the negative plate: $U = q_0E(d - x)$ rises from $-|q_0|Ed$ to $0$.
>
>The sign of $q_0$ sets the slope. The choice of zero shifts the whole line up or down.

# Intuition

A uniform electric field acts on a charge the way gravity near the ground acts on a mass. Gravity does work $mgh$ on a mass that falls a height $h$, whatever path it takes. A uniform field does work $q_0E\,\Delta x$ on a charge, whatever path it takes. In both cases the work depends only on the start and end points. That is the condition for a potential energy to exist.

A positive charge moving along $\vec E$ loses potential energy, as a falling mass does. A negative charge feels a force opposite to $\vec E$. Moving along $\vec E$, it has negative work done on it, and its potential energy grows. Gravity has no case like this, because mass has only one sign.

The value of $U$ at one point means nothing by itself. Adding the same constant everywhere leaves every difference in $U$ unchanged. Work and force depend only on differences, so no measurement can detect the constant. The zero is picked once at the start of each problem and kept to the end.

A charge follows the three paths of the Medium example, one at a time. A running tally adds the work of each step. Every path ends with $W = 2.4\ \mu\text{J}$.

# Derivation

Put the $x$-axis along the field. A small step $d\vec s = dx\,\hat x + dy\,\hat y + dz\,\hat z$ has the work
$$dW = \vec F\cdot d\vec s = q_0E\,\hat x\cdot d\vec s = q_0E\,dx.$$
The dot product keeps only the part of the step along the field. Here $q_0$ and $E$ are constants. Adding the steps along $C$,
$$W = q_0E\int_{x_1}^{x_2} dx = q_0E\,(x_2 - x_1).$$
The steps in $y$ and $z$ dropped out, so every path between the two points gives this result.

Now test the function $U(x) = -q_0Ex + C$ against the definition $\Delta U = -W$:
$$U(x_2) - U(x_1) = (-q_0Ex_2 + C) - (-q_0Ex_1 + C) = -q_0E\,(x_2 - x_1) = -W.$$
The constant $C$ cancels in the difference, so no calculation of work can fix it.

# Properties

#### *(Only Differences Are Physical)* Adding a constant to $U$ changes no prediction.

Work and force come from differences in $U$, so a constant added to $U$ changes neither. The total energy $K + U$ also shifts by that constant, and its changes stay the same. [[1.3c Work-Energy Principle|Energy conservation]] also uses only changes. The one forbidden choice is a point where $U$ is infinite. Setting $U = 0$ there would need an infinite constant $C$. Every other point would then have an infinite potential energy.

#### *(Source and Test Charge)* The potential energy depends on the charges that make the field and on the charge placed in it.

The plates are the source charges, and they make the field. The test charge $q_0$ responds to it. The potential energy depends on both. That is why the sign of $q_0$ flips the slope of the graph. Each new test charge needs its own graph of $U$. Dividing $U$ by $q_0$ removes the test charge and leaves a quantity set by the plates alone. That quantity is the [[3.4.2. Electric Potential|electric potential]].

#### *(Released From Rest)* A charge released from rest in a field moves toward lower potential energy.

With only the electric force acting, $K + U$ stays constant, so $\Delta K = -\Delta U$. The kinetic energy starts at zero and cannot become negative. So $U$ can only decrease from its starting value. A positive charge therefore moves along $\vec E$, and a negative charge moves against it. Both lose potential energy and gain the same amount of kinetic energy.

>[!example] (Easy) Proton Moved Along a Field
>A proton moves $2.0\ \text{cm}$ in the direction of a uniform field of $5.0 \times 10^{4}\ \text{N/C}$.

>>The potential energy changes by $\Delta U = -W = -1.6 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{J}$. The field pushes the proton in the direction it moves, so its potential energy falls.

>- Path A is the straight line.
>- Path B goes up the $y$-axis to $(0, 3.0\ \text{cm})$, then across to the end point.
>- Path C goes along the $x$-axis to $x = 10\ \text{cm}$, back to $x = 4.0\ \text{cm}$, then up.
>
>>[!example]- Find the work done by the field along each path.

>>- Path A has a total change $\Delta x = 0.040\ \text{m}$.
>>- Path B: the vertical leg gives zero, and the horizontal leg has $\Delta x = 0.040\ \text{m}$.

>>
>>Every path has a net $\Delta x = 0.040\ \text{m}$, so every path gives
>>$$W = q_0E\,(0.040) = (2.0 \times 10^{-9})(3.0 \times 10^{4})(0.040) = 2.4 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{J}.$$
>>On path C, the negative work of the return leg cancels part of the outward leg's work.

>[!example] (Hard) An Electron Between Plates
>Two large plates sit $6.0\ \text{mm}$ apart with a uniform field of $5.0 \times 10^{4}\ \text{N/C}$ between them. The positive plate is at $x = 0$, and the field points along $+x$. An electron starts at rest at $x = 5.0\ \text{mm}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the electron's potential energy at $x = 5.0\ \text{mm}$ and at $x = 1.0\ \text{mm}$ for both choices of zero. Then find its speed at $x = 1.0\ \text{mm}$.
>>The electron has $q_0 = -e$, so $U = eEx + C$. This rises with $x$, so the electron moves toward smaller $x$, toward the positive plate. With the zero at the positive plate, $C = 0$ and
>>$$U(5.0\ \text{mm}) = (1.602 \times 10^{-19})(5.0 \times 10^{4})(0.0050) = 4.0 \times 10^{-17}\ \text{J}, \qquad U(1.0\ \text{mm}) = 8.0 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}.$$
>>With the zero at the negative plate, $U(d) = 0$ gives $C = -eEd$, so $U = eE(x - d)$:
>>$$U(5.0\ \text{mm}) = -8.0 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}, \qquad U(1.0\ \text{mm}) = -4.0 \times 10^{-17}\ \text{J}.$$
>>Both choices give $\Delta U = -3.2 \times 10^{-17}\ \text{J}$. The electron gains that much kinetic energy. With the electron mass $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$, its speed is
>>$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2\,|\Delta U|}{m_e}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(3.2 \times 10^{-17})}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}} = 8.4 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m/s}.$$
