>[!note] Electric Potential
>The **electric potential** $V$ at a point is the potential energy per unit charge of a small test charge there. A test charge $q_0$ moves from point A to point B. Its [[3.4.1. Electric Potential Energy|potential energy]] changes by $\Delta U$. The **potential difference** between the points is
>$$V_B - V_A = \frac{\Delta U}{q_0}.$$
>The test charge divides out, so $V$ depends only on the charges that make the field. A potential difference is also called a **voltage**. The unit of potential is the **volt**,
>$$1\ \text{V} = 1\ \text{J/C}.$$
>The field unit can then be written as $1\ \text{N/C} = 1\ \text{V/m}$.
>
>Potential is to potential energy as field is to force. A test charge $q_0$ placed in the field has
>$$\vec F = q_0\vec E, \qquad U = q_0V, \qquad \Delta U = q_0\,\Delta V.$$
>The potential is a **scalar**, a number with a sign and no direction. Like $U$, it needs a zero, a reference point where $V = 0$.
>
>In a uniform field $E\,\hat x$ between [[3.3.3b Parallel Conducting Plates|parallel plates]], put the positive plate at $x = 0$ and the negative plate at $x = d$:
>- With the zero at the positive plate, $V = -Ex$.
>- With the zero at the negative plate, $V = E(d - x)$.
>
>Both lines have slope $-E$, whatever the test charge. The potential falls in the direction of the field. A charge released from rest moves toward lower potential energy, and $\Delta U = q_0\,\Delta V$ turns this into a rule for $V$:
>- A positive charge moves toward lower potential.
>- A negative charge moves toward higher potential.
>
>The name causes confusion. "Electric potential" is often shortened to "potential", which sounds like "potential energy". The two are different quantities. $V$ is measured in volts and is set by the source charges alone. $U$ is measured in joules and also depends on the charge placed in the field.
>
>The **electron-volt** is a unit of energy for single particles. It is the energy an electron gains when it crosses a potential difference of $1\ \text{V}$:
>$$1\ \text{eV} = (1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C})(1\ \text{V}) = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}.$$

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not define the electron-volt. It follows Giancoli section 23.8.

# Intuition

Gravity near the ground gives a useful comparison. A mass $m$ at height $h$ has $U = mgh$. The product $gh$ is the energy per kilogram, the same for every mass. Electric potential is the same idea for charge. It is the energy per coulomb, the same for every test charge. A mass released from rest moves to lower $gh$. A positive charge released from rest moves to lower $V$.

The comparison fails for negative charges, since mass has only one sign. A negative charge has $U = q_0V$ with $q_0 < 0$. Its potential energy is lowest where $V$ is highest, so it moves toward higher potential. The potential is the same for both signs of charge. Only the factor $q_0$ in $U = q_0V$ differs.

The potential has the same advantage over $U$ that $\vec E$ has over $\vec F$. It is computed once from the source charges. Multiplying by any test charge $q_0$ then gives its potential energy.

The plates are those of the Hard example, with $V = 0$ at the negative plate. Charges $q_0$ and $2q_0$ at one point have the same $V$ and different $U$. Then a positive and a negative charge are released from rest at the midpoint.

# Properties

#### *(Voltage Versus Energy)* A large potential difference can deliver a small energy, because the energy also depends on the charge.

A charge of $1.0\ \text{nC}$ crossing $10{,}000\ \text{V}$ changes its energy by $q_0\,\Delta V = 1.0 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{J}$. A charge of $1.0\ \text{C}$ crossing $12\ \text{V}$ changes its energy by $12\ \text{J}$. That is over a million times more energy, at a voltage over 800 times smaller.

#### *(Speed From a Voltage)* A charge accelerated from rest through a potential difference $\Delta V$ gains the kinetic energy $|q_0|\,|\Delta V|$.

Energy conservation gives $K = -\Delta U = -q_0\,\Delta V$ for a charge that starts at rest. The charge moves in the direction that makes this positive. Its final speed is $v = \sqrt{2|q_0|\,|\Delta V|/m}$. Particles with the same size of charge gain the same energy across the same voltage, whatever their mass. The lighter particle reaches the higher speed.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Energy Change From a Voltage
>A $+3.0\ \text{nC}$ charge moves from a point at $+120\ \text{V}$ to a point at $-80\ \text{V}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the change in its potential energy and the work done by the field.
>>The potential difference is $\Delta V = -80 - 120 = -200\ \text{V}$. Then
>>$$\Delta U = q_0\,\Delta V = (3.0 \times 10^{-9})(-200) = -6.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{J}.$$
>>The field does the work $W = -\Delta U = +6.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{J}$. A positive charge moving to lower potential loses potential energy.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The next example uses the electron-volt, from Giancoli section 23.8.

>[!example] (Medium) Electron Gun
>An electron starts at rest and moves through a potential difference of $500\ \text{V}$ toward higher potential.
>
>>[!example]- Find its kinetic energy in joules and in electron-volts, and its speed.
>>The electron has $q_0 = -e$ and $\Delta V = +500\ \text{V}$, so
>>$$\Delta U = q_0\,\Delta V = -(1.602 \times 10^{-19})(500) = -8.01 \times 10^{-17}\ \text{J}.$$
>>It gains the kinetic energy $K = 8.01 \times 10^{-17}\ \text{J}$, which is $500\ \text{eV}$. With the electron mass $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$, its speed is
>>$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2K}{m_e}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(8.01 \times 10^{-17})}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}} = 1.33 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m/s}.$$

>[!example] (Hard) Proton and Electron Between Plates
>Two large parallel plates sit $8.0\ \text{mm}$ apart, with a potential difference of $400\ \text{V}$ between them. A proton and an electron are each released from rest at the midpoint. The proton mass is $m_p = 1.673 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$, and the electron mass is $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$.
>
>>[!example]- Which way does each move, and how fast is each going when it reaches a plate?
>>Put the positive plate at $x = 0$ and the zero of potential at the negative plate, so $V = E(d - x)$. The positive plate sits at $V = Ed = 400\ \text{V}$, so $E = 400/0.0080 = 5.0 \times 10^{4}\ \text{V/m}$. The potential falls linearly, so the midpoint is at $200\ \text{V}$.
>>- The proton moves toward lower potential, to the negative plate. It crosses $\Delta V = -200\ \text{V}$.
>>- The electron moves toward higher potential, to the positive plate. It crosses $\Delta V = +200\ \text{V}$.
>>
>>Each loses $|q_0\,\Delta V| = (1.602 \times 10^{-19})(200) = 3.20 \times 10^{-17}\ \text{J}$ of potential energy and gains that much kinetic energy. The speeds are
>>$$v_p = \sqrt{\frac{2(3.20 \times 10^{-17})}{1.673 \times 10^{-27}}} = 1.96 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m/s}, \qquad v_e = \sqrt{\frac{2(3.20 \times 10^{-17})}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}}} = 8.39 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m/s}.$$
>>The kinetic energies are equal. The electron is $1836$ times lighter, so it moves $\sqrt{1836} \approx 43$ times faster.
