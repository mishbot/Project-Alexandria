>[!note] Controllability
>An LTI system $\dot{x} = Ax + Bu$ is **controllable** iff, for any initial state $x_0$ and any target state $x_1$, there exists a finite time $T$ and input $u(\cdot)$ on $[0, T]$ that drives the system from $x(0) = x_0$ to $x(T) = x_1$.
>
>The **Kalman rank condition**: the system is controllable iff the **controllability matrix**
>$$\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} B & AB & A^2 B & \cdots & A^{n-1} B \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times nm}$$
>has full row rank, $\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{C}) = n$.
>
>Equivalent statements ("$(A, B)$ is controllable") include: (i) the Kalman rank test above; (ii) the **PBH test**: $\mathrm{rank}[\lambda I - A \mid B] = n$ for every eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $A$; (iii) the controllability Gramian $W_c(T) = \int_0^T e^{At} B B^T e^{A^T t} dt$ is nonsingular for every $T > 0$ (continuous time). A system that fails any of these is **uncontrollable** — there are states it cannot reach from the origin regardless of input.
>
>Controllability is the precondition for **pole placement**: $(A, B)$ controllable iff, for any desired closed-loop spectrum $\{\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n\}$ (closed under complex conjugation), there exists a feedback gain $K$ with $\mathrm{spec}(A - BK) = \{\lambda_i\}$. Uncontrollable systems have **uncontrollable modes** — eigenvalues of $A$ that no choice of $K$ can move.
>
>The dual concept is **observability**: $(A, C)$ is observable iff the state can be reconstructed from input and output history. The observability matrix $\mathcal{O} = [C^T \mid A^T C^T \mid \cdots \mid (A^T)^{n-1} C^T]^T$ plays the analogous role, with the same rank condition.
>
>>[!warning] Controllability Is Binary; Reality Is Gradual
>>The rank test is all-or-nothing. In practice, a system with $\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{C}) = n$ but with $\mathcal{C}$ nearly rank-deficient is *barely* controllable — reaching states in the near-null-space takes enormous input. The **controllability Gramian** $W_c$ quantifies this: its smallest singular value is the energy required to reach the most controllable direction; the largest is the energy for the least. A well-conditioned $W_c$ means the system is controllable in a robust, practical sense.

# Intuition
Controllability asks: can the input move the system anywhere in state space? For a scalar system, this is trivial — as long as $B \neq 0$, the input can push $x$ up or down to any target. In higher dimensions, the question is whether the input's influence, propagated forward through the dynamics, can span the entire state space.

The controllability matrix $\mathcal{C}$ is the geometric answer. Its columns are the state directions reachable from the input, directly ($B$) and at each step of propagation through $A$:
- $B$: where the input pushes right now.
- $AB$: where that push is "rotated" by the dynamics one instant later.
- $A^2 B$: two instants later.
- …

If these span $\mathbb{R}^n$, every direction is reachable — the input's influence, after being shaped by $A$, can be pointed anywhere. If they span only a proper subspace, the complement is an **uncontrollable subspace** — initial conditions in it stay in it, regardless of input. The Cayley–Hamilton theorem says $A^n$ is a linear combination of $\{I, A, \ldots, A^{n-1}\}$, so more than $n-1$ powers add nothing: $\mathcal{C}$ is $n \times nm$, not infinite.

The PBH test provides an eigenvalue-level picture: a mode is uncontrollable iff its eigenvector is orthogonal to every column of $B$. Geometrically, an uncontrollable mode is one whose eigendirection cannot be excited by any input, so no feedback can affect it. Such a mode evolves according to its intrinsic rate $\lambda$, and if that rate is unstable, no controller can stabilize it.

# Properties

## Kalman Rank Condition

#### *(Statement)* Controllability iff $\mathrm{rank}[B \mid AB \mid \cdots \mid A^{n-1} B] = n$.

The proof rests on the Cayley–Hamilton theorem: $A^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} c_k A^k$ for coefficients $c_k$ from the characteristic polynomial. Consequently, $e^{At} B = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} g_k(t) A^k B$ for some scalar functions $g_k$, so the reachable set is $\mathrm{range}([B \mid AB \mid \cdots \mid A^{n-1} B])$. Full rank means reachable = entire $\mathbb{R}^n$.

#### *(Computation)* Build $\mathcal{C}$ column-by-column, stop early if rank saturates.

For $\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{C}_k) = \mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{C}_{k-1})$ at some step $k \leq n-1$, further powers add no new columns and you can stop. This speeds up large systems and is the basis for iterative Krylov-subspace tests. Numerical caution: use SVD to compute rank, not floating-point determinants.

#### *(Single input)* For $m = 1$, $\mathcal{C}$ is $n \times n$ and controllability iff $\det(\mathcal{C}) \neq 0$.

In this case, $K$ is uniquely determined by the desired spectrum, and the pole-placement formula is **Ackermann's formula**: $K = [0, \ldots, 0, 1] \mathcal{C}^{-1} \phi_d(A)$, where $\phi_d$ is the desired characteristic polynomial. Numerically ill-conditioned for large $n$; prefer `place` over `acker` in practice.

## Equivalent Characterizations

#### *(PBH test)* Controllability iff $\mathrm{rank}[\lambda I - A \mid B] = n$ for every eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $A$.

Eigenvalue-by-eigenvalue: mode $\lambda$ is controllable iff $[\lambda I - A \mid B]$ has full row rank, iff no left eigenvector of $A$ (for eigenvalue $\lambda$) is orthogonal to $B$. Useful for identifying *which* specific modes are uncontrollable when the Kalman test fails. Numerically better-conditioned than the Kalman rank test for large systems.

#### *(Gramian)* Controllability iff $W_c(T) = \int_0^T e^{At} BB^T e^{A^T t} dt$ is positive definite for any $T > 0$.

The Gramian is the identity-scaled covariance of the reachable set. Its inverse gives the minimum-energy input to reach a target: $u^*(t) = B^T e^{A^T(T-t)} W_c^{-1}(T) (x_1 - e^{AT} x_0)$, with total energy $\|u^*\|^2 = (x_1 - e^{AT} x_0)^T W_c^{-1}(T) (x_1 - e^{AT} x_0)$. Eigenvalues of $W_c$ that are small indicate directions that require large control effort.

#### *(Modal decomposition)* $A = V \Lambda V^{-1}$; $(A, B)$ controllable iff every row of $V^{-1} B$ is nonzero.

In eigenbasis coordinates $z = V^{-1} x$, the system decouples: $\dot{z}_i = \lambda_i z_i + (V^{-1} B)_i u$. Mode $i$ is controllable iff its input-channel row is nonzero. An all-zero row means the input has no direct handle on that mode, and its eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ is an uncontrollable pole.

## Uncontrollability

#### *(Uncontrollable modes)* Eigenvalues of $A$ not appearing as closed-loop eigenvalues of $A - BK$ for any $K$.

An uncontrollable eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ is fixed — it always appears in the spectrum of $A - BK$, regardless of $K$. If $\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_i) \geq 0$, the system cannot be stabilized. **Stabilizability** is the weaker condition that all unstable modes are controllable; a stabilizable system can be driven to the origin (but cannot have arbitrary closed-loop dynamics).

#### *(Kalman decomposition)* Any $(A, B)$ decomposes into controllable and uncontrollable parts.

Change of coordinates partitions the state into $x = (x_c, x_{\bar c})$ where $x_c$ is controllable and $x_{\bar c}$ is not, with $A = \begin{bmatrix} A_c & A_{12} \\ 0 & A_{\bar c} \end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix} B_c \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. The uncontrollable subsystem $\dot{x}_{\bar c} = A_{\bar c} x_{\bar c}$ evolves autonomously; only $A_c$'s eigenvalues can be moved by feedback.

#### *(Practical causes of uncontrollability)* Hidden symmetry, un-actuated modes, or model simplifications.

Examples: a symmetric mechanical system with inputs only at symmetric pairs of joints cannot excite asymmetric modes; a robot with one missing joint torque loses controllability of modes requiring that axis; model reduction that averages out fast modes makes them uncontrollable in the reduced model. Identifying the physical cause of uncontrollability is often more useful than re-designing the controller.

## Duality with Observability

#### *(Dual pairs)* $(A, B)$ controllable iff $(A^T, B^T)$ observable.

The duality swaps inputs and outputs, states and co-states. Every theorem about controllability has a dual for observability: $\mathcal{O}$ plays the role of $\mathcal{C}$; the observability Gramian plays the role of $W_c$; PBH becomes $\mathrm{rank}[(\lambda I - A)^T \mid C^T] = n$. This duality underlies observer design (a feedback in the dual space) and the separation principle.

#### *(Minimal realization)* A transfer function $G(s)$ corresponds to a unique minimal (controllable and observable) state-space realization up to coordinate change.

Non-minimal realizations contain uncontrollable or unobservable modes that do not appear in $G(s)$. The minimal realization has dimension equal to the McMillan degree of $G(s)$. Pole-zero cancellation in $G(s)$ is the transfer-function signature of uncontrollable or unobservable modes.

# Examples
>[!example] Controllability of a Double Integrator
>Is $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ controllable?
>>[!example]- Solution
>>$AB = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$.
>>
>>$\mathcal{C} = [B \mid AB] = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $\det = -1 \neq 0$.
>>
>>**Controllable.** Any desired pole placement is achievable (consistent with the example in [[7.6. State Feedback]]).

>[!example] Uncontrollable System
>Is $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ controllable?
>>[!example]- Solution
>>$AB = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, so $\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, rank $1 < 2$.
>>
>>**Uncontrollable.** Modally, $B$ has a zero component in the eigendirection of $\lambda = 2$: the $x_2$ mode has no input coupling. By the PBH test, $[\lambda I - A \mid B] = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda - 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & \lambda - 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ at $\lambda = 2$ becomes $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, rank $1$ — confirming $\lambda = 2$ is the uncontrollable eigenvalue.
>>
>>Because $\lambda = 2 > 0$, this system is also *unstabilizable*: no $K$ can move $\lambda = 2$ into the LHP.

>[!example] Under-Actuated Two-Mass System
>Two carts connected by a spring, with force applied only to cart $1$: $\ddot{q}_1 = (u - k(q_1 - q_2))/m$, $\ddot{q}_2 = k(q_1 - q_2)/m$. State $x = (q_1, q_2, \dot{q}_1, \dot{q}_2)$. Is this controllable?
>>[!example]- Solution
>>$A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\ -k/m & k/m & 0 & 0 \\ k/m & -k/m & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1/m \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$.
>>
>>$AB = (1/m)(0, 0, 0, 0)^T$… wait, let me redo: $AB$'s fourth entry is $A_{4, :} \cdot B = (k/m)(0) + (-k/m)(0) + (0)(1/m) + (0)(0) = 0$. The third entry is $(-k/m)(0) + (k/m)(0) + (0)(1/m) + (0)(0) = 0$. First two entries come from the identity rows: $AB = (1/m, 0, 0, 0)^T$.
>>
>>$A^2 B = A \cdot AB$; proceeding gives successive nonzero rows in previously-zero positions.
>>
>>Working it out, $\mathcal{C} = [B \mid AB \mid A^2 B \mid A^3 B]$ has rank $4$: the system is **controllable**. Intuition: even though only cart $1$ is actuated, the spring coupling propagates the input to cart $2$ through the dynamics, and four derivatives of the input reach every state. Under-actuation does not, in itself, imply loss of controllability.
