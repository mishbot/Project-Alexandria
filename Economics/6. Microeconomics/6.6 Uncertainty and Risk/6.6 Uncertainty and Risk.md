>[!note] Uncertainty and Risk
>This section extends the consumer model to choices made before some numbers are known, such as next year's income or the return on a stock. [[6.6.1 Expected Utility]] gives the rule for ranking uncertain prospects. A lottery that pays $M_i$ with probability $p_i$ is worth $E(U) = \sum_i p_i\,U(M_i)$, the average of the utilities of its outcomes. This differs from $U(E(M))$, the utility of the average outcome, whenever $U$ is curved. A linear $U$ makes a person risk neutral, a concave $U$ risk averse, and a convex $U$ risk loving.
>
>[[6.6.2 Risk Aversion]] links the two sides through Jensen's inequality: $u$ is concave exactly when the person prefers $E(x)$ to the lottery $x$ for every lottery. The Arrow-Pratt measures $r_A = -u''/u'$ and $r_R = -x\,u''/u'$ say how strong the dislike is. The certainty equivalent and the risk premium $\text{RP} \approx \tfrac12 r_A V$ put it in dollars.
>
>Two applications follow. In [[6.6.3 Insurance]] a person with loss probability $p$ buys coverage at premium $q$ per dollar. At a fair price, $q = p$, she buys full insurance, $\alpha^* = L$. At $q > p$ she buys partial insurance or none. In [[6.6.4 Investment in a Risky Asset]] she splits wealth between a riskless bond and a stock with expected excess return $Er > 0$. A risk-neutral person puts everything in the stock. A risk-averse person always holds a positive share, because at zero risk the gain in mean is first order and the cost of risk is second order. The same slope-at-zero test explains both results.

# Contents

- [[6.6.1 Expected Utility]]: lotteries, expected value and variance, expected utility versus the utility of the expected value, and the three cases for the sign of $U''$. **Not taught:** uniqueness of $U$ up to positive affine transformations.
- [[6.6.2 Risk Aversion]]: Jensen's inequality, diminishing marginal utility, and the Arrow-Pratt measures $r_A$ and $r_R$. **Not taught:** certainty equivalent, risk premium and its small-risk approximation, invariance of $r_A$ and $r_R$, fixed versus proportional gambles.
- [[6.6.3 Insurance]]: coverage choice, the first- and second-order conditions, full insurance at a fair price, and partial insurance at an unfair price. **Not taught:** the two-state diagram, the condition for buying nothing, and the effect of $q$ on coverage.
- [[6.6.4 Investment in a Risky Asset]]: the bond-stock split, $\alpha^* = 1$ under risk neutrality, and $\alpha^* > 0$ under risk aversion. **Not taught:** the upper corner $\alpha^* = 1$ for a risk-averse person, the case $Er \le 0$, the log closed form, the approximation $\alpha^* \approx Er/(r_R V(r))$, the link between wealth and the share, and the comparison with insurance.

# Recommended Path

Read [[6.6.1 Expected Utility]] first, then [[6.6.2 Risk Aversion]], since both applications use the chord picture and the curvature of $u$. [[6.6.3 Insurance]] and [[6.6.4 Investment in a Risky Asset]] have the same structure, one choice variable with a strictly concave objective. Read insurance first because its full-insurance result needs no functional form. If short on time, read the four callouts, the Medium example of 6.6.1, and the Easy examples of the two applications. Then work the Hard example of 6.6.4, which checks a corner, and the property *(Slope at Zero)* in the same note, which ties the two models together.
