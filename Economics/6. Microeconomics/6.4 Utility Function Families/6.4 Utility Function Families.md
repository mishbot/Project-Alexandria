>[!note] Utility Function Families
>Four **utility function families** cover most two-good problems in consumer theory. Each is a function $u : \mathbb{R}^2_+ \to \mathbb{R}$ of a bundle $(x_1, x_2)$, with weights $\alpha, \beta > 0$ on the two goods. The families are [[6.4a Cobb-Douglas]] $x_1^{\alpha}x_2^{1-\alpha}$, [[6.4b Perfect Substitutes|perfect substitutes]] $\alpha x_1 + \beta x_2$, [[6.4c Perfect Complements|perfect complements]] $\min(\alpha x_1, \beta x_2)$, and [[6.4d CES Utility|CES]] $(\alpha x_1^{\rho} + \beta x_2^{\rho})^{1/\rho}$, which adds a curvature parameter $\rho < 1$, $\rho \neq 0$. Each family has closed-form [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|Marshallian demands]] $x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)$ at prices $\mathbf{p} = (p_1, p_2)$ and income $M$. In all four, demand is proportional to $M$ and [[6.3.2 Homogeneity of Demand|homogeneous of degree zero]] in $(\mathbf{p}, M)$.
>
>The families differ in how easily the consumer substitutes one good for the other. An economist picks a family by asking how the consumer uses the two goods. Goods used in fixed proportions, such as left and right shoes, are perfect complements. Goods that do the same job at a fixed exchange rate, such as the same brand of batteries from two stores, are perfect substitutes. Goods on which the consumer spends a constant share of income are Cobb-Douglas, which is also the default when nothing else is known. CES is the right choice when the degree of substitutability is unknown or is the object of study. The CES family contains Cobb-Douglas and perfect substitutes as special cases, and perfect complements with equal weights as a limit.
>
>Each family has its own solution method. Solve Cobb-Douglas and CES with the tangency condition $\text{MRS} = -p_1/p_2$. Solve perfect substitutes by comparing the two [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions|corners]]. Solve perfect complements by placing the bundle on the kink ray $\alpha x_1 = \beta x_2$ and on the budget line.

# Intuition

The four families differ in how far the consumer moves toward good 2, at fixed utility, when good 1 becomes relatively more expensive. A consumer with perfect complements does not move at all, because the recipe is fixed. A consumer with perfect substitutes moves all the way to a corner once the price ratio crosses the exchange rate. A Cobb-Douglas consumer moves by the amount that keeps spending shares fixed. CES covers this whole range with one parameter. As $\rho$ falls from $1$ toward $-\infty$, its indifference curves bend more sharply and the consumer substitutes less.

The special cases give short formulas and sharp predictions. Each one, however, fixes the degree of substitution before the economist sees any data. With CES an economist can estimate $\rho$ from data, at the price of a longer demand formula. The diagram gives each family equal weights and the same budget line, $3x_1 + 4x_2 = 12$. The Cobb-Douglas and CES consumers choose interior tangency points. The perfect-substitutes consumer buys only good 1, the cheaper good. The perfect-complements consumer buys equal amounts at the kink, $x_1^* = x_2^* = 12/7$.

# When to Use Which

Start by asking whether one good can do the other's job. If a unit of good 1 always replaces the same number of units of good 2, use perfect substitutes. A baker who buys the same grade of flour from two mills is this case with $\alpha = \beta$. The baker buys all the flour from the cheaper mill. Next ask whether the goods are useful only together, in a fixed recipe. If so, use perfect complements. The same baker's flour and yeast for one bread recipe fit here, since extra flour without yeast makes no extra bread. The weights come from the recipe. A recipe with $a$ units of good 1 and $b$ units of good 2 per loaf gives $u = \min(x_1/a, x_2/b)$.

If neither extreme fits, use Cobb-Douglas when the consumer keeps spending shares roughly constant as prices move. It is the default in most problems, because its demands are short and always interior. Switch to CES when the answer depends on how strongly the consumer substitutes, or when data should decide it. With $0 < \rho < 1$ a rise in one price raises demand for the other good, and with $\rho < 0$ it lowers it.

Recognize the family before solving. A strictly increasing transformation of $u$ leaves the preferences unchanged, so it also leaves the family unchanged. For example, $\ln x_1 + 2\ln x_2$ and $x_1x_2^2$ are both Cobb-Douglas with $\alpha = 1/3$. The function $5\min(x_1, x_2)$ describes perfect complements, and $3x_1 + 3x_2 + 7$ describes perfect substitutes. The function $(\sqrt{x_1} + \sqrt{x_2})^2$ is CES with $\alpha = \beta = 1$ and $\rho = 1/2$.
