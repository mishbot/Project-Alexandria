>[!note] Forward Contract
>A **forward contract** is a legal obligation to trade an asset on a future date at a price fixed today. The buyer holds the **long** position and must buy. The seller holds the **short** position and must sell. The two parties agree on the contract directly, over the counter (OTC), so they can set every term. A forward fixes five terms:
>- The **underlying** is the asset traded, such as a stock, a bond, a foreign currency, a commodity, or a cryptocurrency.
>- The **contract size** is the amount of the underlying traded, such as 1,000 shares.
>- The **maturity date** $T$ is the date on which the trade takes place.
>- The **delivery price** $K$ is the price per unit that the long pays at $T$.
>- The **settlement method** says whether the asset itself changes hands or only cash does.
>
>Let $S_T$ be the price of the underlying at $T$. Per unit of the underlying, the payoffs at $T$ are
>$$\text{long: } S_T - K, \qquad \text{short: } K - S_T.$$
>The long gains when the asset ends above $K$, and the short gains when it ends below $K$. The two payoffs sum to zero in every state. The parties choose $K$ so that the contract is worth zero on the day they sign it. Neither side pays anything to enter. This choice of $K$ is the forward price $F_0$, derived in [[2.2.2b Forward Price]]. Since nothing is paid at the start, the payoff at $T$ is also the profit.
>
>A forward settles in one of two ways.
>- Under **physical settlement**, the long pays $K$ per unit and the short delivers the asset.
>- Under **cash settlement**, the losing side pays the winning side $|S_T - K|$ per unit, and no asset changes hands.
>
>Both methods give each side the same payoff. Each side bears counterparty risk, the risk that the other side fails to pay or deliver. A [[2.2.3 Futures|futures contract]] is a forward with standard terms traded on an exchange. An [[2.3.1a Option Contract|option]] gives the right to trade at $K$ without the obligation. [[2.2.2c Value of a Forward Contract]] gives the value of a forward after the day it is signed.

>[!warning] Names for $K$
>Many texts call $K$ the forward price, and some call it the strike. These notes keep $K$ for the price written into the contract. They keep $F_0$ for the price that makes a new contract worth zero. The two are equal only on the signing date.

# Intuition

In trade, payment often comes months after the deal. A US importer who orders goods priced in yen may pay for them three months later. Over those months the exchange rate moves, and the dollar cost of the goods moves with it. A forward removes this risk. The importer and a bank agree today on the exchange rate for the payment date. The importer's dollar cost is then known, whatever happens to the yen. The bank takes on the currency risk and can offset it in other markets.

# Properties

#### *(Fixed Delivery Price)* The delivery price stays fixed for the life of the contract, while the forward price changes every day.

On the signing date, $K = F_0$ and the contract is worth zero. A day later the asset price has moved. A new contract for the same date $T$ would use a new forward price $F_t$. The old contract still trades at $K$, so it now has a value. The long's contract gains value when $F_t > K$, since it buys at $K$ what a new buyer would buy at $F_t$. [[2.2.2c Value of a Forward Contract]] derives the size of this value. Parties can also sign an off-market forward, one whose delivery price differs from the forward price. Such a contract has a nonzero value at signing, so one side pays the other that value up front.

#### *(Settlement Mechanics)* Physical and cash settlement give the same payoff, but they move very different amounts of money.

Under physical settlement, the long pays $K$ times the contract size and receives the asset. Under cash settlement, only $|S_T - K|$ times the contract size changes hands. The parties must agree on a reference price for $S_T$, such as a closing price on an exchange. Cash settlement suits an underlying that is hard to deliver, such as a stock index. Physical delivery often takes place a few days after the maturity date, on the **settlement date**. For example, a contract to buy 1,000 shares on a Friday may deliver the share certificates on the following Tuesday.

#### *(Counterparty Risk)* A losing party may fail to pay, and the amount at risk is the cost of replacing the contract.

At $T$ only one side is owed money. If $S_T > K$, the long is owed and bears the risk that the short defaults. If $S_T < K$, the short bears that risk. The side that loses on the contract is the side with a reason to default. Suppose the short defaults on physical delivery and the long pays only on delivery. The long keeps its cash and buys the asset at $S_T$ instead of $K$. It loses $S_T - K$ per unit, not the full $K$. Before $T$, the amount at risk is the contract's current value when that value is positive. OTC trading adds two more problems. A party may struggle to find a counterparty with matching needs. A party that wants to exit early needs the counterparty's consent or a second, offsetting forward. Futures remove these problems with standard contracts traded on an exchange ([[2.2.3 Futures]]).

#### *(Hedging)* A firm that must buy or sell the asset at $T$ fixes its price at $K$ with a forward.

A firm that must buy one unit at $T$ pays $S_T$ in the spot market. If it also holds a long forward, its net cost is
$$S_T - (S_T - K) = K.$$
The price $S_T$ cancels, so the firm knows its cost today. A firm that must sell one unit at $T$ takes the short side and receives $K$ in every state. The hedge removes gains as well as losses. A hedger that loses on the forward has still reached its goal of a known price. The forward cancels the risk exactly only when it matches the exposure in asset, date, and amount. Any mismatch leaves some risk.

#### *(Unequal Loss Limits)* The long can lose at most $K$ per unit, while the short's loss has no limit.

The asset price cannot fall below zero, so the worst payoff of the long is $-K$, at $S_T = 0$. The asset price has no upper bound, so the short can lose any amount. A forward costs nothing to enter. A speculator therefore takes the full gain or loss on the asset without paying anything today. A percentage return on the forward cannot be computed, because the amount invested at signing is zero. A speculator who expects a fall takes the short side and faces the unlimited loss.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Payoffs for Both Sides
>A fund agrees to buy 2,000 shares of a stock from a dealer in six months at $K = \$73.50$ per share. The contract uses physical settlement. All cash flows occur at $T$, so no interest rate is needed.
>
>>[!example]- Find each side's payoff if the stock ends at $\$61.25$ or $\$86.00$, and the cash paid under cash settlement.
>>The fund is long and the dealer is short. Per share the fund receives $S_T - 73.50$. Under physical settlement the fund pays $2{,}000 \times 73.50 = \$147{,}000$ in both cases and receives the shares.
>>
>>| $S_T$ | Fund pays | Shares worth | Fund (long) | Dealer (short) |
>>|---|---|---|---|---|
>>| $\$61.25$ | $\$147{,}000$ | $\$122{,}500$ | $-\$24{,}500$ | $+\$24{,}500$ |
>>| $\$86.00$ | $\$147{,}000$ | $\$172{,}000$ | $+\$25{,}000$ | $-\$25{,}000$ |
>>
>>Under cash settlement no shares change hands. At $\$61.25$ the fund pays the dealer $\$24{,}500$. At $\$86.00$ the dealer pays the fund $\$25{,}000$. A fund that still wants the shares can buy them in the market at $S_T$. At $\$61.25$ it pays $24{,}500 + 122{,}500 = \$147{,}000$ in total. At $\$86.00$ it pays $172{,}000 - 25{,}000 = \$147{,}000$. The two settlement methods leave the fund in the same place.

>[!example] (Medium) Hedging a Payment in Yen
>Andrew imports handcrafted furniture from Japan. A US client has already paid him $\$118{,}000$ for a custom table. In 90 days he must pay his Japanese partner 15,000,000 yen. A bank offers a 90-day forward at 150.00 yen per dollar. Andrew takes the long side to buy the yen. All cash flows occur in 90 days, so no interest rate is needed.
>
>>[!example]- Find Andrew's cost and profit with and without the forward, at 140 or 162 yen per dollar in 90 days.
>>These notes quote a currency in dollars per unit, so the delivery price is $K = 1/150 = \$0.0066667$ per yen. With the forward, Andrew pays
>>$$15{,}000{,}000 \times \frac{1}{150} = \$100{,}000.$$
>>His gross profit is $118{,}000 - 100{,}000 = \$18{,}000$, whatever the exchange rate does. The forward pays $15{,}000{,}000\,(S_T - K)$ dollars, where $S_T$ is the dollar price of one yen at $T$.
>>
>>| Rate at $T$ | Cost without hedge | Forward payoff | Cost with hedge | Profit without hedge |
>>|---|---|---|---|---|
>>| 140 yen per $\$$ | $\$107{,}142.86$ | $+\$7{,}142.86$ | $\$100{,}000$ | $\$10{,}857.14$ |
>>| 162 yen per $\$$ | $\$92{,}592.59$ | $-\$7{,}407.41$ | $\$100{,}000$ | $\$25{,}407.41$ |
>>
>>At 140 yen per dollar the yen is worth more. The unhedged cost rises, and the forward pays Andrew the difference. At 162 yen per dollar the forward loses $\$7{,}407.41$. Andrew earns $\$18{,}000$ instead of the unhedged $\$25{,}407.41$. He gave up the extra $\$7{,}407.41$ to remove the risk of a lower profit. He wanted a known profit, and the forward gave him one.

>[!example] (Hard) Who Bears the Default Risk
>A farmer agrees to sell 40,000 bushels of wheat to a flour mill in six months at $K = \$6.15$ per bushel. The contract uses physical settlement. The mill pays only on delivery. All cash flows occur at $T$, so no interest rate is needed.
>
>>[!example]- Find each side's loss if the other side defaults at $T$, for wheat prices of $\$7.40$ and $\$5.30$. Compare each loss with the notional (the contract size times $K$). Then find the mill's net loss and its effective price per bushel if it recovers $30\%$ of its claim.
>>The notional is $40{,}000 \times 6.15 = \$246{,}000$.
>>
>>At $\$7.40$ the farmer could sell his wheat in the market for $40{,}000 \times 7.40 = \$296{,}000$. Defaulting would earn him $\$50{,}000$ more than delivering. If he defaults, the mill keeps its $\$246{,}000$ and buys wheat at the spot price for $\$296{,}000$. The mill loses $40{,}000 \times (7.40 - 6.15) = \$50{,}000$, which equals its payoff as the long.
>>
>>At $\$5.30$ the mill could buy wheat in the market for $\$212{,}000$. If the mill refuses delivery, the farmer sells at the spot price instead. He loses $40{,}000 \times (6.15 - 5.30) = \$34{,}000$, his payoff as the short.
>>
>>The losses are $50{,}000 / 246{,}000 = 20.3\%$ and $34{,}000 / 246{,}000 = 13.8\%$ of the notional. Each party is exposed only in the states where the contract is in its favor. A cash-settled contract would give the same losses, since the defaulting side would owe the same difference.
>>
>>With a $30\%$ recovery, the mill receives $0.30 \times 50{,}000 = \$15{,}000$ of its $\$50{,}000$ claim. Its net loss is $\$35{,}000$. It paid $\$296{,}000$ for the wheat and recovered $\$15{,}000$, so its effective price is $281{,}000 / 40{,}000 = \$7.03$ per bushel. The forward was meant to fix that price at $\$6.15$.
