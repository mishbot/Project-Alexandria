>[!note] Expenditure Minimization
>In **expenditure minimization** (EMIN) the consumer fixes a target utility $\bar u$ and looks for the cheapest bundle that reaches it:
>$$\min_{x_1, x_2}\ p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad u(x_1, x_2) \ge \bar u .$$
>If $u$ is continuous and strictly increasing in each good, the constraint binds. A bundle with utility above $\bar u$ can be cut back slightly, still reach $\bar u$, and cost less. The solution is the **Hicksian demand**, also called **compensated demand**, $h_i(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$. The minimal cost is the **expenditure function**
>$$e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = p_1 h_1(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) + p_2 h_2(\mathbf{p}, \bar u).$$
>With the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 - \lambda\,(u(x_1, x_2) - \bar u)$ the first-order conditions are
>$$p_i = \lambda\, u'_{x_i} \quad (i = 1, 2), \qquad u(x_1, x_2) = \bar u .$$
>Dividing gives $u'_{x_1}/u'_{x_2} = p_1/p_2$, the tangency condition of [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]]. The multiplier is different. Here $\lambda = p_i/u'_{x_i} = \partial e/\partial \bar u$ is the cost of one more unit of utility, in dollars per unit. When the two problems describe the same bundle, with $\bar u = v(\mathbf{p}, M)$ and $M = e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$, the EMIN multiplier is the reciprocal of the UMP multiplier.
>
>The two problems are **dual**, meaning each one inverts the other. For continuous, strictly increasing $u$ with a unique optimum,
>$$h_i(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = x_i^*\big(\mathbf{p}, e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)\big), \qquad x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M) = h_i\big(\mathbf{p}, v(\mathbf{p}, M)\big),$$
>and $e(\mathbf{p}, v(\mathbf{p}, M)) = M$, $v(\mathbf{p}, e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)) = \bar u$, where $v$ is the [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function|indirect utility]]. Two results hold for every utility function. Hicksian demand never rises with its own price, $\partial h_i/\partial p_i \le 0$. By the [[7.3 Envelope Theorem|envelope theorem]], $\partial e/\partial p_i = h_i(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$, a result also known as Shephard's lemma. For Cobb-Douglas $u = x_1^{\alpha}x_2^{1-\alpha}$,
>$$h_1 = \bar u\left(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{1-\alpha}, \qquad h_2 = \bar u\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right)^{\alpha}, \qquad e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = \bar u\left(\frac{p_1}{\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}\left(\frac{p_2}{1-\alpha}\right)^{1-\alpha}.$$

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture noted only that the EMIN multiplier differs from the UMP multiplier. The results $\lambda = \partial e/\partial \bar u$ and $\lambda_{\text{EMIN}} = 1/\lambda_{\text{UMP}}$ are extensions, and the name Shephard's lemma was not used.

# Intuition

A company wants to reward a team after a long project. It can pay for team dinners and for concert tickets. Management sets a target utility level $\bar u$ that the team must reach. The company wants to reach that target at the lowest cost. Utility maximization fixes the money and asks for the most happiness. Expenditure minimization fixes the happiness and asks for the least money. Both problems end at a bundle where an indifference curve is tangent to a budget line.

In the diagram the indifference curve $\bar u$ stays fixed and the budget lines move. All lines have slope $-p_1/p_2$, and each one belongs to a different amount of money. The two upper lines cross $\bar u$, so they leave money to spare. As the line slides inward, the last line that still touches $\bar u$ is the tangent one. Its income is $e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$ and the point of contact is $(h_1, h_2)$. Any line further in misses $\bar u$ entirely.

# Properties

#### *(Why the Multipliers Invert)* The EMIN multiplier is dollars per unit of utility, and the UMP multiplier is utility per dollar.

The envelope theorem gives $\partial e/\partial \bar u = \lambda_{\text{EMIN}}$ and $\partial v/\partial M = \lambda_{\text{UMP}}$. At fixed prices, $e(\mathbf{p}, \cdot)$ and $v(\mathbf{p}, \cdot)$ are inverse functions, since $v(\mathbf{p}, e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)) = \bar u$. The derivative of an inverse function is the reciprocal of the original derivative, so $\lambda_{\text{EMIN}} = 1/\lambda_{\text{UMP}}$ at matching points. The FOCs show the same thing directly. At one bundle, EMIN gives $\lambda = p_i/u'_{x_i}$ and UMP gives $\lambda = u'_{x_i}/p_i$. The identity fails if the two problems are evaluated at unrelated values of $\bar u$ and $M$.

#### *(Hicksian Demand Slopes Down)* A higher own price moves the cheapest point along the indifference curve toward less of that good.

A rise from $p_1$ to $p_1'$ makes every budget line steeper. The cheapest line that touches $\bar u$ must also be steeper, so it touches a convex indifference curve further up and to the left. In the diagram the contact point moves from $B$ to $B'$ and Hicksian demand falls from $h_1$ to $h_1'$. Utility is the same at both points, so this move is a pure substitution away from the good that became expensive.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The argument below proves the result without convexity. The lecture stated that the result holds for non-convex indifference curves but did not prove it.

Let $\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$ and $\mathbf{h}' = \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{p}', \bar u)$. Both bundles reach $\bar u$. The bundle $\mathbf{h}$ is cheapest at $\mathbf{p}$, so $\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{h} \le \mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{h}'$. The bundle $\mathbf{h}'$ is cheapest at $\mathbf{p}'$, so $\mathbf{p}'\cdot\mathbf{h}' \le \mathbf{p}'\cdot\mathbf{h}$. Adding the two gives $(\mathbf{p}' - \mathbf{p})\cdot(\mathbf{h}' - \mathbf{h}) \le 0$. When only $p_1$ changes, this reads $(p_1' - p_1)(h_1' - h_1) \le 0$, so $h_1$ cannot rise when $p_1$ rises.

>[!warning] Lecture Wording: Always Decreasing
>The lecture answers "Is $h_i$ always decreasing in $p_i$?" with "Yes!". The precise statement is that $h_i$ never increases with $p_i$. For [[6.4c Perfect Complements|perfect complements]] the cheapest bundle is the kink of the indifference curve at every price, so $\partial h_i/\partial p_i = 0$.

#### *(Reading Shephard's Lemma)* A small price rise $dp_i$ raises the cost of staying at $\bar u$ by $h_i\,dp_i$.

The consumer does switch toward the other good. At the EMIN optimum both goods cost the same per unit of utility, so the switch saves nothing to first order. For a large price rise the exact extra cost is smaller than $h_i\,\Delta p_i$, because substitution saves money on the way. The Medium example measures this gap. The derivation of the [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]] relies on $\partial e/\partial p_i = h_i$ to split a price effect into substitution and income parts.

#### *(Three Routes to Hicksian Demand)* Hicksian demand can come from solving EMIN, from inverting $v$, or from differentiating $e$.

In the direct route we solve the EMIN FOCs, as in the derivation below. In the dual route we start from Marshallian demand. Solve $v(\mathbf{p}, M) = \bar u$ for $M$ to get $e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$, then substitute: $h_i = x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u))$. For Cobb-Douglas, $v$ is linear in $M$, so the inversion takes one line, and $h_1 = \alpha\, e/p_1$. In the third route we differentiate a known $e$ with respect to $p_i$. This is Shephard's lemma read from right to left, and it is the EMIN counterpart of Roy's identity in [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function]], which recovers Marshallian demand from $v$. Roy's identity divides by $\partial v/\partial M$ to convert utility into dollars. Shephard's lemma needs no division, because $e$ is already measured in dollars. On an exam the dual route is often fastest when the Marshallian demand is already known.

#### *(No Giffen Hicksian Demand)* Marshallian demand can rise with its own price, and Hicksian demand cannot.

Hicksian demand holds utility fixed, so it contains no income effect. The sign of $\partial h_i/\partial p_i$ comes from the shape of one indifference curve and is never positive. Marshallian demand also reacts to the loss of purchasing power, which can push demand for an inferior good up. A [[6.3.4 Price Changes|Giffen good]] is therefore possible for $x_i^*$ and impossible for $h_i$. Hicksian demand does not depend on $M$ at all, because income is not a parameter of EMIN.

# Derivation

We want $h_1$, $h_2$, and $e$ for $u = x_1^{\alpha}x_2^{1-\alpha}$ with $0 < \alpha < 1$. The utility is strictly increasing, so the constraint binds. The Lagrangian is
$$\mathcal{L} = p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 - \lambda\left(x_1^{\alpha}x_2^{1-\alpha} - \bar u\right).$$
The FOCs are
$$p_1 = \lambda\,\alpha\, x_1^{\alpha-1}x_2^{1-\alpha}, \qquad p_2 = \lambda\,(1-\alpha)\, x_1^{\alpha}x_2^{-\alpha}, \qquad x_1^{\alpha}x_2^{1-\alpha} = \bar u .$$
Dividing the first by the second removes $\lambda$:
$$\frac{p_1}{p_2} = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\frac{x_2}{x_1}, \qquad \text{so} \qquad x_2 = \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,\frac{p_1}{p_2}\, x_1 .$$
Substitute into the constraint. The powers of $x_1$ add to one:
$$x_1\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right)^{1-\alpha} = \bar u, \qquad \text{so} \qquad h_1 = \bar u\left(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{1-\alpha}.$$
Multiplying $h_1$ by $\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{p_1}{p_2}$ lowers the exponent from $1-\alpha$ to $\alpha$ and gives $h_2 = \bar u\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right)^{\alpha}$.

For the expenditure function, both $p_1h_1$ and $p_2h_2$ contain the factor $\bar u\,p_1^{\alpha}p_2^{1-\alpha}$:
$$e = \bar u\, p_1^{\alpha}p_2^{1-\alpha}\left[\alpha^{1-\alpha}(1-\alpha)^{\alpha-1} + (1-\alpha)^{\alpha}\alpha^{-\alpha}\right] = \bar u\, p_1^{\alpha}p_2^{1-\alpha}\,\alpha^{-\alpha}(1-\alpha)^{\alpha-1}\left[\alpha + (1-\alpha)\right].$$
The last bracket equals one, which gives $e$ in the callout. The same algebra shows $p_1h_1 = \alpha e$, so the spending shares match those of utility maximization.

The multiplier follows from the first FOC. At the optimum $u'_{x_1} = \alpha\bar u/h_1$, so $\lambda = p_1h_1/(\alpha\bar u) = e/\bar u = (p_1/\alpha)^{\alpha}(p_2/(1-\alpha))^{1-\alpha}$. This is the reciprocal of the UMP multiplier $\alpha^{\alpha}(1-\alpha)^{1-\alpha}/(p_1^{\alpha}p_2^{1-\alpha})$ from [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function]].

Finally, we compute the price effects. Hicksian demand $h_1$ is proportional to $p_1^{-(1-\alpha)}p_2^{1-\alpha}$, and $h_2$ to $p_1^{\alpha}p_2^{-\alpha}$. So
$$\frac{\partial h_1}{\partial p_1} = -(1-\alpha)\frac{h_1}{p_1} < 0, \quad \frac{\partial h_1}{\partial p_2} = (1-\alpha)\frac{h_1}{p_2} > 0, \quad \frac{\partial h_2}{\partial p_2} = -\alpha\frac{h_2}{p_2} < 0, \quad \frac{\partial h_2}{\partial p_1} = \alpha\frac{h_2}{p_1} > 0 .$$
Each good's compensated demand falls with its own price and rises with the other price. The two cross effects are equal, a symmetry that [[6.3.6 Complements and Substitutes]] uses. The derivative $\partial e/\partial p_1 = \alpha e/p_1$ equals $h_1$, as Shephard's lemma requires.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) The Cheapest Way to Make a Teenager Happy
>A parent wants a teenager to reach utility $\bar u = 6$ from movie tickets ($x_1$, $p_1 = \$8$) and pizza slices ($x_2$, $p_2 = \$2$). The teenager's utility is $u = x_1^{1/2}x_2^{1/2}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the cheapest bundle and its cost. Check it against utility maximization.
>>With $\alpha = 1/2$ the Hicksian demands are
>>$$h_1 = 6\left(\frac{2}{8}\right)^{1/2} = 3, \qquad h_2 = 6\left(\frac{8}{2}\right)^{1/2} = 12 .$$
>>The cost is $e = 8\cdot 3 + 2\cdot 12 = \$48$, and $u(3, 12) = \sqrt{36} = 6$ as required. The MRS is $-x_2/x_1 = -4 = -p_1/p_2$, so the budget line is tangent. A teenager who is given $M = \$48$ and chooses freely buys $x_1^* = 24/8 = 3$ and $x_2^* = 24/2 = 12$, the same bundle. The multiplier is $e/\bar u = 8$, so one more unit of utility costs $\$8$.

>[!example] (Medium) Compensating for a Price Rise
>The pizza price in the Easy example rises from $\$2$ to $\$2.50$. The parent still wants $\bar u = 6$.
>
>>[!example]- Estimate the extra cost with Shephard's lemma, compute the exact extra cost, and find the new bundle.
>>Shephard's lemma gives $\partial e/\partial p_2 = h_2 = 12$, so the estimate is $12 \cdot 0.50 = \$6$. For this utility $e = 2\bar u\sqrt{p_1p_2}$, so the exact new cost is $12\sqrt{8 \cdot 2.5} = 12\sqrt{20} \approx \$53.67$, an increase of $\$5.67$.
>>
>>The new bundle is $h_1 = 6\sqrt{2.5/8} \approx 3.35$ tickets and $h_2 = 6\sqrt{8/2.5} \approx 10.73$ slices. Pizza falls and movies rise, as $\partial h_2/\partial p_2 < 0$ and $\partial h_1/\partial p_2 > 0$ predict. The parent switches part of the reward from pizza to movies, which is why the exact extra cost is $\$0.33$ below the estimate.

>[!example] (Hard) Comparing the Two Multipliers
>A consumer has $u = x_1^{1/3}x_2^{2/3}$, prices $p_1 = \$3$ and $p_2 = \$5$, and income $M = \$45$. Utility maximization gives $x^* = (5, 6)$, $v = 5^{1/3}6^{2/3} \approx 5.646$, and $\lambda_{\text{UMP}} \approx 0.1255$. A classmate argues that EMIN at $\bar u = 5.646$ must have the same multiplier, since both problems impose the same tangency condition.
>
>>[!example]- Solve EMIN at $\bar u = v$, check the duality identities, and test the classmate's claim.
>>With $\alpha = 1/3$,
>>$$h_1 = 5.646\left(\frac{1/3}{2/3}\cdot\frac{5}{3}\right)^{2/3} = 5.646\,(0.8855) \approx 5.00, \qquad h_2 = 5.646\left(2\cdot\frac{3}{5}\right)^{1/3} = 5.646\,(1.0627) \approx 6.00 .$$
>>So $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{p}, v(\mathbf{p}, M)) = \mathbf{x}^*(\mathbf{p}, M)$, and $e = 3\cdot 5 + 5\cdot 6 = 45 = M$.
>>
>>The EMIN multiplier is $\lambda_{\text{EMIN}} = e/\bar u = 45/5.646 \approx 7.97$. The classmate is wrong. The tangency condition fixes only the ratio of the two FOCs. The level of $\lambda$ depends on which expression is the objective. Here $1/\lambda_{\text{UMP}} = 1/0.1255 \approx 7.97$, so the multipliers are reciprocals. One more unit of utility costs about $\$7.97$, and one more dollar buys about $0.1255$ units of utility.
