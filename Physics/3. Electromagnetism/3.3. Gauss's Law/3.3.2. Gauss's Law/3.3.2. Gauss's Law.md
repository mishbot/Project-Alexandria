>[!note] Gauss's Law
>**Gauss's law** relates the electric flux through any closed surface to the charge inside it:
>$$\Phi_{E} = \oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}.$$
>The closed surface is called a **Gaussian surface**. It is an imaginary surface, chosen freely, and $d\vec A$ points outward on it. The left side is the [[3.3.1. Electric Flux|electric flux]] out through the surface. The field $\vec E$ in it is the total field, made by every charge inside and outside. The **enclosed charge** $Q_{\text{enc}}$ is the net charge inside the surface, counted with signs. Charge outside the surface adds zero net flux. The constant $\varepsilon_0$ is the **permittivity of free space**, and it replaces the Coulomb constant $k$:
>$$\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{C}^2/(\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2), \qquad k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}.$$
>From here on the notes write the point-charge field as $E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|Q|}{r^2}$. Gauss's law is the first of **Maxwell's equations**, the four laws behind all of electromagnetism. Coulomb's law comes from experiment, so Gauss's law does too.
>
>A zero flux does not mean a zero field. The product $\vec E\cdot d\vec A$ can be positive on one part of a surface and negative on another.
>
>The law holds for every closed surface and every set of charges. It is used for two kinds of problems:
>- To find the field from known charges. This works only with enough symmetry.
>- To find the charge from a known field. In these "where is the charge" problems the field is known in advance, as inside a [[3.3.3. Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium|conductor]].
>
>To find $E$, the flux must reduce to $E$ times an area. Two conditions make this possible:
>- Symmetry fixes the field's direction exactly at every point of the Gaussian surface.
>- The field's size is constant on every part of the surface where $\vec E$ is not perpendicular to $d\vec A$.
>
>The procedure runs as follows:
>- Decide whether Gauss's law can find $E$. Sketch the field lines from the charges. Gauss's law can find $E$ when the sketch fixes the direction of every line exactly. The shape and the charge must then share the same [[3.2.3. Symmetry Arguments|symmetry]].
>- Draw a large picture, and find the one variable that the field's size depends on.
>- Choose a closed surface with the same symmetry, passing through the point where $E$ is wanted.
>- Draw $d\vec A$ on each part of the surface, pointing outward.
>- Split the flux into parts. On each part, $\vec E\cdot d\vec A$ is either zero or $\pm E\,dA$ with $E$ constant. Each part then gives $0$ or $\pm E$ times its area.
>- Find $Q_{\text{enc}}$, counting only the charge inside the surface.
>- Set the flux equal to $Q_{\text{enc}}/\varepsilon_0$ and solve for $E$. Check the units and a limiting case.

# Intuition

Gauss's law is a statement about field lines. Lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges. The flux through a closed surface is proportional to the lines leaving it minus the lines entering it. Charges outside the surface add nothing to this net count, as the property *Total Field, Enclosed Charge* explains. If each charge sends out lines in proportion to its size, the net count is proportional to $Q_{\text{enc}}$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not state that line counts are proportional to charge, as [[3.2.2. Electric Field Lines]] notes. The line-count picture follows Giancoli chapter 22.

The charges set the field. The person solving the problem sets the surface, and with it every $d\vec A$. The flux combines the two. A surface that matches the symmetry of the charges makes the flux easy to compute.

Gauss's law is an integral equation for $\vec E$. In general the integral cannot be done without knowing $\vec E$ first. For a symmetric charge arrangement, the direction of $\vec E$ is known in advance. Only its size is unknown. On a well-chosen surface that size is constant and comes out of the integral.

The animation first grows a Gaussian sphere around a charge. The field falls as $1/r^2$ and the area grows as $r^2$, so the flux stays the same. Then it counts field lines as the charge moves inside the sphere and then outside it.

# Derivation

Take a point charge $q > 0$ and a sphere of radius $r$ centered on it. By symmetry the field points radially outward and has the same size $E(r)$ everywhere on the sphere. The outward $d\vec A$ also points radially, so $\vec E\cdot d\vec A = E\,dA$ at every point. The size $E(r)$ is constant on the sphere and comes out of the integral:
$$\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A = E(r)\oint dA = E(r)\,4\pi r^2.$$
No formula for $E$ has been used yet. Inserting the point-charge field $E = kq/r^2$ gives
$$\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A = \frac{kq}{r^2}\,4\pi r^2 = 4\pi k q.$$
The radius cancels. A sphere a millimeter across and a sphere hundreds of light-years across give the same flux. Writing $k = 1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$ turns $4\pi kq$ into $q/\varepsilon_0$. That is the reason for writing the constant this way.

Gauss's law states that the same result holds for any closed surface around any charges. A region with a field but no charge shows what this means. Every closed surface in that region has zero flux. Split such a surface into the part where the field enters and the part where it leaves. Where it enters, the angle between $\vec E$ and $d\vec A$ exceeds $90^\circ$, so $\vec E\cdot d\vec A < 0$. Where it leaves, the angle is less than $90^\circ$, and $\vec E\cdot d\vec A > 0$. The two parts cancel exactly, for every surface in the region.

# Properties

#### *(Tied to the Inverse Square)* Gauss's law holds because the point-charge field falls off exactly as $1/r^2$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture showed only that the point-charge flux does not depend on the radius. The comparison with other force laws, and the link to Coulomb's law, follow Giancoli chapter 22.

A sphere's area grows as $r^2$, and the field on it falls as $1/r^2$. The two factors cancel in the flux. Suppose the field fell as $1/r^{2.1}$ instead. The flux through a sphere would then fall as $r^{-0.1}$. Between radii of $0.1\ \text{m}$ and $10\ \text{m}$ it would drop by $37\%$. No single constant could then stand on the right side of the law. For charges at rest, Gauss's law follows from Coulomb's law and superposition.

#### *(Total Field, Enclosed Charge)* The field in Gauss's law comes from all charges, while the right side counts only the charges inside.

A charge outside the surface changes $\vec E$ at every point of the surface. Each of its field lines that meets the surface crosses it twice, once inward and once outward. The inward crossing adds negative flux, and the outward crossing adds an equal positive flux. A line from a charge inside crosses outward one more time than inward. Only the enclosed charges therefore change the net flux. The outside charges still change the field itself. Gauss's law always gives the correct total flux. It gives the field only when symmetry keeps $E$ constant on the surface, with every charge included. Part (e) of the Hard example below shows a case where it does not.

#### *(What Zero Flux Allows)* A zero flux gives a zero field only with extra information, such as symmetry or a conductor.

Zero flux means that the outward and inward parts of $\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A$ balance. A uniform field through a closed box gives zero flux, while the field has the same nonzero size everywhere. Concluding $E = 0$ needs a second fact about the field. Suppose symmetry makes $\vec E$ radial and equal in size over a sphere. Then the flux is $4\pi r^2 E$, and zero flux forces $E = 0$. Inside a conductor, the field is known to be zero for a different reason. [[3.3.3. Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium]] uses that fact to locate charge.

#### *(One Surface per Region)* The Gaussian surface must pass through the point where $E$ is wanted, and each region needs its own surface.

The law gives information about $E$ only on the surface itself. A point inside a charged ball and a point outside it need two different spheres. The flux has the same form for both, $4\pi r^2 E(r)$, because the symmetry is the same. The enclosed charge differs, because the two spheres hold different amounts of charge. [[3.3.2a Spherical Symmetry]] works out both cases.

#### *(A Real Integral Means the Wrong Method)* If the flux cannot be found without knowing $E$, Gauss's law cannot find $E$.

With the right surface, every part of the flux reduces to $0$ or $\pm E$ times an area. No real integration is left. A surface integral computed point by point means the symmetry is missing or the surface is poorly chosen. Gauss's law is still true in that case. It no longer gives $E$. The [[3.2.4. Electric Field of a Continuous Charge Distribution|slice-and-dice method]] works for any distribution, and it is the method to use then. When the symmetry is complete, Gauss's law gives the same answer in a few lines.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Three Surfaces
>Charges $q_1 = +4.0\ \text{nC}$, $q_2 = -1.5\ \text{nC}$, and $q_3 = +6.0\ \text{nC}$ sit in empty space. Closed surface $S_1$ encloses $q_1$ and $q_2$. Surface $S_2$ encloses only $q_1$. Surface $S_3$ encloses $q_2$ and $q_3$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the flux through each surface.
>>Only the enclosed charge counts. For $S_1$ the flux is
>>$$\Phi_1 = \frac{q_1 + q_2}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{2.5 \times 10^{-9}}{8.85 \times 10^{-12}} = 282\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}.$$
>>For $S_2$, $\Phi_2 = q_1/\varepsilon_0 = 452\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}$. For $S_3$, $\Phi_3 = (q_2 + q_3)/\varepsilon_0 = 508\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}$. The shapes and sizes of the surfaces do not matter. The field on each surface depends on all three charges.

>[!example] (Medium) Half a Sphere
>A charge $q = +2.0\ \text{nC}$ sits at the center of an imaginary sphere. A plane through the charge cuts the sphere into two hemispheres.
>
>>[!example]- Find the flux through one curved hemisphere, with $d\vec A$ pointing away from the charge.
>>The whole sphere has the flux
>>$$\Phi = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-9}}{8.85 \times 10^{-12}} = 226\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}.$$
>>The field is radial and has the same size everywhere on the sphere. A half turn about a line through the charge, lying in the cutting plane, carries one hemisphere onto the other. The two hemispheres therefore have equal flux, $113\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}$ each. The flat disk through the charge has zero flux, since the field lies in the plane of the disk. The answer does not depend on the sphere's radius.

>[!example] (Hard) Can Gauss's Law Find the Field?
>Six charge distributions:
>- (a) a uniformly charged solid cube;
>- (b) two concentric thin spherical shells, each with its own uniform charge;
>- (c) an infinite uniform line of charge on the axis of an infinite cylindrical shell with uniform charge;
>- (d) a uniformly charged thin spherical shell with a small hole cut in it;
>- (e) a uniformly charged ball with a point charge outside it;
>- (f) an infinite thin cylindrical shell whose surface charge density changes with the angle around its axis.
>
>>[!example]- For which ones can Gauss's law alone find the field?
>>Sketch the field lines in each case.
>>- (a) No. Lines leave the faces and curve near the edges and corners. Their exact directions are unknown.
>>- (b) Yes. Both shells look the same after any rotation about the center. The lines point radially, and the field's size depends only on $r$.
>>- (c) Yes. The line and the shell share one axis. Both look the same after rotations about it and shifts along it. The lines point straight away from the axis.
>>- (d) No. The hole breaks the rotation symmetry. Near the hole the lines bend, and their directions are unknown.
>>- (e) No. The combination looks the same only after rotations about the line through both centers. That symmetry fixes the lines only on that line. Gauss's law can still find each field separately, and [[3.1.5. Superposition Principle|superposition]] adds them.
>>- (f) No. The shape looks the same after any rotation about the axis. The charge changes with the angle, so it does not. The lines are no longer radial, and their directions are unknown.
