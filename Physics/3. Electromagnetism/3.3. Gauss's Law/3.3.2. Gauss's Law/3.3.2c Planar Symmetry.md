>[!note] Planar Symmetry
>A charge distribution has **planar symmetry** when it looks the same after any shift along a plane. The standard case is an **infinite sheet of charge**, a flat sheet with constant surface charge density $\sigma$. Shifts along the sheet and rotations about its perpendicular leave it unchanged. The field is therefore perpendicular to the sheet. The sheet is also its own mirror image, so the field has equal sizes at equal distances on both sides. It points away from the sheet when $\sigma > 0$ and toward it when $\sigma < 0$.
>
>The Gaussian surface is a **pillbox**, a short closed cylinder that crosses the sheet. Its two flat faces, each of area $A$, lie parallel to the sheet on opposite sides. Its side wall is perpendicular to the sheet. The flux has two parts:
>- On each face, $\vec E$ is parallel to $d\vec A$, and the flux is $EA$.
>- On the side wall, $\vec E$ is perpendicular to $d\vec A$, and the flux is zero.
>
>The pillbox encloses the charge $\sigma A$, so [[3.3.2. Gauss's Law|Gauss's law]] reads $2EA = \sigma A/\varepsilon_0$. The field of the sheet is
>$$E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}.$$
>It does not depend on the distance from the sheet. Two parallel sheets with $+\sigma$ and $-\sigma$ combine by [[3.1.5. Superposition Principle|superposition]]:
>- Between the sheets the two fields point the same way, from $+\sigma$ to $-\sigma$, and $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$.
>- Outside the sheets the two fields point in opposite directions and cancel, so $E = 0$.
>
>A real sheet behaves as infinite at distances much smaller than its size, away from its edges.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not treat a charged sheet, alone or in pairs. It used a pillbox only at the surface of a conductor. This note follows Giancoli chapters 21 and 22.

# Intuition

The field lines leave an infinite sheet as parallel straight lines. Parallel lines never spread apart, so their density stays the same at every distance. The field does too. Around a line of charge the field lines spread over cylinders, and around a point charge they spread over spheres.

A pillbox crosses a charged sheet. The flux through its two caps gives $E = \sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)$. When the pillbox stretches, the flux and the enclosed charge stay the same. So the field is the same at every distance.

# Properties

#### *(How Close Is Close)* On the axis of a charged disk of radius $R$, the field reaches $90\%$ of $\sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)$ at a distance $R/10$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The disk formula comes from slicing the disk into rings, as in Giancoli chapter 21.

A uniformly charged disk is the simplest finite sheet. On its axis, at a distance $z$ from its center, its field is
$$E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(1 - \frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2 + R^2}}\right).$$
The bracket is the fraction of the infinite-sheet value. At $z = R/10$ it equals $0.90$. At $z = R$ it has fallen to $0.29$. At $z = 10R$ it is $0.005$. There the disk's field is close to that of a point charge with the same total charge. The infinite-sheet result is within $10\%$ at distances below about a tenth of the disk's radius.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Charged Sheet
>A large flat plastic sheet carries $\sigma = +3.5\ \mu\text{C/m}^2$ spread evenly.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field $1.0\ \text{cm}$ and $5.0\ \text{cm}$ from the sheet, near its middle.
>>Both points are close to the sheet compared with its size, so the field is the same at both:
>>$$E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} = \frac{3.5 \times 10^{-6}}{2(8.85 \times 10^{-12})} = 1.98 \times 10^{5}\ \text{N/C}.$$
>>It points away from the sheet on each side.

>[!example] (Medium) Two Unequal Sheets
>Two large parallel sheets carry $\sigma_1 = +6.0\ \mu\text{C/m}^2$ on the left and $\sigma_2 = -2.0\ \mu\text{C/m}^2$ on the right.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field to the left of both sheets, between them, and to the right of both.
>>Each sheet alone makes a field of constant size:
>>$$E_1 = \frac{\sigma_1}{2\varepsilon_0} = 3.39 \times 10^{5}\ \text{N/C}, \qquad E_2 = \frac{|\sigma_2|}{2\varepsilon_0} = 1.13 \times 10^{5}\ \text{N/C}.$$
>>The field of sheet 1 points away from it, and the field of sheet 2 points toward it. With right taken as positive, the sums are:
>>- Left of both: $E = -3.39 \times 10^{5} + 1.13 \times 10^{5} = -2.26 \times 10^{5}\ \text{N/C}$, pointing left.
>>- Between: $E = +3.39 \times 10^{5} + 1.13 \times 10^{5} = +4.52 \times 10^{5}\ \text{N/C}$, pointing right.
>>- Right of both: $E = +3.39 \times 10^{5} - 1.13 \times 10^{5} = +2.26 \times 10^{5}\ \text{N/C}$, pointing right.
>>
>>Outside, the field equals that of one sheet with the net density $+4.0\ \mu\text{C/m}^2$.
