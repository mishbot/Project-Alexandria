>[!note] Mean Squared Error
>The **mean squared error** (MSE) averages the squared residuals of $n$ predictions:
>$$\mathrm{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat y_i)^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i^2 .$$
>Here $y_i$ is the true value, $\hat y_i$ is the prediction and $e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$ is the residual. The pointwise loss is the **squared error** $\ell(y, \hat y) = (y - \hat y)^2$, also called the L2 loss, and MSE is its empirical risk. Least squares minimizes the sum $\mathrm{SSE} = n \cdot \mathrm{MSE}$, which has the same minimizer. The **root mean squared error** returns to the units of $y$:
>$$\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}}.$$
>MSE has five headline facts:
>
>- **Minimizer**: the best constant prediction is the sample mean $\bar y$. Over all functions of $\mathbf{x}$, the population risk $\mathbb{E}[(y - f(\mathbf{x}))^2]$ is minimized by the conditional mean $f^*(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}]$.
>- **Gaussian likelihood**: if $y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(f(\mathbf{x}_i), \sigma^2)$ independently, the negative log-likelihood is $\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) + \mathrm{SSE}/(2\sigma^2)$. Minimizing MSE is then maximum likelihood, and the MLE of $\sigma^2$ is the training MSE.
>- **Gradient**: $\partial \ell/\partial \hat y = -2(y - \hat y)$, so each point pulls on the fit in proportion to its residual.
>- **Outliers**: a residual $10$ times larger contributes $100$ times more, so a few large errors can dominate MSE.
>- **Bias and variance**: the expected test MSE of a learned model splits into noise, squared bias and variance ([[2.3.1. Bias-Variance Decomposition]]).
>
>MSE is the default loss and metric for regression. [[2.1.2. Mean Absolute Error|MAE]] replaces it when outliers dominate, and [[2.1.3. Huber Loss|Huber loss]] combines the two.

# Intuition

The name lists the steps. Take each error, square it, and average the squares. The square of any error is nonnegative, so errors in opposite directions cannot cancel. The square also weighs each error by its own size. One error of $10$ contributes $100$, while ten errors of $1$ contribute $10$ in total. A model trained on MSE therefore works hardest on its worst predictions. MSE is in squared units of $y$, such as squared dollars, which are hard to read. RMSE takes the square root and returns to dollars.

Squared error has two further justifications. The best constant guess under squared error is the mean, and the best guess given $\mathbf{x}$ is the conditional mean. A model trained on MSE therefore estimates the average outcome for each input. MSE also follows from a noise model. If the noise is Gaussian with constant variance, the most likely parameters are the least squares parameters. Least squares therefore works well when the noise is close to Gaussian and poorly when the noise has heavy tails.

# Properties

#### *(Offset Plus Spread)* MSE equals the squared mean residual plus the variance of the residuals.

Write $\bar e$ for the mean residual. Then
$$\mathrm{MSE} = \bar e^{\,2} + \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (e_i - \bar e)^2 .$$
The first term measures a systematic offset, such as a price model that underpredicts every house by $4$ thousand dollars. The second term measures the scatter around that offset. Subtracting $\bar e$ from every prediction removes the first term and leaves the second. OLS with an intercept sets $\bar e = 0$ on its training data, so its training MSE is pure spread. On new data the offset can return, for example after prices rise. For a parameter estimate $\hat\theta$, the same split reads $\mathbb{E}[(\hat\theta - \theta)^2] = \mathrm{bias}^2 + \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)$ ([[1.2.4 Minimum Mean Squared Error Estimation]]).

#### *(Reading RMSE Against MAE)* RMSE is never smaller than MAE, and the gap measures how uneven the errors are.

For any residuals, $\mathrm{MAE} \le \mathrm{RMSE} \le \sqrt{n}\,\mathrm{MAE}$. The lower bound is reached when all $|e_i|$ are equal. The upper bound is reached when one residual carries all the error. For Gaussian residuals with mean zero and standard deviation $\sigma$, $\mathrm{RMSE} \approx \sigma$ and $\mathrm{MAE} \approx \sigma\sqrt{2/\pi} \approx 0.80\,\sigma$. A ratio RMSE/MAE near $1.25$ is therefore typical of Gaussian noise. A ratio well above $1.25$ suggests a few large errors that deserve inspection.

#### *(Large Residuals, Large Steps)* Gradient descent on MSE takes steps that scale with the residuals.

For a linear model $\hat y_i = \mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol\beta$, the gradient of SSE is $-2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{e}$, where $\mathbf{e}$ is the vector of residuals. At the same input, a point with residual $30$ pushes $15$ times harder than a point with residual $2$. Near the optimum the residuals and the gradient shrink together, so [[6.4. Gradient Descent|gradient descent]] with a fixed learning rate settles smoothly. Far from the optimum, one huge residual can produce one huge step. Deep regression models with unbounded targets can then suffer exploding gradients. Huber loss caps the pull of each point to prevent this.

#### *(Sensitivity to Outliers)* One corrupted point can move the MSE-optimal prediction arbitrarily far.

The **breakdown point** of an estimator is the smallest fraction of corrupted points that can move it arbitrarily far. For the mean it is $1/n$, since one corrupted value suffices, and it tends to $0$ as $n$ grows. The median has breakdown point $1/2$. Least squares regression inherits the weakness of the mean, and one corrupted response can tilt the whole line. Squared error suits data where large errors are real and costly, such as peak loads on a power grid. It is a poor choice when large errors come from data-entry mistakes or sensor glitches.

#### *(Averaging Over Modes)* When the outcome has several likely values, the MSE-optimal prediction can fall between them.

The conditional mean is the best guess only in the squared-error sense. Suppose a route takes $20$ minutes without traffic and $40$ with traffic. The mean may then be a time that never occurs. Image models show the same effect. A colorization network trained with squared error averages the plausible colors of an object and outputs dull, grayish images ([[8.3. Image Colorization]]). One fix is to predict a full distribution. Others predict the median or another quantile, or add a loss that rewards realistic outputs.

# Derivation

We show that the conditional mean minimizes the expected squared error. Fix an input $\mathbf{x}$ and write $\mu = \mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}]$. For any constant prediction $c$, add and subtract $\mu$ inside the square:
$$\mathbb{E}[(y - c)^2 \mid \mathbf{x}] = \mathbb{E}[(y - \mu)^2 \mid \mathbf{x}] + 2(\mu - c)\,\mathbb{E}[y - \mu \mid \mathbf{x}] + (\mu - c)^2 = \mathrm{Var}(y \mid \mathbf{x}) + (\mu - c)^2 .$$
The middle term vanishes because $\mathbb{E}[y - \mu \mid \mathbf{x}] = 0$. The first term does not depend on $c$, and the second is zero only at $c = \mu$. The minimum expected loss is $\mathrm{Var}(y \mid \mathbf{x})$, the noise that no model can remove. The same steps with a sample average in place of the expectation give $\bar y$ as the best constant.

For the Gaussian link, suppose $y_i = f(\mathbf{x}_i) + \varepsilon_i$ with independent $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$. The log-likelihood of the data is
$$\log L = \sum_{i=1}^n \log\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left(-\frac{e_i^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\right) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 .$$
For fixed $\sigma^2$, only the last sum depends on $f$, so maximizing $\log L$ minimizes SSE. Setting $\partial \log L/\partial \sigma^2 = 0$ gives $\hat\sigma^2 = \mathrm{SSE}/n$, the training MSE. [[3.1. Linear Regression]] carries out the same steps for the line $\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i$. That note also gives the unbiased estimate, which divides SSE by the residual degrees of freedom.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Scoring a House-Price Model
>A model predicts the sale prices of five houses in thousands of dollars. The true prices are $y = [420, 515, 380, 610, 465]$, and the predictions are $\hat y = [400, 530, 390, 580, 470]$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute MSE and RMSE, and split MSE into offset and spread.
>>The residuals are $e = [20, -15, -10, 30, -5]$, and their squares are $[400, 225, 100, 900, 25]$. The sum is $1650$, so $\mathrm{MSE} = 330$ in squared thousands of dollars. The RMSE is $\sqrt{330} = 18.2$, so a typical prediction misses by about $18$ thousand dollars. The mean residual is $\bar e = 4$, so the offset term is $16$ and the spread term is $330 - 16 = 314$. Raising every prediction by $4$ thousand dollars would lower MSE to $314$. The largest residual alone contributes $900/1650 = 55\%$ of the total.

>[!example] (Medium) A Sensor Glitch
>A thermometer in a stable room records $[20.1, 19.8, 20.4, 20.0, 19.7]$ degrees Celsius. A sixth reading of $35.0$ comes from a loose wire.
>
>>[!example]- Find the MSE-optimal constant and its MSE before and after the glitch.
>>The best constant under squared error is the mean. Before the glitch the mean is $20.0$, and the MSE is $(0.01 + 0.04 + 0.16 + 0 + 0.09)/5 = 0.06$. After the glitch the mean is $135/6 = 22.5$, which lies above every correct reading. The MSE at $22.5$ is $31.3$. The glitch alone contributes $(35 - 22.5)^2 = 156.25$ of the total $187.8$, or $83\%$. The median moves only from $20.0$ to $20.05$. One faulty value out of six moved the MSE-optimal estimate by $2.5$ degrees. It raised the MSE by a factor of about $500$.

>[!example] (Hard) Two Traffic Regimes
>A delivery route takes $20$ minutes with probability $0.7$ and $40$ minutes with probability $0.3$, depending on traffic. A model has no traffic information and must predict one number.
>
>>[!example]- Which prediction minimizes the expected squared error, and what is its RMSE? How does it compare with predicting $20$?
>>The mean is $\mu = 0.7 \cdot 20 + 0.3 \cdot 40 = 26$ minutes, and the variance is $0.7 \cdot 36 + 0.3 \cdot 196 = 84$. By the Derivation, $\mathbb{E}[(y - c)^2] = 84 + (26 - c)^2$. The minimum is $84$ at $c = 26$, an RMSE of $9.17$ minutes. Predicting $20$ gives $84 + 36 = 120$. The squared-error optimum is a time the route never takes. Under absolute error the best prediction is the median, $20$. Its expected absolute error is $0.3 \cdot 20 = 6$ minutes, against $0.7 \cdot 6 + 0.3 \cdot 14 = 8.4$ minutes at $26$.
