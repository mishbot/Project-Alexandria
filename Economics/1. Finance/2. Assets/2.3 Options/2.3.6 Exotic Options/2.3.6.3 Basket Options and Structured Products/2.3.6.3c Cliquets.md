>[!note] Cliquets
>A **cliquet**, or **ratchet option**, adds up period returns, each held between a local floor and a local cap. Its building block is the **forward start option**, whose strike is fixed at a future date. Let $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = T$ be the reset dates and $S_{t_i}$ the index level at $t_i$. Two payoffs define the family.
>- A forward start call with $t_0 < t_1 < t_2$ fixes its strike at $KS_{t_1}$ on date $t_1$ and expires at $t_2$. Its payoff depends only on the return from $t_1$ to $t_2$.
>- A typical cliquet with notional $1$ pays the sum of the period returns $S_{t_i}/S_{t_{i-1}} - 1$. Each return is first held between the local floor and the local cap.
>
>The two payoffs are
>$$\text{forward start call} = \max\Big(\frac{S_{t_2}}{S_{t_1}} - K,\ 0\Big), \qquad \text{cliquet} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\max\Big(\min\Big(\frac{S_{t_i}}{S_{t_{i-1}}} - 1,\ \text{cap}\Big),\ \text{floor}\Big).$$
>In the forward start call, $t_0$ is the valuation date, $t_1$ the strike fixing date, and $t_2$ the expiry. The strike is a fraction $K$ of the price $S_{t_1}$. The option's price needs the forward implied volatility, the volatility over $[t_1, t_2]$ that today's term structure implies. So cliquets are exposed to the forward skew. Variants change the caps and floors or subtract the period falls from a fixed coupon. Like [[2.3.6.3b Autocallables|autocallables]], cliquets are sold to investors inside structured notes.

# Intuition

A call on an index pays on the final level only. A fall in the last year can cancel the gains of earlier years. A cliquet locks in each period's gain when the period ends. The strike then resets to the new level, so a later fall cannot take back an earlier gain. The local floor removes the falls. The investor pays for this protection with the local cap, which gives up the large gains. So a cliquet suits an investor who expects steady, moderate returns. Each period is a forward start option, an option whose strike is set when the period begins.

# Properties

#### *(Forward Start Options)* A forward start option fixes its strike at a later date, so its price depends on later volatility.

At $t_1$ the strike becomes $KS_{t_1}$. The payoff depends only on the return from $t_1$ to $t_2$. So the option has no exposure to price moves before $t_1$. It depends on the volatility over $[t_1, t_2]$, the **forward implied volatility** that today's term structure implies. It also depends on the skew that will hold at $t_1$, which today's surface does not fix. This exposure is called forward skew risk.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The value below assumes a constant volatility and follows Hull (Chapter 26). The forward volatility formula extends the total variance rule in [[2.3.5b Volatility Surface]].

At $t_1$ the option is a call on $1/S_{t_1}$ shares with strike $KS_{t_1}$. The Black-Scholes-Merton price scales with the stock price and the strike together. So the value at $t_1$ is $c(1, K, t_2 - t_1)$, the price of a call with spot $1$, strike $K$ and life $t_2 - t_1$. That number is known today. Discounting it from $t_1$ gives the value today,
$$f_0 = e^{-r(t_1 - t_0)}\,c(1, K, t_2 - t_1).$$
Here $c$ uses the rate $r$, the dividend yield $q$ and the volatility $\sigma$. Hull states the dollar version $\max(S_{t_2} - S_{t_1}, 0)$, worth $S_0e^{-qt_1}c(1, 1, t_2 - t_1)$ with $t_0 = 0$. With $r = 4\%$, $q = 2\%$, $\sigma = 20\%$, $t_1 = 1$ and $t_2 = 2$, the at-the-money value is $e^{-0.04} \times 0.087395 = 0.08397$. The $\sigma$ to use is the forward volatility $\sigma_F$ over $[t_1, t_2]$. If the implied volatilities to $t_1$ and $t_2$ are $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$, total variance adds up as
$$\sigma_F^2\,(t_2 - t_1) = \sigma_2^2\,t_2 - \sigma_1^2\,t_1.$$
With $18\%$ to one year and $22\%$ to two years, $\sigma_F = 25.4\%$. A price at $22\%$ for the second year would undervalue the option.

#### *(Cliquets)* Each cliquet period is a forward start call spread, and the sum keeps each period's gain up to the cap.

A capped and floored return splits into a floor plus a long call and a short call,
$$\max\big(\min(R_i, \text{cap}), \text{floor}\big) = \text{floor} + \max(R_i - \text{floor}, 0) - \max(R_i - \text{cap}, 0).$$
Here $R_i = S_{t_i}/S_{t_{i-1}} - 1$ is the return over period $i$. The two calls form a [[2.3.2a Vertical Spread|bull call spread]] on the period return, with strikes $1 + \text{floor}$ and $1 + \text{cap}$. Both calls are forward start options that begin at $t_{i-1}$. So a cliquet is a strip of forward start call spreads. Each one needs the volatility and the skew at its own start date. For this reason a cliquet's value needs a model of how the implied volatility surface moves.

#### *(Cliquet Variations)* Variants change the caps and floors, subtract falls from a fixed coupon, or pay on digital, lock-in or multi-asset terms.

- Local caps and floors may differ from period to period. A global cap and a global floor limit the sum of the period terms.
- A **reverse cliquet** starts from a maximum coupon $M$ and subtracts every period's fall.
- A **Napoleon** pays $M$ plus the worst period return, floored at zero.
- Digital cliquets, cliquets with a lock-in feature (accumulators), and cliquets on several assets are further variants.

The reverse cliquet and the Napoleon pay
$$\text{reverse cliquet} = \max\Big(M - \sum_{i=1}^{n}\max\Big(1 - \frac{S_{t_i}}{S_{t_{i-1}}},\ 0\Big),\ 0\Big), \qquad \text{Napoleon} = \max\Big(M + \min_{i = 1, \dots, n}\Big(\frac{S_{t_i}}{S_{t_{i-1}}} - 1\Big),\ 0\Big).$$
Both pay the most when no period falls. The reverse cliquet subtracts every fall, and the Napoleon subtracts only the worst one. On the path in the animation, a reverse cliquet with $M = 20\%$ pays $20\% - 6\% - 2\% = 12\%$. A Napoleon with $M = 8\%$ pays $8\% - 6\% = 2\%$.

# Derivation

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules say a cliquet's value needs a model of how the implied volatility surface moves. The value below assumes a constant volatility, as in Hull's forward start formula, and so ignores the forward skew.

We value a cliquet with $n$ periods of equal length $\Delta t$, with the whole sum paid at $T = n\Delta t$. The local cap and floor are the same in every period. The rate $r$, the dividend yield $q$ and the volatility $\sigma$ are constant. Write $c(1, K, \Delta t)$ for the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton]] call with spot $1$, strike $K$ and life $\Delta t$.

1. Split each period term into the floor plus a call spread on $R_i$, as in the (Cliquets) property.
2. The floor is a fixed amount paid at $T$, so it is worth $\text{floor} \times e^{-rT}$ today.
3. The call spread is a pair of forward start calls that start at $t_{i-1}$, with strikes $1 + \text{floor}$ and $1 + \text{cap}$. At $t_{i-1}$ it is worth $c(1, 1 + \text{floor}, \Delta t) - c(1, 1 + \text{cap}, \Delta t)$ for a payment at $t_i$. Call this difference $C_\Delta$.
4. The payment comes at $T$, not at $t_i$. Waiting from $t_i$ to $T$ adds a discount factor $e^{-r(T - t_i)}$. So at $t_{i-1}$ the term is worth $e^{-r(T - t_i)}C_\Delta$.
5. Discounting from $t_{i-1}$ to today gives $e^{-rt_{i-1}}e^{-r(T - t_i)}C_\Delta = e^{-r(T - \Delta t)}C_\Delta$, since $t_i - t_{i-1} = \Delta t$.

Every period has the same value, so the cliquet is worth
$$V_0 = n\Big[\text{floor} \times e^{-rT} + e^{-r(T - \Delta t)}\big(c(1, 1 + \text{floor}, \Delta t) - c(1, 1 + \text{cap}, \Delta t)\big)\Big].$$
Here $n$ is the number of periods and $T = n\Delta t$. In practice the forward volatility and the skew differ from period to period. Each term then needs its own inputs, and the terms are no longer equal.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Cliquet on a Four-Year Path
>A four-year cliquet on an index has a notional of \$100,000 and resets every year. Each annual return is capped at $6\%$ and floored at $0$. The index returns are $+5\%$, $+12\%$, $-8\%$ and $+3\%$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the cliquet's payment, and compare it with a four-year at-the-money call. Then compare it with a reverse cliquet with $M = 15\%$ and a Napoleon with $M = 10\%$.
>>The cliquet keeps $5\%$ and cuts $12\%$ to the cap of $6\%$. It raises $-8\%$ to the floor of $0$ and keeps $3\%$. So it pays $14\%$, or \$14,000.
>>
>>The index ends at $1.05 \times 1.12 \times 0.92 \times 1.03 = 1.11438$ times its start. An at-the-money call on the whole period pays $11.44\%$, or \$11,438. The cliquet pays more, because its floor removes the third year's fall. The call's payment includes that fall.
>>
>>The reverse cliquet subtracts every fall from $15\%$. Only the third year falls, so it pays $15\% - 8\% = 7\%$, or \$7,000. The Napoleon adds the worst return to $10\%$, so it pays $10\% - 8\% = 2\%$, or \$2,000.

>[!example] (Medium) A Forward Start Call and the Forward Volatility
>A bank sells a forward start call on an index with a notional of \$1,000,000. In six months the strike is set at that day's index level. The option then runs for one more year, so $t_1 = 0.5$ and $t_2 = 1.5$. It pays $\$1{,}000{,}000 \times \max(S_{t_2}/S_{t_1} - 1, 0)$. The risk-free rate is $3\%$ and the dividend yield is $1\%$, both continuously compounded. Implied volatility is $20\%$ to six months and $24\%$ to eighteen months.
>
>>[!example]- Find the forward volatility, the option's value, and the error from pricing it at $24\%$.
>>Total variance adds up over the two intervals:
>>$$\sigma_F^2 \times 1 = 0.24^2 \times 1.5 - 0.20^2 \times 0.5 = 0.0864 - 0.0200 = 0.0664.$$
>>So $\sigma_F = 25.77\%$. The forward volatility is higher than either quoted volatility, because the term structure rises.
>>
>>At $t_1$ the option is worth $c(1, 1, 1)$, a one-year at-the-money call with spot $1$. With $\sigma_F = 0.25768$,
>>$$d_1 = \frac{0.03 - 0.01 + 0.25768^2/2}{0.25768} = \frac{0.05320}{0.25768} = 0.20646, \qquad d_2 = 0.20646 - 0.25768 = -0.05123.$$
>>With $N(d_1) = 0.58178$ and $N(d_2) = 0.47957$, the call is $c = e^{-0.01}(0.58178) - e^{-0.03}(0.47957) = 0.57599 - 0.46540 = 0.11059$. Discounting from $t_1$ gives $f_0 = e^{-0.015} \times 0.11059 = 0.10895$, or \$108,948. A simulation of one million paths gives $0.10893 \pm 0.00018$. It uses $20\%$ volatility for six months and $25.77\%$ after that.
>>
>>At $24\%$ the same steps give $c = 0.10376$ and a value of \$102,211. That price is \$6,737 too low. A bank that used the eighteen-month volatility would ignore the higher volatility that the term structure implies after six months.

>[!example] (Hard) Valuing a Three-Year Cliquet
>A note pays the sum of three annual index returns per unit of notional. Each return is capped at $8\%$ and floored at $0$. It pays the whole sum at $T = 3$. The risk-free rate is $3\%$ and the dividend yield is $1\%$, both continuously compounded. Assume a constant volatility of $20\%$.
>
>>[!example]- Value the cliquet with the formula from the derivation. Compare it with a three-year at-the-money call on the index, and reprice both at volatilities of $15\%$ and $25\%$.
>>Each period needs two one-year calls with spot $1$. At strike $1$, $d_1 = (0.03 - 0.01 + 0.02)/0.20 = 0.2$ and $d_2 = 0$. With $N(0.2) = 0.57926$,
>>$$c(1, 1, 1) = e^{-0.01}(0.57926) - e^{-0.03}(0.5) = 0.57350 - 0.48522 = 0.08827.$$
>>At strike $1.08$, $\ln(1/1.08) = -0.07696$. So $d_1 = (-0.07696 + 0.04)/0.20 = -0.18481$ and $d_2 = -0.38481$. With $N(d_1) = 0.42669$ and $N(d_2) = 0.35019$,
>>$$c(1, 1.08, 1) = e^{-0.01}(0.42669) - 1.08e^{-0.03}(0.35019) = 0.42245 - 0.36703 = 0.05542.$$
>>The call spread is worth $0.03286$. The floor is zero, so each period is worth $e^{-0.06} \times 0.03286 = 0.03094$. The cliquet is worth $3 \times 0.03094 = 0.09283$. A simulation of four million paths gives $0.09284 \pm 0.00003$. The most the note can pay is $24\%$, worth $0.21934$ today.
>>
>>A three-year at-the-money call has $d_1 = (0.02 + 0.02) \times 3/(0.20\sqrt{3}) = 0.34641$ and $d_2 = 0$. With $N(d_1) = 0.63548$, it costs $e^{-0.03}(0.63548) - e^{-0.09}(0.5) = 0.61670 - 0.45697 = 0.15974$. The cliquet costs $58\%$ of the call.
>>
>>At $\sigma = 25\%$ the cliquet is worth $0.09228$ and the call $0.19128$. At $\sigma = 15\%$ they are worth $0.09234$ and $0.12821$. The call moves by about $0.0315$ either way. The cliquet moves by less than $0.0006$, and it falls in both directions. In each call spread, the vegas of the long call and the short call almost cancel. So one flat volatility cannot measure a cliquet's risk. That risk comes from the forward volatility and the forward skew at each reset date.
