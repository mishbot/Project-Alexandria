>[!note] Electric Potential of a Point Charge
>A source point charge $q$ makes the potential
>$$V(r) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r},$$
>where $r$ is the distance from the charge. The zero of potential is at infinity, $V(\infty) = 0$. The sign of $V$ follows the sign of $q$:
>- Near a positive charge, $V > 0$, and $V$ falls toward zero with distance.
>- Near a negative charge, $V < 0$, and $V$ rises toward zero with distance.
>
>The potential falls off as $1/r$. Infinity is the only natural zero. At $r = 0$ the potential is infinite, so it cannot be zero there.
>
>A test charge $q_0$ placed at distance $r$ has the potential energy
>$$U = q_0V = \frac{q\,q_0}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r}.$$
>The source $q$ sets $V$. The test charge $q_0$ only multiplies it.
>
>Potentials from several charges add. With every term using the zero at infinity, the potential at a point P is
>$$V = \sum_i \frac{q_i}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r_i},$$
>where $r_i$ is the distance from charge $q_i$ to P. The sum adds signed numbers:
>- Each $q_i$ enters with its own sign. No absolute values are used.
>- No components and no directions appear. Each term needs only one distance.
>
>Fields and forces add as vectors, with magnitudes on a diagram and signs in the components. Potentials need none of that work. A charge $Q$ placed at P then has the potential energy $U = QV$.

# Intuition

The sign of the potential follows from the work done on a test charge. Bring a positive test charge in from infinity toward a positive charge. The field pushes outward while the test charge moves inward, so the field does negative work. The potential energy rises, and $V$ near the charge is positive. Near a negative charge, the field pulls the test charge in and does positive work. The potential energy falls, and $V$ is negative.

Superposition of potentials is the main practical gain over fields. Each charge contributes one signed number, set by its distance alone. No angle, unit vector, or free-body diagram is needed.

Three charges add their potentials at P as signed bars. The field at P needs vector components instead. Moving P to the center repeats the sum.

# Derivation

Put $q$ at the origin. Its field is $\vec E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\,\hat r$. Pick a reference point at distance $R$, and integrate along a radial line from $R$ to $r$. On that line $\hat r\cdot d\vec s = d\tilde r$, where the tilde marks the integration variable:
$$V(r) - V(R) = -\int_R^r \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,\tilde r^2}\,d\tilde r = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{R}\right).$$
Any other path gives the same result, since only the radial part of each step counts. Now choose the zero. At $R = 0$ the term $1/R$ is infinite, so that choice fails. At $R = \infty$ the term $1/R$ is zero. Setting $V(\infty) = 0$ gives $V(r) = q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$.

For several charges the total field is $\vec E = \sum_i \vec E_i$. The integral of a sum is the sum of the integrals:
$$V(P) = -\int_\infty^P \sum_i \vec E_i\cdot d\vec s = \sum_i\left(-\int_\infty^P \vec E_i\cdot d\vec s\right) = \sum_i \frac{q_i}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r_i}.$$
The dot product turns each field into a number before the sum is taken. That is why potentials add as numbers.

# Properties

#### *(Zero Potential, Nonzero Field)* A point where $V = 0$ can have a strong field, and a point where $\vec E = 0$ can have a large potential.

Take charges $+q$ and $-q$ a distance $d$ apart. At the midpoint their potentials cancel, so $V = 0$. Both fields there point toward $-q$, so they add. Now take two charges $+q$. At their midpoint the fields cancel, so $\vec E = 0$. The potentials add to $4q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 d)$. The potential at one point says nothing about the field there. The field depends on how $V$ changes from point to point.

#### *(Slower Falloff Than the Field)* Doubling the distance halves the potential and quarters the field.

The potential is an integral of the field, and integrating $1/\tilde r^2$ out to infinity gives $1/r$. At $1.0\ \text{m}$ from a $1.0\ \text{nC}$ charge, the potential is $8.99\ \text{V}$ and the field is $8.99\ \text{V/m}$. At $10\ \text{m}$ they are $0.899\ \text{V}$ and $0.0899\ \text{V/m}$. Far from a charge, the potential drops much more slowly than the field.

#### *(Mixed Reference Points)* Giving one term a different reference point shifts the whole sum by a constant.

Suppose the term for $q_1$ uses its zero at a distance $R_1$ from $q_1$. That term becomes $\frac{q_1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{R_1}\right)$. It differs from the usual term by the same constant, $-q_1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 R_1)$, at every point. The sum shifts by that constant everywhere. Potential differences stay the same, and so do all work and energy changes. Only the place where $V = 0$ moves, and $V(\infty)$ is no longer zero. Using infinity for every term keeps $V(\infty) = 0$ for the whole sum.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Near a Small Charged Sphere
>A small sphere carries $+5.0\ \text{nC}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the potential $30\ \text{cm}$ and $10\ \text{cm}$ from it. Then find the work the field does on a $+1.0\ \text{nC}$ test charge moved from the first point to the second.
>>The potentials are
>>$$V(0.30\ \text{m}) = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(5.0 \times 10^{-9})}{0.30} = 150\ \text{V}, \qquad V(0.10\ \text{m}) = 450\ \text{V}.$$
>>The charge moves to a potential $300\ \text{V}$ higher, so $\Delta U = (1.0 \times 10^{-9})(300) = 3.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{J}$. The field does the work $W = -\Delta U = -3.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{J}$. The repulsion pushes against the motion.

>[!example] (Medium) Three Charges on a Rectangle
>A rectangle has corners at $(0, 0)$, $(0.30\ \text{m}, 0)$, $(0.30\ \text{m}, 0.40\ \text{m})$, and $(0, 0.40\ \text{m})$. Charges of $+4.0\ \text{nC}$, $-2.0\ \text{nC}$, and $+3.0\ \text{nC}$ sit at the first, second, and fourth corners.
>
>>[!example]- Find the potential at the empty corner, and the potential energy of a $+1.0\ \text{nC}$ test charge placed there.
>>The distances to the empty corner are $0.50$, $0.40$, and $0.30\ \text{m}$. Each charge enters with its sign:
>>$$V = (8.99 \times 10^{9})\left(\frac{4.0}{0.50} - \frac{2.0}{0.40} + \frac{3.0}{0.30}\right)(10^{-9}) = 8.99 \times 13 = 117\ \text{V}.$$
>>The potential energy is $U = q_0V = (1.0 \times 10^{-9})(117) = 1.2 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{J}$.

>[!example] (Hard) Where the Potential Vanishes
>A charge of $+6.0\ \text{nC}$ sits at the origin, and a charge of $-2.0\ \text{nC}$ sits at $x = 0.40\ \text{m}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find every point on the $x$-axis where $V = 0$. Then find the point where $\vec E = 0$.
>>At position $x$ on the axis, the potential is
>>$$V(x) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{6.0\ \text{nC}}{|x|} - \frac{2.0\ \text{nC}}{|x - 0.40|}\right).$$
>>The absolute values make the distances positive. The charges keep their signs. Setting $V = 0$ requires $|x| = 3\,|x - 0.40|$.
>>- Between the charges, $x = 3(0.40 - x)$ gives $x = 0.30\ \text{m}$.
>>- Beyond the negative charge, $x = 3(x - 0.40)$ gives $x = 0.60\ \text{m}$.
>>- Left of the origin, $-x = 3(0.40 - x)$ gives $x = 0.60\ \text{m}$. That point is not left of the origin, so there is no solution there.
>>
>>The field is zero only where the two fields have equal sizes and opposite directions. Between the charges, both fields point along $+x$, so they cannot cancel. Left of the origin, the larger charge is also the closer one, so its field is larger. Beyond the negative charge, equal sizes need $6.0/x^2 = 2.0/(x - 0.40)^2$. Then $x = \sqrt3\,(x - 0.40)$, and
>>$$x = \frac{0.40\sqrt3}{\sqrt3 - 1} = 0.95\ \text{m}.$$
>>The zeros of $V$ and the zero of $\vec E$ sit at different points.
