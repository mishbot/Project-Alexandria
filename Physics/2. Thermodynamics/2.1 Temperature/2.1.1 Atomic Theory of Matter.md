>[!note] Atomic Theory of Matter
>The **atomic theory of matter** states that all ordinary matter consists of atoms. An atom is the smallest unit of a chemical element, and atoms bond together to form molecules. The atoms and molecules of every substance are in constant random motion. A typical atom is about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ across.
>
>Atomic and molecular masses are measured in **unified atomic mass units**, written $\text{u}$. The scale assigns the carbon-12 atom a mass of exactly $12\ \text{u}$, which fixes the unit in kilograms as
>$$1\ \text{u} = 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}.$$
>On this scale a hydrogen atom has a mass of about $1.008\ \text{u}$. The mass of a molecule is the sum of the masses of its atoms. A water molecule ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) therefore has a mass of about $18.0\ \text{u}$.
>
>The forces between neighboring atoms and molecules are electrical. They attract at moderate separations and repel strongly at very small separations. The phase of a substance depends on how these forces compare with the [[1.3b Kinetic Energy|kinetic energy]] of the particles. In a **solid**, the forces hold each particle near a fixed position, and the particle vibrates about it. In a **liquid**, the particles stay close together but slide past one another. In a **gas**, the particles are far apart and move freely, and the forces act only during collisions.
>
>The theory links the two descriptions used in thermodynamics. The macroscopic description uses quantities that instruments measure directly, such as pressure, volume, and temperature. The microscopic description follows the masses, speeds, and energies of the particles. For an ideal gas, the absolute temperature is proportional to the average translational kinetic energy of the molecules. The note [[2.3.1 Molecular Interpretation of Temperature]] derives this result.

# Intuition

Nobody can see an atom with an ordinary microscope, so the first evidence for atoms was indirect. A classroom demonstration shows the evidence directly. Smoke is blown into a small cavity, a laser lights it from the side, and a camera magnifies the view. The smoke particles, about a micron across, jiggle along jagged, random paths in still air. This **Brownian motion** has a direct explanation in the atomic theory. Air molecules move fast and strike each particle from all sides. At any instant the hits on one side slightly outnumber the hits on the other. The particle gets a small push in a random direction. A particle much larger than a micron receives so many hits that the imbalance averages out, and it stays still. Robert Brown first saw the motion in pollen grains in 1827. Einstein's 1905 analysis of it let experimenters estimate the size of atoms. Probability theory models such a path as a [[4.6 Brownian Motion|Wiener process]].

The atomic theory also sets the plan for the whole subject. A cup of gas holds about $10^{22}$ molecules, and nobody can follow them one at a time. Thermodynamics gives up on the individual molecules and keeps only a handful of macroscopic variables: the internal energy $E_{\text{int}}$, the pressure $P$, the volume $V$, the number of particles $N$, the temperature $T$, and later the entropy $S$. Each one is an average or a total over all the molecules, and with $10^{22}$ of them the averages are steady to many decimal places. Of these variables energy matters most, because it is conserved. Nearly every argument in the subject starts by asking where the energy went.

The three phases differ in how the binding forces compare with the random motion. In a solid, the particles lack the energy to leave their positions, and each one vibrates in place. Heating the solid increases the vibration energy. At the melting point the particles have enough energy to leave their fixed positions, and the solid becomes a liquid. Further heating lets the particles escape from their neighbors completely, and the liquid becomes a gas. The diagram shows the arrangement of the particles in each phase, with arrows for the velocities of the gas particles.

# Properties

#### *(Interatomic Potential)* Two neighboring atoms have a potential energy with a minimum at their equilibrium spacing.

The [[1.3d.1 Potential Energy|potential energy]] $U(r)$ of two atoms depends on the distance $r$ between their centers. The curve rises steeply when the atoms are pushed closer than the equilibrium spacing $r_0$. It rises gently when they are pulled farther apart. The force $F = -dU/dr$ is therefore repulsive for $r < r_0$ and attractive for $r > r_0$. The spacing $r_0$ is a few times $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ in solids and liquids. Near the minimum the force is close to a [[1.2.6. Spring Force|spring force]], so atoms in a solid oscillate about $r_0$. For a displacement $x$ from its site, an atom has potential energy $U \approx \tfrac{1}{2} \kappa x^2$, with $\kappa$ the stiffness of the bond. Its average energy has two parts, $\overline{E} = \overline{K} + \overline{U}$. The curve is not symmetric about $r_0$, and this asymmetry causes [[2.1.4 Thermal Expansion|thermal expansion]].

#### *(Number Density)* Mass density and atomic mass together give the number of atoms in a unit volume.

An atom of atomic mass $A$ (in $\text{u}$) has a mass $m = A \times 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$. A material of [[1.7.1. Fluids|density]] $\rho$ therefore holds $n = \rho / m$ atoms per cubic meter. If each atom occupies a cube of side $d$, then $d = n^{-1/3}$ estimates the spacing between neighbors. For solids and liquids $d$ is about $2$ to $3 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}$, close to the size of the atom itself. The atoms already touch, which is why solids and liquids resist compression. Air at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and one atmosphere has $n \approx 2.7 \times 10^{25}\ \text{m}^{-3}$ and $d \approx 3 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m}$. The molecules sit about ten molecular diameters apart, so a gas compresses easily.

#### *(Macroscopic Averages)* Every macroscopic quantity is an average over an enormous number of particles.

One cubic centimeter of air holds about $2.7 \times 10^{19}$ molecules. Following each one is hopeless. Newton's second law, $m_i \ddot{\vec r}_i = \vec F_i$, for $10^{22}$ molecules moving in three dimensions is about $3 \times 10^{22}$ coupled equations. The macroscopic description replaces them with a few averages. The central one is the average energy per particle,
$$\overline{E} = \frac{E_{\text{int}}}{N},$$
which is proportional to the absolute temperature. A pressure gauge or a thermometer responds to the combined effect of all of them. The random differences between one molecule and the next cancel almost completely in such a large average. The measured pressure and temperature therefore stay steady while individual molecules change speed at every collision. This is why a few macroscopic variables can describe a sample of $10^{19}$ particles. The averaging fails for very small objects, and Brownian motion is the visible result.

#### *(Phase Changes)* During a phase change, added energy separates the particles and leaves their average speed unchanged.

The temperature of a substance stays constant while it melts or boils, even though energy keeps flowing in. The energy raises the potential energy of the particles by pulling them away from their neighbors. The average kinetic energy does not change, so the temperature does not change. The energy needed per kilogram is the [[2.4.4 Latent Heat|latent heat]] of the substance.

# Examples

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not use the atomic mass unit to count atoms. This example follows Giancoli section 17.1.

>[!example] (Easy) Atoms in a Gold Ring
>A ring contains $5.00\ \text{g}$ of pure gold. The atomic mass of gold is $197\ \text{u}$.
>
>>[!example]- How many gold atoms does the ring contain?
>>One gold atom has a mass of
>>$$m = 197 \times 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg} = 3.27 \times 10^{-25}\ \text{kg}.$$
>>The number of atoms is the mass of the ring divided by the mass of one atom,
>>$$N = \frac{5.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{kg}}{3.27 \times 10^{-25}\ \text{kg}} = 1.53 \times 10^{22}.$$
>>A small piece of jewelry therefore holds about $10^{22}$ atoms.

>[!example] (Medium) Spacing of Aluminum Atoms
>Aluminum has a density of $2.70 \times 10^{3}\ \text{kg/m}^3$ and an atomic mass of $27.0\ \text{u}$.
>
>>[!example]- Estimate the distance between neighboring aluminum atoms.
>>One aluminum atom has a mass of $m = 27.0 \times 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg} = 4.48 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg}$. The number of atoms per cubic meter is the density divided by this mass,
>>$$n = \frac{2.70 \times 10^{3}\ \text{kg/m}^3}{4.48 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg}} = 6.02 \times 10^{28}\ \text{m}^{-3}.$$
>>We model each atom as occupying a small cube of side $d$, so that $n d^3 = 1$. The side of the cube is
>>$$d = n^{-1/3} = 2.55 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}.$$
>>The spacing is about the diameter of one atom, as expected for atoms that touch their neighbors.

>[!example] (Medium) Pollen Particle and Baseball
>A pollen particle $1\ \mu\text{m}$ across shows Brownian motion in water. A baseball floating in the same water does not.
>
>>[!example]- Explain the difference with the atomic theory.
>>Water molecules strike both objects at random. The number of hits on one side during a short time varies around its average value. For $N$ hits, the typical imbalance between two sides is a fraction of about $1/\sqrt{N}$ of the total. The small particle receives few enough hits that this fraction is noticeable. The particle also has a mass of only about $5 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{kg}$, so a small net push changes its velocity visibly. The baseball receives a far larger number of hits, and the fractional imbalance is negligible. Its mass of $0.145\ \text{kg}$ is also about $10^{14}$ times larger, so the same push would produce no visible motion.
