>[!note] Linearization
>A nonlinear state-space system
>$$\dot{x}(t) = f(x(t), u(t)), \qquad y(t) = h(x(t), u(t))$$
>can be approximated by a linear-time-invariant (LTI) model near an **equilibrium point** $(x_0, u_0)$ satisfying $f(x_0, u_0) = 0$. Defining perturbations $\delta x = x - x_0$, $\delta u = u - u_0$, $\delta y = y - h(x_0, u_0)$, the first-order Taylor expansion gives
>$$\dot{\delta x} = A\,\delta x + B\,\delta u, \qquad \delta y = C\,\delta x + D\,\delta u,$$
>where the matrices are the **Jacobians** of $f$ and $h$ evaluated at the equilibrium:
>$$A = \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{(x_0, u_0)}, \qquad B = \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\right|_{(x_0, u_0)}, \qquad C = \left.\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}\right|_{(x_0, u_0)}, \qquad D = \left.\frac{\partial h}{\partial u}\right|_{(x_0, u_0)}.$$
>
>The linearized model is valid only *near* $(x_0, u_0)$: it captures the first-order response to small perturbations and discards second-order and higher terms. The approximation is accurate as long as the state stays close to the equilibrium; far-from-equilibrium behavior requires the original nonlinear model or a different local linearization.
>
>Linearization is the bridge that lets all of [[7.1. State-Space Representation|LTI theory]] — eigenvalues, pole placement, controllability — apply to real, nonlinear systems. For a robot, the standard move is: identify an operating configuration $(\theta_0, \dot{\theta}_0 = 0)$ with gravity-compensating torque $\Upsilon_0$, linearize the manipulator dynamics, and design a controller for small deviations.
>
>>[!warning] Linearization Is About a Point, Not a Trajectory
>>Linearization about an equilibrium produces a single LTI model. For tracking a moving reference, one either re-linearizes continuously along the trajectory (yielding a time-varying linear model $\dot{\delta x} = A(t)\,\delta x + B(t)\,\delta u$) or uses feedback linearization to cancel the nonlinearity exactly. The simple "linearize about equilibrium" approach only works for set-point regulation.

# Intuition
Linearization is a multivariable version of replacing a curve by its tangent line. At the equilibrium, $f$ and $h$ are zero (by construction) and vary smoothly with $x$ and $u$. The first-order Taylor expansion $f(x_0 + \delta x, u_0 + \delta u) \approx A\,\delta x + B\,\delta u$ captures how small deviations propagate — the curved nonlinear dynamics look straight if you zoom in enough.

The matrices $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ are all Jacobians. This matches the convention of [[5.2. Robot Jacobians|robot Jacobians]], which are also Jacobians of a nonlinear map (the forward kinematics). In both cases, the Jacobian tells you how small changes in the input translate to small changes in the output. Control-theory Jacobians take partial derivatives with respect to *state* and *input* rather than configuration, but the calculus is identical.

The crucial point is that linearization is a *local* approximation. A simple pendulum linearized about its downward equilibrium behaves like a harmonic oscillator for small angles, but linearization about the upward (inverted) equilibrium gives a fundamentally different system — unstable, with eigenvalues on both sides of the imaginary axis. Which equilibrium you pick determines the LTI model you get.

# Properties

## Procedure

#### *(Find the equilibrium)* Solve $f(x_0, u_0) = 0$ for a fixed-point $(x_0, u_0)$.

Equilibria are the states where, given a constant input, the system remains at rest. For the pendulum $\ddot{\theta} = -g/\ell \sin\theta + u$, the equilibria satisfying $\dot{\theta} = 0$, $\ddot{\theta} = 0$ are $\theta_0 = 0$ (down, $u_0 = 0$) and $\theta_0 = \pi$ (up, $u_0 = 0$). A system may have multiple equilibria, each with its own LTI model.

#### *(Compute the Jacobians)* $A = \partial f/\partial x$, $B = \partial f/\partial u$, $C = \partial h/\partial x$, $D = \partial h/\partial u$, each evaluated at $(x_0, u_0)$.

The Jacobian $A$ captures how small state perturbations feed back into $\dot{x}$; $B$ captures how input perturbations drive $\dot{x}$. The output Jacobians $C$ and $D$ capture how $(x, u)$ perturbations affect the measured $y$. For physical systems $D = 0$ almost always.

#### *(Work in perturbation variables)* $\delta x \triangleq x - x_0$, $\delta u \triangleq u - u_0$, $\delta y \triangleq y - y_0$.

The linearized system governs $\delta x$, not $x$ directly. Converting back to absolute variables requires adding $(x_0, u_0, y_0)$ to the linearized solution. This is straightforward but easy to forget when interpreting simulation results.

## Properties of the Jacobian

#### *(Linear map)* The Jacobian of a linear function $f(x) = Mx$ is just $M$.

This is why LTI systems are their own linearization: $\partial(Ax)/\partial x = A$. For a linear input-affine system $\dot{x} = Ax + Bu$, linearization at any equilibrium gives back the same $(A, B)$.

#### *(Chain rule)* Composition composes Jacobians: if $f = g \circ h$, then $Df = Dg \cdot Dh$.

This is the multivariable chain rule. For cascaded systems (a motor driving a gearbox driving a link), the overall linearization is the product of per-stage Jacobians.

#### *(Symbolic tools)* SymPy, MATLAB Symbolic Math Toolbox, Mathematica, and Maple compute Jacobians symbolically.

For anything beyond a 2- or 3-DoF system, computing $A = \partial f/\partial x$ by hand is error-prone. Symbolic computation is the standard practice; the result is then evaluated numerically at the equilibrium point.

## Validity

#### *(Local approximation)* The linear model agrees with the nonlinear one to first order in $(\delta x, \delta u)$; errors grow quadratically with distance from equilibrium.

If the nonlinear $f$ is Taylor-expanded as $f(x_0 + \delta x) = A\,\delta x + \frac{1}{2}\delta x^T H \delta x + \cdots$, the Hessian term $H$ bounds how fast the linear model degrades. Rule of thumb: within $\sim 10°$ of a joint equilibrium the linear model is usually adequate; beyond $\sim 30°$ you probably need the nonlinear model.

#### *(Stability preservation)* If the linearization has all eigenvalues in the open LHP, the nonlinear system is **locally asymptotically stable** at the equilibrium (Lyapunov's first method).

Linearization provides a rigorous stability test: negative real parts imply local stability of the nonlinear system. Eigenvalues with zero real parts are inconclusive — the nonlinear terms determine stability there.

# Examples
>[!example] Linearizing a Pendulum
>The pendulum $\ddot{\theta} = -(g/\ell) \sin\theta + (1/m\ell^2)\tau$ has state $x = (\theta, \dot{\theta})$. Linearize about the downward equilibrium $(\theta_0, \dot{\theta}_0, \tau_0) = (0, 0, 0)$.
>>[!example]- Solution
>>$f(x, u) = \begin{bmatrix} x_2 \\ -(g/\ell) \sin x_1 + u/(m\ell^2) \end{bmatrix}$.
>>
>>$A = \partial f/\partial x = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -(g/\ell)\cos x_1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. At $x_0 = 0$: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -g/\ell & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.
>>
>>$B = \partial f/\partial u = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/(m\ell^2) \end{bmatrix}$.
>>
>>Eigenvalues of $A$: $\lambda = \pm i\sqrt{g/\ell}$ — purely imaginary, so small oscillations about the downward equilibrium are undamped sinusoids at the pendulum's natural frequency. This matches the expected behavior of a frictionless pendulum.

>[!example] Inverted Pendulum
>Repeat the linearization at the *upward* equilibrium $\theta_0 = \pi$.
>>[!example]- Solution
>>At $x_1 = \pi$: $\cos \pi = -1$, so $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ g/\ell & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.
>>
>>Eigenvalues: $\lambda = \pm \sqrt{g/\ell}$ — one positive, one negative. The system is an unstable saddle: small angular deviations grow exponentially (the pendulum falls). Stabilizing the inverted equilibrium requires feedback — see [[7.6. State Feedback]].

>[!example] Linearizing Manipulator Dynamics
>Linearize the 1-DoF revolute link $I\ddot{\theta} = \tau - \frac{1}{2}mg\ell \sin\theta$ about equilibrium $(\theta_0, 0, \tau_0)$.
>>[!example]- Solution
>>At equilibrium: $\tau_0 = \tfrac{1}{2} m g \ell \sin\theta_0$ (gravity compensation).
>>
>>State $x = (\theta, \dot{\theta})$, input $u = \tau$. $f = (x_2, (u - \tfrac{1}{2}mg\ell \sin x_1)/I)$.
>>
>>$A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -(mg\ell/2I)\cos\theta_0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/I \end{bmatrix}$.
>>
>>For $\theta_0 \in (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$ (arm pointing downward from horizontal), $\cos\theta_0 > 0$ so the $A_{21}$ entry is negative: stable oscillatory dynamics. For $\theta_0 = \pi/2$ (arm horizontal), $A_{21} = 0$ — marginally stable, unstable under nonlinear terms. For $\theta_0 \in (\pi/2, 3\pi/2)$ (arm pointing up), $\cos\theta_0 < 0$, $A_{21} > 0$: unstable equilibrium.
