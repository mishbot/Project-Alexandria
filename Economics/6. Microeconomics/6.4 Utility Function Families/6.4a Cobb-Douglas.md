>[!note] Cobb-Douglas
>A **Cobb-Douglas** utility function over two goods is
>$$u(x_1, x_2) = x_1^{\alpha}x_2^{1-\alpha}, \qquad 0 < \alpha < 1,$$
>defined on bundles $(x_1, x_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2_+$. The exponent $\alpha$ is the weight on good 1, and $1-\alpha$ is the weight on good 2. The general form $x_1^{\alpha}x_2^{\beta}$ with $\alpha, \beta > 0$ represents the same preferences as $x_1^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}x_2^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}$. The log form $\alpha\ln x_1 + (1-\alpha)\ln x_2$ also represents them. The [[6.1.4 Indifference Curves and MRS|marginal rate of substitution]] is
>$$\text{MRS} = -\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\frac{x_2}{x_1},$$
>so indifference curves are smooth and strictly convex, and they approach both axes without touching them. At prices $\mathbf{p} = (p_1, p_2)$ and income $M$, the [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|Marshallian demands]] are
>$$x_1^*(\mathbf{p}, M) = \frac{\alpha M}{p_1}, \qquad x_2^*(\mathbf{p}, M) = \frac{(1-\alpha)M}{p_2}.$$
>For the general form they are $x_1^* = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\frac{M}{p_1}$ and $x_2^* = \frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\frac{M}{p_2}$. The consumer spends the fixed share $p_1x_1^*/M = \alpha$ of income on good 1 and the share $1-\alpha$ on good 2, whatever the prices. Demand for each good does not depend on the other good's price, and both goods are [[6.3.3 Income Changes|normal]]. The [[6.2.4 Indirect Utility Function|indirect utility]] and the [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization|expenditure function]] are
>$$v(\mathbf{p}, M) = M\left(\frac{\alpha}{p_1}\right)^{\alpha}\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{p_2}\right)^{1-\alpha}, \qquad e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = \bar u\left(\frac{p_1}{\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}\left(\frac{p_2}{1-\alpha}\right)^{1-\alpha}.$$
>Here $\bar u$ is a target utility level, and $e$ is the least income that reaches it. The Hicksian (compensated) demands, derived in [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]], are
>$$h_1(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = \bar u\left(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{1-\alpha}, \qquad h_2(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = \bar u\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\,\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right)^{\alpha}.$$
>Each $h_i$ is the amount of good $i$ in the cheapest bundle with utility $\bar u$. Cobb-Douglas is the $\rho \to 0$ case of [[6.4d CES Utility|CES utility]]. It sits between [[6.4b Perfect Substitutes|perfect substitutes]] and [[6.4c Perfect Complements|perfect complements]], and it is the default model when budget shares are stable.

# Intuition

Many households spend a fairly stable fraction of their budget on broad categories such as housing. When one category becomes more expensive, they buy less of it and keep its share of spending about the same. Cobb-Douglas builds this behavior into the utility function. The consumer is willing to substitute, since indifference curves bend smoothly. Yet a price rise on good 1 leaves the quantity of good 2 unchanged. Two forces act on good 2 and they cancel. The consumer shifts toward good 2 because it is now relatively cheap. The consumer is also poorer and so buys less of everything.

The diagram uses $\alpha = 1/3$, $M = 12$, $p_1 = 1$ and $p_2 = 2$. The consumer spends one third of income, $4$, on good 1 and two thirds, $8$, on good 2. The optimum is $A = (4, 4)$, where the budget line touches the indifference curve $u = 4$. Curves above $u = 4$ are out of reach, and curves below it cross the budget line at bundles the consumer can improve on.

# Properties

#### *(Why Shares Are Constant)* The tangency condition fixes the ratio of spending on the two goods.

Setting the MRS equal to $-p_1/p_2$ gives $\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{x_2}{x_1} = \frac{p_1}{p_2}$. Rearranged, this reads $\frac{p_2x_2}{p_1x_1} = \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}$. Prices and quantities enter only as spending, so a price change moves the quantity and leaves the spending ratio fixed. Combined with the budget $p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 = M$, spending on good 1 is $\alpha M$. An economist can therefore estimate $\alpha$ as the observed budget share of good 1. If observed shares move a lot when prices change, Cobb-Douglas is the wrong model and [[6.4d CES Utility|CES]] with $\rho \neq 0$ fits better.

#### *(Drawing the Optimum)* The optimal bundle lies the fraction $\alpha$ of the way along the budget line, starting from the $x_2$ intercept.

Since $x_1^* = \alpha \cdot M/p_1$, the optimum's horizontal coordinate is the fraction $\alpha$ of the $x_1$ intercept. Its height $x_2^*$ is the fraction $1-\alpha$ of the $x_2$ intercept $M/p_2$. On an exam graph, draw the budget line, mark this point, and draw the indifference curve tangent there. With $\alpha = 1/2$ the optimum is the midpoint of the budget line. A rise in $p_1$ pivots the line around $M/p_2$, and the new optimum stays at the same height $x_2^*$.

#### *(Cancelling Effects)* A change in $p_2$ leaves $x_1^*$ unchanged because its substitution and income effects on good 1 are equal and opposite.

In the [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]], the cross substitution effect $\partial h_1/\partial p_2$ equals $\alpha(1-\alpha)M/(p_1p_2) > 0$. The income effect $-x_2^*\,\partial x_1^*/\partial M$ equals the same amount with a minus sign. The two cancel at every price. [[6.3.6 Complements and Substitutes]] therefore classifies the goods as net substitutes, but neither gross substitutes nor gross complements. Within CES, Cobb-Douglas is therefore the dividing case. Goods with $0 < \rho < 1$ are gross substitutes, and goods with $\rho < 0$ are gross complements.

#### *(Only Ratios Matter)* Scaling both exponents or taking logs changes the utility numbers and leaves demand unchanged.

The identity $x_1^{\alpha}x_2^{\beta} = \left(x_1^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}x_2^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}\right)^{\alpha+\beta}$ writes the general form as a strictly increasing function of the normalized form. By [[6.1.3 Utility Functions|ordinality]], both rank bundles the same way. So $x_1x_2$ and $x_1^{1/2}x_2^{1/2}$ give the same demands, while $x_1^{1/2}x_2^{1/2}$ and $x_1^{1/3}x_2^{2/3}$ do not. The log form is the easiest to differentiate. Its first-order conditions are $\alpha/x_1 = \lambda p_1$ and $(1-\alpha)/x_2 = \lambda p_2$, which give $\lambda = 1/M$. The [[7.2 Lagrange Multipliers|multiplier]] $\lambda$ is the marginal utility of income, so it depends on the utility scale. For $x_1^{\alpha}x_2^{1-\alpha}$ it equals $v/M$ instead.

#### *(Second-Order Check)* For $0 < \alpha < 1$ the shortcut test on the bordered Hessian passes at every interior bundle.

The second derivatives are $u''_{x_1x_1} = \alpha(\alpha-1)x_1^{\alpha-2}x_2^{1-\alpha} < 0$, $u''_{x_2x_2} < 0$, and $u''_{x_1x_2} = \alpha(1-\alpha)x_1^{\alpha-1}x_2^{-\alpha} > 0$. The [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|bordered Hessian]] determinant $-p_1^2u''_{x_2x_2} + 2p_1p_2u''_{x_1x_2} - p_2^2u''_{x_1x_1}$ is then a sum of positive terms, so the tangency is a maximum. For the general form with an exponent above $1$, such as $x_1^2x_2^2$, $u''_{x_1x_1} > 0$ and the shortcut does not apply. The full determinant is still positive at the optimum. At a tangency point, a strictly increasing transformation of $u$ multiplies the determinant by a positive number, so its sign cannot change.

#### *(Endowments)* Demand starts to depend on the other price when income comes from selling something the consumer owns.

The formulas above assume income $M$ is fixed in money. In [[6.5.2 Labor Supply]] the consumer owns 24 hours and sells some of them at wage $w$. Income becomes $M + 24w$, and the wage is also the price of leisure. With $u = c^{\alpha}l^{1-\alpha}$, demand for consumption is $c^* = \alpha(M + 24w)/p$, which rises with the price of the other good. The share rule still holds once income is measured as the market value of everything the consumer owns.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Same Budget, Different Functions
>Three roommates each spend $M = 40$ a week on coffee at $p_1 = 2$ a cup and sandwiches at $p_2 = 5$ each. Their utility functions are $u_A = x_1^{1/4}x_2^{3/4}$, $u_B = \ln x_1 + 3\ln x_2$, and $u_C = x_1^{3/4}x_2^{1/4}$, where $x_1$ is cups of coffee and $x_2$ is sandwiches.
>
>>[!example]- Which roommates buy the same bundle? Find every bundle.
>>Roommate B's function is $\ln(x_1x_2^3)$. The function $x_1x_2^3$ equals $(x_1^{1/4}x_2^{3/4})^4$, and both $\ln$ and the fourth power are strictly increasing. So A and B have the same preferences and the same demand. Both spend the share $1/4$ on coffee, which gives $x_1^* = 10/2 = 5$ cups and $x_2^* = 30/5 = 6$ sandwiches.
>>
>>Roommate C uses the same two numbers in the other order. C spends the share $3/4$ on coffee, so $x_1^* = 30/2 = 15$ cups and $x_2^* = 10/5 = 2$ sandwiches. Only the ratio of the exponents matters, and C's ratio differs from A's.

>[!example] (Medium) A Restaurant Price Rise
>A graduate student spends $M = 600$ a month on food. Utility is $u = x_1^{0.4}x_2^{0.6}$, where $x_1$ is restaurant meals at $p_1 = 15$ and $x_2$ is units of groceries at $p_2 = 5$. Restaurant prices then rise by $25\%$ to $p_1' = 18.75$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the bundle before and after the price rise, and the spending on each category.
>>Before the rise the student spends $0.4 \times 600 = 240$ on meals and $360$ on groceries. That buys $x_1^* = 240/15 = 16$ meals and $x_2^* = 360/5 = 72$ units of groceries.
>>
>>After the rise the shares stay the same. Meals now cost $18.75$, so $x_1^* = 240/18.75 = 12.8$ meals. Groceries stay at $x_2^* = 72$. Spending on meals is still $240$, so the number of meals falls by exactly the factor $1/1.25$. The student does not shift money into groceries, because the substitution effect toward groceries and the income effect away from them cancel.

>[!example] (Hard) Compensating the Student
>Take the student from the previous example. A parent wants to raise the monthly allowance by the smallest amount that restores the student's old utility after the price rise.
>
>>[!example]- How much extra income is needed, and why is it less than the extra cost of the old bundle?
>>The old utility is $\bar u = 16^{0.4}\,72^{0.6} \approx 39.45$. The expenditure function is proportional to $\bar u$ and to $p_1^{\alpha}$, and $e(\mathbf{p}, \bar u) = 600$ at the old prices. At the new prices it is therefore
>>$$e(\mathbf{p}', \bar u) = 600 \times 1.25^{0.4} \approx 656.02.$$
>>The parent must add about $56.02$.
>>
>>Buying the old bundle at the new prices would cost $16 \times 18.75 + 72 \times 5 = 660$, which is $60$ more. The cheapest bundle that reaches $\bar u$ at the new prices is the Hicksian bundle $h_1 \approx 14.00$ meals and $h_2 \approx 78.72$ units of groceries, which costs $656.02$. The student gives up two meals and buys more groceries. By substituting, the student reaches $\bar u$ for about $3.98$ less than the cost of the old bundle.
