>[!note] 2D Image Transformations
>In images, **affine transformations** map points from one coordinate system to another, changing their position, orientation, or scale while **preserving parallelism**. To aid with translation, points in **heterogenous coordinates** $\begin{bmatrix}x & y\end{bmatrix}^T$ are converted to points in **homogeneous coordinates** $\begin{bmatrix}x & y & 1\end{bmatrix}^T$, such that given a point in homogeneous coordinates, one returns to heterogeneous by dividing by $w$:
>$$\begin{bmatrix}x & y & w\end{bmatrix}^T \to \begin{bmatrix}x / w & y / w\end{bmatrix}^T.$$
>
>Generally, a 2D affine transformation can be defined as the matrix of the form
>$$A=\begin{bmatrix}a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix},$$
>such that any general $A$ has exactly 6 degrees of freedom.
>
>There are numerous affine transformation that can interact with a homogeneous point $p$:
>1. **Scaling** is a 2-DoF transformation that enlarges or shrinks objects relative to the origin, such that
>$$S=\begin{bmatrix}s_{x} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & s_{y} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}.$$
>Uniform scaling has $s_x = s_y$, whereas non-uniform (**anisotropic**) scaling changes the aspect ratio of the image.
>⠀
>2. **Translation** (2 DoF) shifts all points by a vector $\begin{bmatrix}t_{x} & t_{y}\end{bmatrix}^T$, such that
>$$T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & t_x \\ 0 & 1 & t_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$
>In homogeneous coordinates, translation becomes a simple matrix multiplication $p’ = T p$, replacing addition in Cartesian space.
>⠀
>3. **Rotation** (1 DoF) rotates the image counterclockwise by an angle $\theta$, such that
>$$R = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta & 0 \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$
>Chaining two rotations with angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is the same than applying a rotation with an angle $\alpha+\beta$. A rotation matrix is always orthogonal with $\det R = 1$, and therefore $R^{-1}=R^T$.
>⠀
>4. **Shearing** (2 DoF) distorts shape while keeping lines parallel (i.e., turns a rectangle into a parallelogram), such that
>$$Q = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & q_x & 0 \\ q_y & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$
>
>Homogeneous coordinates also allow chaining the four transformations as matrix multiplications. For example, a shearing matrix $Q$ can be the product of a rotate, then scale non-uniformly (or vice versa). Similarly, a **rigid (Euclidean) transform** $TR$ is the product of a rotation and translation transformations (and preserves lengths), whereas a **similarity transform** $sTR$ adds scaling to the equation (and only preserves angles).
