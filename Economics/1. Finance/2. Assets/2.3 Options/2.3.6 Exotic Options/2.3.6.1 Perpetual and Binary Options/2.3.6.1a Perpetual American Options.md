>[!note] Perpetual American Options
>A **perpetual American option** never expires, so the holder may exercise at any time. It is one of the simplest [[2.3.6 Exotic Options|exotic options]]. An exotic option is a contract whose payoff rule differs from a standard call or put. The option is priced here in the Black-Scholes-Merton setting. The stock price $S$ follows geometric Brownian motion with volatility $\sigma$. The risk-free rate $r$ and the dividend yield $q$ are constant and continuously compounded.
>
>The value $f(S)$ of a perpetual option depends on the stock price alone. In the region where the holder waits, $f$ solves the [[2.3.3e Black-Scholes-Merton Equation|BSM equation]] without its time derivative. This is the ordinary differential equation (ODE)
>$$\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2f''(S) + (r - q)Sf'(S) - rf(S) = 0.$$
>A power $S^\alpha$ solves the ODE when $\alpha$ is a root of the characteristic quadratic. With $b = r - q - \sigma^2/2$, the quadratic and its two roots are
>$$\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\alpha(\alpha - 1) + (r - q)\alpha - r = 0, \qquad \alpha_\pm = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 + 2r\sigma^2}}{\sigma^2}.$$
>For $r > 0$ and $q > 0$ the roots satisfy $\alpha_+ > 1$ and $\alpha_- < 0$. The holder exercises the first time $S$ reaches an exercise level $S^*$. **Value matching** sets the value equal to the exercise payoff at $S^*$. **Smooth pasting** sets their slopes equal there, and it picks the $S^*$ that gives the highest value.
>- The **perpetual call** keeps $\alpha_+$ and is exercised when $S$ rises to $S^* = \alpha_+K/(\alpha_+ - 1)$, above the strike $K$. Below that level it is worth $C(S) = (S^* - K)(S/S^*)^{\alpha_+}$.
>- The **perpetual put** keeps $\alpha_-$ and is exercised when $S$ falls to $S^* = \alpha_-K/(\alpha_- - 1)$, below $K$. Above that level it is worth $P(S) = (K - S^*)(S/S^*)^{\alpha_-}$.
>- A **perpetual convertible preferred stock** pays a coupon at rate $Q$ on a notional $N$ and converts into $R_C$ shares. It is worth $QN/r$ plus a term in $S^{\alpha_+}$, and the holder converts when $S$ rises to $S^* = \frac{QN}{rR_C}\,\frac{\alpha_+}{\alpha_+ - 1}$.
>
>In the waiting region each value is a constant times a power of $S$. Its delta and vega are
>$$\Delta = \frac{\alpha f}{S}, \qquad \mathcal{V} = f\ln\Big(\frac{S}{S^*}\Big)\frac{d\alpha}{d\sigma}, \qquad \frac{d\alpha}{d\sigma} = -\frac{\sigma\alpha(\alpha - 1)}{\sigma^2(\alpha - \frac{1}{2}) + r - q},$$
>with $\alpha = \alpha_+$ for the call and $\alpha = \alpha_-$ for the put. Both vegas are positive. A **perpetual warrant** is a call with no expiry that a company issues on its own shares. It is the traded form of the perpetual call. Perpetual puts trade mostly over the counter in offshore markets and are common in cryptocurrency markets.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The perpetual put's closed form, its delta, and the vega of perpetual options are covered only in the homework. The lecture states that the put follows from the call's steps and points to Hull.

>[!warning] Notation
>The lecture writes the roots as $\alpha^+$ and $\alpha^-$. This note writes $\alpha_+$ and $\alpha_-$, and it writes vega as $\mathcal{V}$, as in [[2.3.4c Vega and Rho]].

# Intuition

A perpetual option faces the same problem every day. Its remaining life is always infinite, and $r$, $q$ and $\sigma$ do not change. Only the stock price differs from one day to the next. So the value depends on $S$ alone. The holder follows a fixed rule and exercises the first time $S$ crosses $S^*$. For a put, exercise pays $K - S$ now, and the holder earns interest on $K$ from today. Waiting keeps the chance of a larger payoff after a further fall. A rise in the stock can never make the payoff negative. With a higher $\sigma$, waiting is worth more, so the put's $S^*$ falls.

# Properties

#### *(Choosing the Root)* Each option keeps the root whose power stays bounded at the far end of its waiting region.

Write the quadratic as $g(\alpha) = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\alpha(\alpha - 1) + (r - q)\alpha - r$. Its leading coefficient is positive, and $g(0) = -r < 0$ and $g(1) = -q < 0$. An upward parabola that is negative at $0$ and at $1$ has one root below $0$ and one above $1$. A call waits while $S < S^*$, so its value must stay finite as $S \to 0$. The term $S^{\alpha_-}$ grows without bound there, so its coefficient is zero. A put waits while $S > S^*$ and is worth at most $K$. The term $S^{\alpha_+}$ grows without bound as $S \to \infty$, so the put drops it. The signs also place the exercise levels. For the call, $\alpha_+/(\alpha_+ - 1) > 1$, so $S^* > K$. For the put, $\alpha_-/(\alpha_- - 1) = |\alpha_-|/(|\alpha_-| + 1) < 1$, so $S^* < K$.

The call needs $q > 0$ to be exercised at all. With $q = 0$, $g(1) = 0$, so $\alpha_+ = 1$ and $S^*$ is infinite. The holder never exercises, as for an American call on a stock without dividends ([[2.3.1c Early Exercise]]). Take $K = 100$, $r = 5\%$ and $\sigma = 25\%$. With $q = 3\%$, $S^* = 318.51$ and $C(100) = \$40.37$. With $q = 1\%$, $S^* = 853.95$ and $C(100) = \$66.43$. With $q = 0.1\%$, $C(100) = \$93.53$, close to the share price of $\$100$.

#### *(Smooth Pasting)* The best exercise level is the one at which the value curve meets the payoff with the same slope.

Fix any exercise level $L$ below $K$ for a put. Value matching at $L$ gives the value of the rule "exercise when $S$ first falls to $L$",
$$f_L(S) = (K - L)\Big(\frac{S}{L}\Big)^{\alpha_-}, \qquad S > L.$$
Here $f_L$ is the put's value under that rule. Every $L$ meets value matching, so value matching alone does not fix $S^*$. The holder picks the $L$ with the highest value. Setting $\partial f_L/\partial L = 0$ gives $\alpha_-(K - L)/L = -1$. This equation says that the slope of $f_L$ at $L$ equals $-1$, the slope of the payoff. So maximizing the value and smooth pasting give the same $S^*$. The delta follows from the power form. Differentiating $AS^\alpha$ gives $\alpha AS^{\alpha - 1} = \alpha f/S$. At $S^*$ this delta equals $-1$ for the put and $+1$ for the call. So the price and the delta are both continuous at the exercise level. Take the put in the (Medium) example below, with $K = 40$ and $S^* = 19.65$. Exercising at $L = 25$ makes the put worth $\$8.50$ at $S = 45$, below the best value of $\$9.14$. Its value curve meets the payoff at slope $-0.58$, a kink. Exercising at $L = 15$ gives $\$8.65$ and a slope of $-1.61$.

#### *(Vega)* Vega is positive and comes only from the root, since a small shift in $S^*$ has no first-order effect.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The vega of a perpetual option is covered only in the homework.

The put's value $f = (K - S^*)(S/S^*)^{\alpha_-}$ depends on $\sigma$ through the root and through $S^*$. Holding $S$ fixed, the chain rule gives
$$\mathcal{V} = \frac{df}{d\sigma} = \Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \alpha} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial S^*}\,\frac{dS^*}{d\alpha}\Big)\frac{d\alpha}{d\sigma}.$$
The first term in the bracket is the direct effect of the root. The second term acts through the exercise level. The holder chose $S^*$ to maximize $f$, so $\partial f/\partial S^* = 0$ there and the second term drops out. For the same reason, a holder who keeps an old $S^*$ after $\sigma$ moves loses only a second-order amount. The value is a constant times $(S/S^*)^\alpha$, so $\partial f/\partial \alpha = f\ln(S/S^*)$. Differentiating the quadratic in $\sigma$, with $\alpha$ a function of $\sigma$, gives
$$\sigma\alpha(\alpha - 1) + \Big[\sigma^2\big(\alpha - \tfrac{1}{2}\big) + r - q\Big]\frac{d\alpha}{d\sigma} = 0.$$
The bracket equals $\sigma^2\alpha + b$. It is $+\sqrt{b^2 + 2r\sigma^2}$ at $\alpha_+$ and $-\sqrt{b^2 + 2r\sigma^2}$ at $\alpha_-$. For the put, $\alpha_-(\alpha_- - 1) > 0$ and the bracket is negative, so $d\alpha_-/d\sigma > 0$. In the waiting region $\ln(S/S^*) > 0$, so the vega is positive. For the call, $d\alpha_+/d\sigma < 0$ and $\ln(S/S^*) < 0$, so its vega is positive too. A higher $\sigma$ raises the call's $S^*$ and lowers the put's, so both holders wait longer.

#### *(Convertible Preferred Stock)* A perpetual convertible preferred share is a perpetual bond plus a perpetual call on $R_C$ shares.

The holder receives the coupon $NQ\,dt$ in each short period $dt$. A hedged position $\Pi = f - mS$, short $m = \partial f/\partial S$ shares, pays the dividends $qSm\,dt$. The riskless-return argument of the [[2.3.3e Black-Scholes-Merton Equation|BSM equation]] then gives
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + (r - q)S\frac{\partial f}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial S^2} - rf + NQ = 0.$$
The coupon adds the constant $NQ$. So the ODE without the time derivative is inhomogeneous, meaning it has a term without $f$. Its general solution is one particular solution plus the solution of the homogeneous ODE. The constant $QN/r$ solves the inhomogeneous ODE. It is the value of a perpetual bond paying $QN$ a year. The term in $S^{\alpha_-}$ drops out, because the value stays finite as $S \to 0$. Value matching, $AS^{*\alpha_+} + QN/r = R_CS^*$, and the choice of the best $S^*$ give
$$f(S) = \frac{QN}{r} + \frac{QN/r}{\alpha_+ - 1}\Big(\frac{\alpha_+ - 1}{\alpha_+}\,\frac{R_CS}{QN/r}\Big)^{\alpha_+}, \qquad S < S^*,$$
and $f(S) = R_CS$ for $S \ge S^*$. The first term is the bond value, and the second is the value of the right to convert. Take $N = \$100$, $Q = 6\%$, $R_C = 4$, $r = 5\%$, $q = 4\%$ and $\sigma = 25\%$. Then $\alpha_+ = 1.6498$, the bond value is $\$120$, and $S^* = 76.17$. At $S = 25$ the share is worth $\$149.39$. That is above both its bond value and its conversion value of $\$100$. Real issues add features that this model leaves out.
- Coupons, paid as dividends, arrive on discrete dates.
- A non-cumulative clause lets the issuer skip a dividend without paying it later.
- A call provision lets the issuer redeem the shares at a stated price.
- A put provision lets the holder sell the shares back to the issuer at a stated price.
- A contingent conversion ("CoCo") provision makes conversion depend on a stated trigger event.
- Credit risk means the issuer may stop paying, so the bond part is worth less than $QN/r$.

>[!warning] Typo in the Printed Convertible Value
>The lecture's final convertible formula omits the bond term $QN/r$ and the factor $R_C$ inside the bracket. The version above meets value matching and smooth pasting at $S^*$. With the numbers above, the printed version gives $\$18.75$ at $S^*$, where the share must be worth $R_CS^* = \$304.67$.

# Derivation

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The perpetual put is covered only in the homework, and the lecture points to Hull for it. Hull's form of the values at the end of this section is not in the modules.

We want the value of a perpetual American put with strike $K$. The holder waits while $S > S^*$ and exercises when $S$ falls to $S^*$.

In the waiting region the value $P(S)$ solves the ODE in the callout. A trial solution $P = AS^\alpha$ gives $SP' = \alpha AS^\alpha$ and $S^2P'' = \alpha(\alpha - 1)AS^\alpha$. Substituting and dividing by $AS^\alpha$ gives the characteristic quadratic, with roots $\alpha_+ > 1$ and $\alpha_- < 0$. The general solution is
$$P(S) = AS^{\alpha_+} + BS^{\alpha_-}, \qquad S > S^*.$$
The coefficients $A$ and $B$ are constants to be found. A put is worth at most $K$, while $S^{\alpha_+} \to \infty$ as $S \to \infty$. So $A = 0$ and $P = BS^{\alpha_-}$.

At $S^*$ the value matches the payoff $K - S$, and the slopes match:
$$BS^{*\alpha_-} = K - S^*, \qquad \alpha_-BS^{*\alpha_- - 1} = -1.$$
The first equation is value matching, and the second is smooth pasting. Dividing the second by the first removes $B$ and gives $\alpha_-/S^* = -1/(K - S^*)$. Solving for $S^*$ gives
$$S^* = \frac{\alpha_-}{\alpha_- - 1}K.$$
The ratio lies between $0$ and $1$ because $\alpha_- < 0$, so $S^* < K$. Substituting $B = (K - S^*)S^{*-\alpha_-}$ gives the complete value function,
$$P(S) = \begin{cases}(K - S^*)\Big(\dfrac{S}{S^*}\Big)^{\alpha_-}, & S > S^*, \\ K - S, & S \le S^*.\end{cases}$$
The upper branch is the value of waiting, and the lower branch is immediate exercise. The delta is $\alpha_-P/S$ above $S^*$ and $-1$ below it. The vega follows from the (Vega) property above.

Hull writes $w = r - q - \sigma^2/2$, which is $b$ here, so his roots are the same $\alpha_\pm$. He states both values in terms of the strike:
$$C = \frac{K}{\alpha_+ - 1}\Big(\frac{\alpha_+ - 1}{\alpha_+}\,\frac{S}{K}\Big)^{\alpha_+}, \qquad P = \frac{K}{1 - \alpha_-}\Big(\frac{\alpha_- - 1}{\alpha_-}\,\frac{S}{K}\Big)^{\alpha_-}.$$
These are the values above. For the put, $K - S^* = K/(1 - \alpha_-)$ and $S/S^* = \frac{\alpha_- - 1}{\alpha_-}\,\frac{S}{K}$. For the call, $S^* - K = K/(\alpha_+ - 1)$, and the ratio $S/S^*$ has the same form with $\alpha_+$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Valuing a Perpetual Warrant
>A stock trades at $\$36$ and pays a continuous dividend yield of $5\%$. Its volatility is $25\%$, and the risk-free rate is $3\%$ per year with continuous compounding. An investor holds a perpetual warrant that lets her buy one share for $\$30$ at any time. Treat the warrant as a perpetual American call with $K = \$30$.
>
>>[!example]- Find $\alpha_+$, the exercise level and the warrant's value at $S = \$36$. Decide whether to exercise now, find the delta, and check the delta at the exercise level.
>>Here $b = 0.03 - 0.05 - 0.03125 = -0.05125$ and $\sqrt{b^2 + 2r\sigma^2} = \sqrt{0.002627 + 0.00375} = 0.079853$. The positive root is
>>$$\alpha_+ = \frac{0.05125 + 0.079853}{0.0625} = 2.0977.$$
>>The exercise level is $S^* = (2.0977/1.0977) \times 30 = \$57.33$, so $S^* - K = \$27.33$. At $S = 36$ the warrant is worth
>>$$C(36) = 27.33 \times \Big(\frac{36}{57.33}\Big)^{2.0977} = 27.33 \times 0.3768 = \$10.30.$$
>>Exercise now pays only $36 - 30 = \$6$, so the investor waits. The delta is $\alpha_+C/S = 2.0977 \times 10.30/36 = 0.600$. A $\$1$ rise in the stock adds about $\$0.60$ to the warrant.
>>
>>At $S^*$ the delta is $\alpha_+(S^* - K)/S^* = 2.0977 \times 27.33/57.33 = 1.000$. This equals the slope of the payoff $S - K$, as smooth pasting requires. If the stock later rises to $\$60$, it is above $S^*$, and the investor exercises for $\$30$.

>[!example] (Medium) Value, Delta and Vega of a Perpetual Put
>A stock trades at $\$45$ and pays a continuous dividend yield of $2\%$. Its volatility is $25\%$, and the risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A dealer writes a perpetual American put on it with strike $\$40$.
>
>>[!example]- Find $\alpha_-$, the exercise level, and the put's value, delta and vega at $S = \$45$. Check the vega by repricing at $\sigma = 24\%$ and $26\%$, and decide whether the holder should exercise if the stock falls to $\$25$.
>>Here $b = 0.04 - 0.02 - 0.03125 = -0.01125$ and $\sqrt{b^2 + 2r\sigma^2} = \sqrt{0.000127 + 0.005} = 0.07160$. The negative root is
>>$$\alpha_- = \frac{0.01125 - 0.07160}{0.0625} = -0.9656.$$
>>The exercise level is $S^* = (0.9656/1.9656) \times 40 = \$19.65$, so $K - S^* = \$20.35$. At $S = 45$ the put is worth
>>$$P(45) = 20.35 \times \Big(\frac{45}{19.65}\Big)^{-0.9656} = 20.35 \times 0.4493 = \$9.14.$$
>>Its delta is $\alpha_-P/S = -0.9656 \times 9.143/45 = -0.196$. The dealer who wrote the put hedges by selling about $0.196$ shares per put.
>>
>>For the vega, $\alpha_-(\alpha_- - 1) = 1.8980$, and the bracket $\sigma^2(\alpha_- - \frac{1}{2}) + r - q$ equals $-0.07160$. So
>>$$\frac{d\alpha_-}{d\sigma} = -\frac{0.25 \times 1.8980}{-0.07160} = 6.627, \qquad \mathcal{V} = 9.143 \times \ln\Big(\frac{45}{19.65}\Big) \times 6.627 = 9.143 \times 0.8286 \times 6.627 = 50.21.$$
>>A one-point rise in volatility adds about $\$0.50$. Repricing gives $P = \$9.6426$ at $26\%$ and $\$8.6387$ at $24\%$. The central difference is $(9.6426 - 8.6387)/0.02 = 50.20$, which matches. At $26\%$ the exercise level moves to $\$18.98$. A holder who keeps the old level of $\$19.65$ has a put worth $\$9.6322$, only $\$0.0104$ less.
>>
>>At $S = 25$, exercise pays $\$15$. Waiting is worth $20.35 \times (25/19.65)^{-0.9656} = \$16.13$, so the holder waits. Exercise is optimal only once the stock falls to $\$19.65$.

>[!example] (Hard) Setting the Coupon on a Convertible Preferred Share
>A company plans to issue perpetual convertible preferred shares with notional $N = \$50$. Each share pays a coupon at rate $Q$ on $N$, as a continuous dividend. It converts into $R_C = 2$ common shares at any time. The common stock trades at $\$20$, pays a continuous dividend yield of $3\%$, and has volatility $35\%$. The risk-free rate is $6\%$ per year with continuous compounding. Ignore credit risk and the other features of real issues.
>
>>[!example]- Value the share with a coupon rate of $Q = 4\%$. Find the coupon rate at which the share is worth $\$50$ at issue, and its delta at that rate. Explain why that rate is below $r$.
>>Here $b = 0.06 - 0.03 - 0.06125 = -0.03125$ and $\sqrt{b^2 + 2r\sigma^2} = \sqrt{0.000977 + 0.0147} = 0.125206$. The positive root is
>>$$\alpha_+ = \frac{0.03125 + 0.125206}{0.1225} = 1.2772.$$
>>With $Q = 4\%$ the bond value is $QN/r = 2/0.06 = \$33.33$. The holder converts when $S$ rises to $S^* = (33.33/2) \times (1.2772/0.2772) = \$76.79$. The value formula in the (Convertible Preferred Stock) property gives, at $S = 20$,
>>$$f(20) = 33.33 + \frac{33.33}{0.2772}\Big(\frac{0.2772}{1.2772} \times \frac{2 \times 20}{33.33}\Big)^{1.2772} = 33.33 + 120.25 \times 0.26044^{1.2772} = 33.33 + 120.25 \times 0.17937 = \$54.90.$$
>>The first term is the bond value, and the second, $\$21.57$, is the right to convert. The share is worth more than $\$50$, so the issuer can offer a lower coupon.
>>
>>A higher $Q$ raises the value, so a search over $Q$ finds the coupon that gives $\$50$. The rate $Q = 3\%$ gives $\$48.36$, and $Q = 3.5\%$ gives $\$51.55$. Halving the interval repeatedly gives $Q = 3.26\%$. At that rate the bond value is $QN/r = \$27.17$, and the right to convert is worth $50 - 27.17 = \$22.83$. The holder converts at $S^* = \$62.60$, more than three times today's price. The delta is $\alpha_+(f - QN/r)/S = 1.2772 \times 22.83/20 = 1.458$. A dealer who holds one preferred share hedges by selling about $1.46$ common shares.
>>
>>A perpetual bond with no conversion right is worth $QN/r$. It sells at $\$50$ only if $Q = r = 6\%$. The convertible pays $(0.06 - 0.0326) \times 50 = \$1.37$ less each year. As a perpetuity discounted at $6\%$, this saving is worth $1.37/0.06 = \$22.83$. That equals the value of the right to convert. Investors accept the lower coupon because they receive the conversion right in exchange.
