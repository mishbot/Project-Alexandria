>[!note] Bubbles
>A **bubble** is an episode in which an asset's price becomes significantly overvalued relative to a reasonable estimate of its fundamental value, sustains the overvaluation for a meaningful period, and typically ends in a sharp collapse. Empirically, bubble episodes share four markers: high trading volume, extrapolative survey expectations, sophisticated investors riding rather than correcting the bubble, and good fundamental news near the start of the run-up.
>
>**Two theoretical traditions** account for bubbles:
>- *Rational bubbles* exploit the multiplicity of solutions to the asset-pricing Euler equation. A bubble component $B_t$ satisfying $\mathbb{E}_t[B_{t+1}] = (1+r)B_t$ can be added to the fundamental price without violating any rationality condition. But rational bubbles cannot start, cannot be negative, cannot exist on finite-horizon assets, and cannot survive in dynamically efficient economies — so they fail to match the empirical patterns. See [[8.7.1. Rational Bubbles]].
>- *Behavioral bubbles* combine two ingredients: belief disagreement (so prices are not pinned down by a single rational consensus) and limits to arbitrage (so the disagreement cannot be neutralized by smart money). The leading limit to arbitrage in this literature is the short-sale constraint. Three nested models build on this template:
>    1. [[8.7.2. Heterogeneous Beliefs and Overvaluation|Miller (1977)]] — static. Heterogeneity plus short-sale constraints push the price above fundamentals to the optimist's valuation.
>    2. [[8.7.3. Speculative Bubbles|Harrison-Kreps (1978)]] — dynamic extension of Miller. Rotating optimism creates a resale option, and the price exceeds *every* current investor's buy-and-hold value.
>    3. [[8.7.4. Overconfidence and Trading Volume|Scheinkman-Xiong (2003)]] — cognitive microfoundation. Overconfidence about one's own private signal generates the rotating disagreement Harrison-Kreps assumes, and resolves the No-Trade Theorem (Milgrom-Stokey, 1982) that would otherwise rule out the trading volume bubbles produce.
>
>**Decision rule.** Reach for [[8.7.1. Rational Bubbles|Rational Bubbles]] only as a benchmark — the framework matters historically but does not match the data. For *overpricing without volume*, use [[8.7.2. Heterogeneous Beliefs and Overvaluation|Miller]]. For *overpricing with high turnover*, use [[8.7.3. Speculative Bubbles|Harrison-Kreps]]. For a *cognitive microfoundation* explaining where the disagreement and volume come from, use [[8.7.4. Overconfidence and Trading Volume|Scheinkman-Xiong overconfidence]]. The three behavioral models nest each other: each one adds one ingredient and explains one more empirical fact.

# Intuition

The rational story explains overvaluation only as a self-financing mathematical curiosity that the data do not support. The behavioral story replaces it with a single recipe — disagreement plus short-sale constraints — and then adds layers. Static disagreement gives overvaluation. Dynamic rotating disagreement gives overvaluation *and* volume *and* a resale option. Overconfidence gives all three plus a cognitive reason why the disagreement keeps arising. Each step in the sequence resolves one criticism of the previous step.

Two ingredients stay constant across all three behavioral models. First, *short-sale constraints* are not optional — without them, pessimists short the asset until the price falls back to consensus, killing the overvaluation in every model. Bubble episodes empirically concentrate in asset classes where shorting is hardest: IPO-locked tech stocks, hard-to-borrow small caps, illiquid real estate, cryptocurrencies before futures markets. Second, the *cross-section of disagreement* is the testable handle. Diether-Malloy-Scherbina (2003) verify that stocks investors disagree most about (measured by analyst-forecast dispersion) underperform subsequently — exactly Miller's prediction.
