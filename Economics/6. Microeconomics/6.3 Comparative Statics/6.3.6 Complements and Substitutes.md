>[!note] Complements and Substitutes
>Two goods $i \neq j$ are classified by the sign of a **cross-price effect** at given prices $\mathbf{p}$ and income $M$. A cross-price effect is the response of the demand for one good to the price of the other. The gross classification uses [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|Marshallian demand]] $x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)$. Goods $i$ and $j$ are **gross substitutes** if $\partial x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)/\partial p_j > 0$ and **gross complements** if $\partial x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)/\partial p_j < 0$. The net classification uses [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization|Hicksian demand]] $h_i(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$ at the utility $\bar u = v(\mathbf{p}, M)$ the consumer reaches. Goods $i$ and $j$ are **net substitutes** if
>$$\frac{\partial h_i(\mathbf{p}, v(\mathbf{p}, M))}{\partial p_j} = \frac{\partial h_j(\mathbf{p}, v(\mathbf{p}, M))}{\partial p_i} > 0,$$
>and **net complements** if these two equal derivatives are negative. "Net" means net of the income effect. The two versions are linked by the cross-price [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]],
>$$\frac{\partial x_i^*}{\partial p_j} = \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_j} - x_j^*\,\frac{\partial x_i^*}{\partial M},$$
>so the gross effect is the net effect plus an income effect. The income effect is negative when good $i$ is normal.
>
>The net cross effects are always equal, so the net label describes the pair of goods whichever price moves. The gross cross effects $\partial x_i^*/\partial p_j$ and $\partial x_j^*/\partial p_i$ can differ in size and even in sign. With two goods, the goods are never net complements. The net cross effect is positive when the indifference curve is smooth and zero at a kink, as for perfect complements. With three or more goods, net complements are possible. For [[6.4a Cobb-Douglas]] utility $x_1^\alpha x_2^{1-\alpha}$, demand $x_1^* = \alpha M/p_1$ does not depend on $p_2$, so the goods are neither gross substitutes nor gross complements. They are net substitutes, because $h_1 = \bar u\left(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{1-\alpha}$ rises with $p_2$.

# Intuition

Coffee and tea serve the same purpose, so a rise in the price of tea sends drinkers to coffee. Coffee and sugar are used together, so a rise in the price of coffee lowers the demand for sugar. We make these ideas precise with the sign of a cross-price effect. There are two versions because a price change does two things at once, as the [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]] shows. It changes the relative price of the goods, and it changes the consumer's purchasing power. The gross effect is what market data show, with both changes mixed together. The net effect is measured at fixed utility, so it contains only the relative-price change.

With two goods the net effect has a fixed sign. The diagram holds the consumer on the indifference curve $\bar u = 4$ of $u = x_1^{1/2}x_2^{1/2}$, with $p_1 = 1$. When $p_2$ rises from $1$ to $2.25$, the cheapest budget line that still reaches $\bar u$ becomes flatter. The tangency slides along the curve from $A = (4, 4)$ to $B = (6, 2.67)$. The consumer buys less of the dearer good 2. To stay on the same indifference curve, the consumer must then buy more of good 1. So $h_1$ rises with $p_2$, and the goods are net substitutes.

# Properties

#### *(Two Goods Are Never Net Complements)* With two goods, a compensated rise in $p_j$ never lowers $h_i$.

The argument needs strictly monotone preferences, so that the indifference curve slopes down. Hicksian demand for good $j$ falls when $p_j$ rises ([[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]]). With two goods, the only way to stay on the same indifference curve with less of good $j$ is to have more of good $i$. At a kink, as with [[6.4c Perfect Complements|perfect complements]], neither Hicksian demand moves and the net cross effect is zero. With three or more goods, a third good can replace the lost good $j$. The consumer can then buy less of good $i$ as well. When the price of coffee rises, a compensated consumer may switch to tea and buy less sugar. Coffee and sugar are then net complements.

#### *(Gross Labels Include Income Effects)* The income effect can give the gross effect the opposite sign from the net effect.

The gross effect adds $-x_j^*\,\partial x_i^*/\partial M$ to the net effect. If good $i$ is normal, this term is negative. It is large when good $j$ takes a big share of the budget, because then a rise in $p_j$ makes the consumer much poorer. A pair of net substitutes can therefore be gross complements. For example, a rise in rent can lower the number of restaurant meals a household buys, even if housing and restaurant meals are net substitutes. The gross label depends on income effects, so it can also depend on which price moves.

#### *(Why Net Effects Are Symmetric)* The two net cross effects are the same second derivative of the expenditure function.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture built the equality $\partial h_i/\partial p_j = \partial h_j/\partial p_i$ into the definition without proving it. The argument below uses Shephard's lemma and the symmetry of second derivatives.

The [[7.3 Envelope Theorem|envelope theorem]] gives $\partial e/\partial p_i = h_i$, as shown in [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]]. Differentiating once more,
$$\frac{\partial h_i}{\partial p_j} = \frac{\partial^2 e}{\partial p_j\,\partial p_i} = \frac{\partial^2 e}{\partial p_i\,\partial p_j} = \frac{\partial h_j}{\partial p_i}.$$
The middle step uses the symmetry of second partial derivatives, which holds when $e$ is twice continuously differentiable. In general, Marshallian demand is not the price derivative of a single function, so the gross effects have no such property.

#### *(Symmetry in the Standard Families)* When demand is proportional to income, the gross cross effects are also equal.

If $x_i^* = M\,g_i(\mathbf{p})$ for some function $g_i$ of prices alone, then $\partial x_i^*/\partial M = x_i^*/M$. The income term in the Slutsky equation becomes $-x_i^*x_j^*/M$, which is the same for the pair $(i, j)$ and the pair $(j, i)$. The net terms are equal, so the gross terms are equal too. This covers [[6.4a Cobb-Douglas]], [[6.4d CES Utility|CES]], [[6.4c Perfect Complements|perfect complements]], and [[6.4b Perfect Substitutes|perfect substitutes]]. An example of unequal gross effects needs demand that is not proportional to income, as in the Hard example below.

#### *(CES and the Sign of ρ)* For CES utility, $0 < \rho < 1$ gives gross substitutes and $\rho < 0$ gives gross complements.

In the CES demand derived in [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]], $p_2$ enters $x_1^*$ only through the term $(p_2/p_1)^{\rho/(\rho-1)}$ in the denominator. For $0 < \rho < 1$ the exponent $\rho/(\rho-1)$ is negative. A higher $p_2$ then lowers the denominator and raises $x_1^*$. For $\rho < 0$ the exponent is positive, and $x_1^*$ falls. At $\rho = 0$, the Cobb-Douglas case, the cross effect is zero. For every $\rho < 1$ the two goods remain net substitutes, because there are only two goods.

>[!warning] Lecture Wording on the Sign of ρ
>The lecture wrote "$\rho > 0$: goods are (net) substitutes; $\rho < 0$: goods are (net) complements." With two goods, no pair of goods can be net complements, so the sign of $\rho$ cannot separate net substitutes from net complements. The sign rule is correct for gross substitutes and gross complements, as derived above.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Cars and Gasoline
>When the price of gasoline rises, households buy fewer cars.
>
>>[!example]- What does this say about cars and gasoline? Does it show that they are net complements?
>>The data show $\partial x_{\text{car}}^*/\partial p_{\text{gas}} < 0$, so cars are gross complements to gasoline in this direction. The data do not show the net sign. The gross effect includes an income effect. A gasoline price rise makes households poorer, and cars are a normal good, so part of the fall in car purchases comes from lower real income.
>>
>>In a world with only cars and gasoline, the two goods could not be net complements, however the data looked. In the real world there are other goods, such as bus passes and bicycles. A household compensated for the gasoline price rise may switch to the bus and buy both less gasoline and fewer cars. Then cars and gasoline are net complements. Deciding this requires holding utility fixed, which market data alone do not do.

>[!example] (Medium) Gym Visits and Restaurant Meals
>A student has utility $u = x_1^{0.4}x_2^{0.6}$ over gym visits $x_1$ at $p_1 = 2$ and restaurant meals $x_2$ at $p_2 = 5$. Monthly income is $M = 100$. The Hicksian demands are $h_1 = \bar u\left(\frac{0.4}{0.6}\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{0.6}$ and $h_2 = \bar u\left(\frac{0.6}{0.4}\frac{p_1}{p_2}\right)^{0.4}$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute both gross and both net cross-price effects at the student's optimum and classify the pair.
>>Marshallian demand is $x_1^* = 0.4M/p_1 = 20$ and $x_2^* = 0.6M/p_2 = 12$. Neither formula contains the other good's price, so both gross cross effects are $0$. The goods are neither gross substitutes nor gross complements.
>>
>>The Hicksian demand $h_1$ is proportional to $p_2^{0.6}$, so $\partial h_1/\partial p_2 = 0.6\,h_1/p_2$. At the optimum $h_1 = x_1^* = 20$, so $\partial h_1/\partial p_2 = 0.6 \cdot 20/5 = 2.4$. Likewise $h_2$ is proportional to $p_1^{0.4}$, so $\partial h_2/\partial p_1 = 0.4\,h_2/p_1 = 0.4 \cdot 12/2 = 2.4$. The two net effects are equal and positive, so the goods are net substitutes.
>>
>>The Slutsky equation reconciles the two answers. The income term is $-x_2^*\,\partial x_1^*/\partial M = -12 \cdot 0.4/2 = -2.4$. Adding it to the net effect $2.4$ gives the gross effect $0$.

>[!example] (Hard) Coffee and Sandwiches
>A worker has utility $u = 4\ln(1 + x_1) + x_2$ over cups of coffee $x_1$ and sandwiches $x_2$ per week. Coffee costs $p_1 = 2$, a sandwich costs $p_2 = 3$, and the weekly budget is $M = 40$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the demands, both gross cross effects, and both net cross effects. Classify the pair and explain why the gross effects differ.
>>The tangency condition is $u'_{x_1}/u'_{x_2} = 4/(1 + x_1) = p_1/p_2$, so $x_1^* = 4p_2/p_1 - 1$. The budget gives $x_2^* = (M - p_1x_1^*)/p_2 = (M + p_1 - 4p_2)/p_2$. At the given numbers $x_1^* = 5$ and $x_2^* = 10$, both positive, so the optimum is interior.
>>
>>The gross cross effects are $\partial x_1^*/\partial p_2 = 4/p_1 = 2$ and $\partial x_2^*/\partial p_1 = 1/p_2 = 1/3$. Both are positive, so the goods are gross substitutes in both directions. The sizes differ. A one-dollar rise in the sandwich price adds $2$ cups of coffee a week. A one-dollar rise in the coffee price adds only a third of a sandwich.
>>
>>For the net effects, use the Slutsky equation with the income derivatives $\partial x_1^*/\partial M = 0$ and $\partial x_2^*/\partial M = 1/p_2 = 1/3$:
>>$$\frac{\partial h_1}{\partial p_2} = 2 + x_2^* \cdot 0 = 2, \qquad \frac{\partial h_2}{\partial p_1} = \frac{1}{3} + x_1^* \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{5}{3} = 2.$$
>>The net effects are equal, as they must be, and positive. The goods are net substitutes.
>>
>>The gross effects differ only because of the income effects. Coffee demand does not respond to income, since every extra dollar goes to sandwiches. So a sandwich price rise affects coffee only through substitution. A coffee price rise makes the worker poorer by $x_1^* = 5$ dollars per dollar of price increase. That loss comes entirely out of sandwiches and cancels most of the substitution toward them.
