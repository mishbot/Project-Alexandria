>[!note] Absolute Temperature
>**Absolute zero** is the temperature at which the pressure of a low-density gas held at constant volume would fall to zero. The volume of a low-density gas held at constant pressure extrapolates to zero at the same temperature. Every gas gives the same value,
>$$T_{\text{abs zero}} = -273.15\ ^\circ\text{C}.$$
>No lower temperature exists, because a volume or a pressure cannot be negative. The molecular version of the same argument is shorter. Temperature measures the average kinetic energy per molecule, kinetic energy cannot be negative, so temperature has a floor, and the floor is where the motion would stop. The note [[2.3.1 Molecular Interpretation of Temperature]] makes this precise.
>
>**Absolute temperature** is temperature measured from absolute zero. Its SI unit is the **kelvin**, written $\text{K}$ with no degree sign. One kelvin is the same size as one Celsius degree, so the two scales differ only by a shift,
>$$T = T_C + 273.15,$$
>where $T$ is in kelvins and $T_C$ is the Celsius reading. Water freezes at $273.15\ \text{K}$ and boils at $373.15\ \text{K}$ at one atmosphere. Room temperature is about $293\ \text{K}$. Every formula in these notes that contains a bare $T$, rather than a difference $\Delta T$, needs $T$ in kelvins. That includes the [[2.2.3 Ideal Gas Law|ideal gas law]] $PV = NkT$, the [[2.2.2 Gas Laws|gas laws]] in ratio form, $\tfrac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2} = \tfrac{3}{2} kT$, and every ratio of temperatures, as the [[2.1.2 Temperature Scales|Kelvin-for-ratios rule]] states. A **constant-volume gas thermometer** reads absolute temperature directly, since at fixed volume and low density $T_2 / T_1 = P_2 / P_1$, and one reference temperature then fixes the scale.

# Intuition

The shortest route to absolute zero runs through the [[2.1.1 Atomic Theory of Matter|atomic theory]]. In a low-density gas the molecules rarely meet, so the energy of a molecule is its kinetic energy. Temperature measures the average of this energy per molecule. Kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2} m v^2$ is never negative, so its average is never negative either. Temperature therefore has a floor, the state in which every molecule would stand still. An absolute scale puts its zero at this floor. Only on such a scale is temperature proportional to the energy per molecule. That is why a bare $T$ must be in kelvins. The sketch shows the same gas at three temperatures. The arrows are the molecular velocities, and their length grows as $\sqrt{T}$.

The same floor shows up in experiments with real gases. The volume of a gas at fixed pressure grows in a straight line with Celsius temperature. Different gases and different amounts of gas give lines with different slopes. Extended backward, every line reaches zero volume at the same temperature, $-273.15\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The diagram shows three such lines. The solid parts are measured and the dashed parts are extrapolations, because every real gas turns into a liquid before it gets that cold. Zero volume is a floor that no substance can pass, so the common intercept is a floor for temperature itself. The zero of the Celsius scale is a human choice, the freezing point of water. The zero of the absolute scale belongs to nature.

# Properties

#### *(Same Intercept for Every Gas)* The extrapolated zero does not depend on the gas, the amount, or the pressure.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover the gas extrapolation. This property follows Giancoli section 17.6.

Changing the gas or the amount changes the slope of the line and nothing else. Lowering the density at which the measurement is made moves the intercept toward $-273.15\ ^\circ\text{C}$, and the agreement between gases becomes exact in the limit of zero density, where the molecules never interact. This is what allows a gas to serve as a thermometer that does not favor one substance.

#### *(Unreachable Floor)* Absolute zero has never been reached, and the extrapolation is never observed directly.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture only remarked in passing that absolute zero is never reached. The helium and laboratory figures below are standard textbook material.

Every gas liquefies before its volume can shrink to zero. Helium stays a gas the longest and becomes a liquid at $4.2\ \text{K}$ at atmospheric pressure. Laboratory cooling has brought small samples to below one billionth of a kelvin, and the floor stays out of reach, for reasons that extend the [[2.7.2 Second Law of Thermodynamics|second law]]. Nothing in this note requires reaching it. The scale needs only the location of the zero, and the extrapolation supplies that.

# Examples

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not locate absolute zero by extrapolation. This example follows Giancoli section 17.6.

>[!example] (Easy) Locating Absolute Zero
>A sample of gas at fixed pressure has a volume of $1.000\ \text{L}$ at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $1.366\ \text{L}$ at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- Use these two measurements to estimate the temperature at which the volume would reach zero.
>>The volume grows by $0.366\ \text{L}$ over $100$ Celsius degrees, so the slope of the line is
>>$$\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta T_C} = \frac{0.366\ \text{L}}{100\ ^\circ\text{C}} = 0.00366\ \text{L}/^\circ\text{C}.$$
>>Starting from $1.000\ \text{L}$ at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$, the volume reaches zero after a drop of
>>$$\frac{1.000\ \text{L}}{0.00366\ \text{L}/^\circ\text{C}} = 273\ ^\circ\text{C},$$
>>so the line hits $V = 0$ at $-273\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Two measurements of one gas already give absolute zero to three figures.

>[!example] (Medium) Doubling the Pressure
>A sealed rigid container holds gas at $1.00\ \text{atm}$ and $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>```tikz

>\begin{document}

>\node[right] at (2.8,0.9) {rigid walls: $V$ fixed};
>\node[right] at (2.8,-0.4) {heat from below};
>\end{tikzpicture}
>\end{document}
>```
>
>>[!example]- To what temperature must the gas be heated so that its pressure reaches $2.00\ \text{atm}$?
>>At fixed volume the pressure is proportional to the absolute temperature, so the absolute temperature must double,
>>$$T_2 = 2\,T_1 = 2 \times 293.15\ \text{K} = 586.3\ \text{K}.$$
>>In Celsius this is $586.3 - 273.15 = 313\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Doubling the Celsius reading to $40\ ^\circ\text{C}$ would raise the pressure by only $7$ percent.
