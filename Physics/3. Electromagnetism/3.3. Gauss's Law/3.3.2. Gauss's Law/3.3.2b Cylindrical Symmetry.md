>[!note] Cylindrical Symmetry
>A charge distribution has **cylindrical symmetry** when any rotation about an axis or shift along it leaves it unchanged. Common cases include:
>- an infinite straight line with constant linear charge density $\lambda$;
>- an infinite thin cylindrical tube with charge spread evenly over it;
>- an infinite cylinder whose volume density depends only on the distance $r$ from its axis.
>
>The field then points straight away from the axis or straight toward it, and its size depends only on $r$. In symbols, $\vec E = E(r)\,\hat r$, where $\hat r$ points away from the axis and perpendicular to it. The Gaussian surface is a closed cylinder on the same axis, called a **can**. It has radius $r$, length $h$, and three parts:
>- On the top and bottom caps, $d\vec A$ points along the axis, perpendicular to $\vec E$. Their flux is zero.
>- On the curved side, called the **label**, $d\vec A$ points along $\hat r$, parallel to $\vec E$. The size $E(r)$ is constant there, so the label's flux is $E(r)\,2\pi r h$.
>
>[[3.3.2. Gauss's Law|Gauss's law]] then gives
>$$E(r)\,2\pi r h = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0},$$
>where $Q_{\text{enc}}$ is the charge inside the can. For the infinite line, $Q_{\text{enc}} = \lambda h$, and the field is
>$$E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}.$$
>This matches the result $2k\lambda/r$ of the [[3.2.4a Infinite Line of Charge|slice-and-dice integral]]. A thin tube carrying $\lambda$ per length has $E = 0$ inside and the same $\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$ outside. For a cylinder with density $\rho(r)$, the enclosed charge is $Q_{\text{enc}} = h\int_0^r \rho(r')\,2\pi r'\,dr'$. A uniform cylinder of radius $R$ and density $\rho$ has the field
>$$E = \frac{\rho r}{2\varepsilon_0} \quad (r \le R), \qquad E = \frac{\rho R^2}{2\varepsilon_0 r} \quad (r \ge R).$$
>Outside any such cylinder, the field equals that of a line with the same charge per length. A density that changes along the axis, such as $\lambda \propto y$, breaks the symmetry. Gauss's law can then no longer find the field.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture showed only that Gauss's law applies to a cylinder whose density depends on $r$. The fields of charged tubes and cylinders follow Giancoli chapter 22.

# Intuition

An infinite line has as much charge above any point as below it. The components along the line cancel at every point. Moving a point along the line changes nothing, so the field cannot depend on height. Moving it around the line changes nothing either, so the field cannot depend on the angle. What remains is a field pointing straight out, with a size set by the distance $r$.

Around a line, the field lines spread over cylinders. The label's area grows as $r$, so the field falls as $1/r$. Around a point charge the lines spread over spheres, whose area grows as $r^2$. That is why a line's field falls off more slowly than a point charge's.

A Gaussian can surrounds a piece of an infinite line of charge. The field runs along the caps, so only the label carries flux. As the can widens, $E$ falls as $1/r$ while the label's area grows as $r$.

# Properties

#### *(The Length Cancels)* The can's length $h$ drops out of every answer, because every height along the axis is the same.

Both sides of Gauss's law grow in proportion to $h$. The label's area is $2\pi r h$, and the enclosed charge of a line is $\lambda h$. Their ratio, and with it the field, cannot depend on $h$. This is the shift symmetry along the axis, written in algebra. An answer that still contains $h$ contains an error.

#### *(Which Step Fails)* Without the shift symmetry, the caps carry flux and the label's field varies, so $E$ stays inside the integral.

Take a line with $\lambda \propto y$, where $y$ runs along the line. The charge above a point no longer matches the charge below it. The field then tilts and gains a component along the line. That component crosses the caps, so their flux is unknown. The field on the label also changes with height. Gauss's law still holds for the can. Neither part of the flux can be written as $E$ times an area, though. The [[3.2.4. Electric Field of a Continuous Charge Distribution|slice-and-dice method]] is the way to find this field.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Charged Wire by Gauss's Law
>A long straight wire carries $\lambda = 4.5\ \text{nC/m}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field $15\ \text{cm}$ from the wire.
>>A can of radius $0.15\ \text{m}$ around the wire gives
>>$$E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = \frac{4.5 \times 10^{-9}}{2\pi(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(0.15)} = 5.4 \times 10^{2}\ \text{N/C},$$
>>pointing straight away from the wire. The slice-and-dice form $2k\lambda/r$ gives the same value.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not compute the fields of charged tubes or cylinders. The next two examples follow Giancoli chapter 22.

>[!example] (Medium) A Coaxial Pair
>A long thin wire carries $\lambda_1 = +3.0\ \text{nC/m}$. A thin plastic tube of radius $1.5\ \text{cm}$ surrounds it on the same axis. The tube carries $\lambda_2 = -5.0\ \text{nC/m}$, spread evenly around it.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field $0.50\ \text{cm}$ and $3.0\ \text{cm}$ from the axis.
>>A can of radius $0.50\ \text{cm}$ encloses only the wire, so
>>$$E = \frac{\lambda_1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^{-9}}{2\pi(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(0.0050)} = 1.08 \times 10^{4}\ \text{N/C},$$
>>pointing away from the axis. A can of radius $3.0\ \text{cm}$ encloses $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = -2.0\ \text{nC}$ per meter of length, so
>>$$E = \frac{-2.0 \times 10^{-9}}{2\pi(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(0.030)} = -1.20 \times 10^{3}\ \text{N/C}.$$
>>The minus sign means the field points toward the axis. Between the wire and the tube, the tube's charge makes no field.

>[!example] (Hard) A Cylinder Densest at Its Axis
>An infinite cylinder of radius $R = 3.0\ \text{cm}$ carries $\lambda = 8.0\ \text{nC}$ per meter of length. Its density falls linearly from the axis to zero at the surface, $\rho = \rho_0(1 - r/R)$.
>
>>[!example]- Find $E$ inside and outside, and the radius where the field is largest. Evaluate $E$ at $1.0\ \text{cm}$, at that radius, and at $6.0\ \text{cm}$.
>>Take a can of radius $r < R$ and length $h$. A thin layer of radius $r'$ has volume $2\pi r' h\,dr'$, so the enclosed charge is
>>$$Q_{\text{enc}} = h\int_0^r \rho_0\left(1 - \frac{r'}{R}\right)2\pi r'\,dr' = \pi\rho_0 h\,r^2\left(1 - \frac{2r}{3R}\right).$$
>>Setting $E\,2\pi r h = Q_{\text{enc}}/\varepsilon_0$ gives the field inside:
>>$$E = \frac{\rho_0 r}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(1 - \frac{2r}{3R}\right) \quad (r \le R).$$
>>At $r = R$ the enclosed charge per length is the total, $\lambda = \pi\rho_0 R^2/3$. Outside, $E = \lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$, and the two forms agree at $r = R$.
>>
>>Let $E_R = \lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 R)$ be the field at the surface. Using $\rho_0 = 3\lambda/(\pi R^2)$, the inside field becomes
>>$$E = 3E_R\,\frac{r}{R}\left(1 - \frac{2r}{3R}\right).$$
>>Its derivative with respect to $r$ is zero at $r = 3R/4$, where $E = \tfrac98 E_R$. The field is largest inside the cylinder, $12.5\%$ above its surface value. A uniform cylinder has its largest field at the surface.
>>
>>With the numbers, $E_R = 8.0 \times 10^{-9}/[2\pi(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(0.030)] = 4.80 \times 10^{3}\ \text{N/C}$. At $1.0\ \text{cm}$, $r/R = 1/3$, so $E = \tfrac79 E_R = 3.73 \times 10^{3}\ \text{N/C}$. At $r = 3R/4 = 2.25\ \text{cm}$, $E = 5.40 \times 10^{3}\ \text{N/C}$. At $6.0\ \text{cm}$, $E = E_R/2 = 2.40 \times 10^{3}\ \text{N/C}$. All three fields point away from the axis.
