>[!note] Prescriptive Behavioral Finance
>**Prescriptive behavioral finance** asks the policy-design question that the descriptive part of the field leaves on the table: given that households make systematic financial mistakes, *what can we do about it*? The label is due to Thaler and Sunstein ($2008$) and contrasts with the *descriptive* program of cataloging biases. The chapter is built around two empirical premises and four policy responses.
>
>**Premise 1 — financial literacy is low.** Lusardi and Mitchell ($2006$) administer the *Big Three* literacy questions (compound interest, inflation, diversification) to a U.S. sample. Only $30\%$ of respondents answer all three correctly. Literacy correlates with subsequent retirement-saving outcomes, so the gap is consequential.
>
>**Premise 2 — markets do not solve the problem.** Audits of financial advisors show that they typically push high-fee active products on clients (Mullainathan-Noth-Schoar $2012$) and inappropriate insurance products (Anagol-Cole-Santosh $2013$). Advisors face conflicts of interest that are not fully resolved by competition.
>
>**Four policy responses, ordered from least to most successful:**
>- **[[8.12.1. Financial Literacy and the Big Three|Financial education]]** — teaching financial concepts directly. Empirical evidence (Skimmyhorn $2012$ on the U.S. military; Cole-Shastry $2012$ on state high-school mandates) finds modest-to-no effect on subsequent financial outcomes.
>- **[[8.12.2. Financial Advice and Audit Studies|Reform of financial-advisor incentives]]** — restricting commissions, mandating fiduciary duty. Mixed empirical record; most evidence on advisor quality comes from audits, with regulatory reform itself harder to test.
>- **[[8.12.3. Nudges and Default Options|Nudges]]** — restructuring the choice architecture so that the default option matches the welfare-improving choice. The most empirically successful policy lever, with retirement-plan auto-enrollment (Madrian-Shea $2001$; Choi et al. $2004$) raising participation rates from $\sim 50\%$ to $\sim 90\%$.
>- **[[8.12.4. Explicit De-biasing|Explicit de-biasing]]** — training individuals to recognize and counter their own biases (e.g., listing three reasons their judgment may be wrong). Some evidence of effectiveness in laboratory settings; field evidence is sparse.
>
>**The libertarian-paternalism framing.** Thaler and Sunstein advocate "libertarian paternalism": choice architectures that nudge people toward better outcomes without restricting their freedom to choose otherwise. The default-option nudge embodies this ideal — every participant retains the right to opt out — and its empirical success has made it the central policy tool of the field. The unifying observation across the four responses is that *changing the choice environment* (premise 4) appears to outperform *changing the chooser* (premises 1–3).

# Intuition

The descriptive behavioral-finance literature documents many well-identified household-portfolio mistakes: people fail to enroll in retirement plans, hold too few stocks, sell winners and hold losers, fail to annuitize, and so on. The natural policy question is whether anything can be done about these mistakes. Three approaches present themselves intuitively. The first is to make people more sophisticated through education. The second is to fix the intermediaries — financial advisors and product manufacturers — so that they direct clients toward better choices. The third is to leave the chooser unchanged and instead redesign the choice environment so the path of least resistance is also the welfare-improving path.

The central empirical finding of the literature is that the third approach works much better than the first two. A modest cognitive cost — having to actively opt *out* of a retirement plan rather than actively opt *in* — generates a $40$-percentage-point swing in plan participation. No comparably large effect has been demonstrated for financial-literacy interventions. The reason for the asymmetry is plausibly that the obstacles to good financial decisions are not lack of information so much as lack of attention, present bias, and computational cost. Choice architecture works *with* these features rather than trying to override them.

Where the cognitive obstacles run particularly deep — overconfidence, confirmation bias — explicit de-biasing techniques offer a fourth lever. The evidence on these is more limited but suggestive: making people list reasons their judgment may be wrong, or asking them to publicly explain their reasoning, shifts behavior in the direction of better-calibrated decisions.
