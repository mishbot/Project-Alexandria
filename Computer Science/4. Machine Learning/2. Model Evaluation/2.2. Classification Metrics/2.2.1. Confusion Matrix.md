>[!note] Confusion Matrix
>A **confusion matrix** counts how a classifier's predicted labels match the true labels on a test set. In a binary problem one class is called **positive** (spam, fraud, disease) and the other **negative**. A model that outputs a score $s(\mathbf{x})$ becomes a classifier once we pick a threshold $t$. It predicts positive when $s(\mathbf{x}) \ge t$. Each test point then falls into one of four cells. Rows give the true class and columns give the prediction:
>
>| | Predicted positive | Predicted negative |
>|---|---|---|
>| **Actual positive** | true positive (TP) | false negative (FN) |
>| **Actual negative** | false positive (FP) | true negative (TN) |
>
>A false positive is a false alarm, or type I error. A false negative is a miss, or type II error. The test set has $P = TP + FN$ actual positives, $N = FP + TN$ actual negatives and prevalence $\pi = P/(P + N)$. The standard metrics are ratios of the four counts:
>
>- **Accuracy**: the fraction of all predictions that are correct, $(TP + TN)/(P + N)$.
>- **Precision** (positive predictive value): the fraction of predicted positives that are positive, $TP/(TP + FP)$.
>- **Recall** (sensitivity, true positive rate, TPR): the fraction of actual positives that the model catches, $TP/(TP + FN)$.
>- **Specificity** (true negative rate): the fraction of actual negatives labeled negative, $TN/(TN + FP)$.
>- **False positive rate** (FPR): the fraction of actual negatives flagged as positive, $FP/(FP + TN) = 1 - \text{specificity}$.
>
>The **$F_1$ score** combines precision and recall through their harmonic mean:
>$$F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot \text{precision} \cdot \text{recall}}{\text{precision} + \text{recall}} = \frac{2\,TP}{2\,TP + FP + FN}.$$
>The second form shows that $F_1$ never uses $TN$. The **$F_\beta$ score** treats recall as $\beta$ times as important as precision:
>$$F_\beta = \frac{(1 + \beta^2) \cdot \text{precision} \cdot \text{recall}}{\beta^2 \cdot \text{precision} + \text{recall}}.$$
>Here $\beta > 0$ is chosen by the user. $F_2$ favors recall, $F_{1/2}$ favors precision, and $\beta = 1$ gives $F_1$.
>
>The **accuracy paradox** names a common failure. A model that always predicts the majority class can score a high accuracy and catch no positives. Accuracy is therefore a poor guide when $\pi$ is far from $1/2$.
>
>With $K > 2$ classes the matrix is $K \times K$, and entry $C_{jk}$ counts points of true class $j$ predicted as class $k$. Each class gets its own precision, recall and $F_1$ by treating it as positive and every other class as negative. Two averages summarize the $K$ values:
>
>- **Macro average**: compute the metric for each class, then take the unweighted mean over the $K$ classes.
>- **Micro average**: add the TP, FP and FN counts over all classes, then compute the metric once from the totals.
>
>The confusion matrix describes one threshold. The [[2.2.2. ROC Curve|ROC curve]] and the [[2.2.3. Precision-Recall Curve|precision-recall curve]] sweep the threshold, and [[2.2.4. Log Loss|log loss]] scores the probabilities directly.

# Intuition

The name comes from the off-diagonal cells. They show which classes the model confuses with which. A single number such as accuracy adds the two kinds of error together. In most applications the two errors have very different costs. A spam filter that hides a job offer does more harm than one that lets an advertisement through. A cancer screen that misses a tumor does more harm than one that orders an extra biopsy. The matrix keeps the errors apart so that each can be weighed.

Precision and recall serve two different people. Precision matters to the analyst who reviews the flagged cases. It is the fraction of their alerts that are real. Recall matters to the person responsible for the positives. It is the fraction of all frauds or tumors that the model catches. The rows of the matrix come from the labels and stay fixed. The threshold decides only how each row splits between the two columns.

# Properties

#### *(Accuracy Is a Weighted Average)* Accuracy equals $\pi \cdot \text{recall} + (1 - \pi) \cdot \text{specificity}$, so the larger class controls it.

Let $n$ be the test set size. Recall divides $TP$ by the $\pi n$ positives, so $TP = \pi n \cdot \text{recall}$. In the same way, $TN = (1 - \pi) n \cdot \text{specificity}$. Adding them and dividing by $n$ gives the formula. With 0.5% fraud, accuracy is $0.995 \cdot \text{specificity} + 0.005 \cdot \text{recall}$, so recall barely moves it. A model that always answers "legitimate" has specificity 1 and recall 0, and it scores 99.5%. Any useful fraud model flags some legitimate transactions, so it can easily score below this trivial model. Report the majority-class rate $\max(\pi, 1 - \pi)$ next to accuracy as its chance level.

#### *(Precision Depends on Prevalence)* Recall and specificity are rates within one true class, while precision mixes both classes and changes with $\pi$.

[[1.6 Bayes' Rule|Bayes' rule]] gives precision as the probability of a positive label given a positive prediction:
$$\text{precision} = \frac{\pi \cdot \text{TPR}}{\pi \cdot \text{TPR} + (1 - \pi) \cdot \text{FPR}}.$$
A screening test with sensitivity 0.99 and specificity 0.95 has the same TPR and FPR in every population. In a clinic where 10% of patients are sick, 68.8% of its positive results are correct. In general screening with 1% prevalence, only 16.7% are correct, so five of every six positive results are false alarms. A precision measured at one prevalence therefore does not carry over to a deployment with another. Recall and specificity do carry over, as long as each class keeps its distribution of scores.

#### *(F1 Ignores True Negatives)* $F_1$ stays the same when correctly rejected negatives are added, which suits retrieval and detection.

In a search or detection task the negatives are "everything else". Adding a million correctly ignored web pages leaves $F_1$ unchanged, while accuracy would approach 1. For the same reason, $F_1$ depends on which class is called positive. Swapping the labels swaps the roles of TP and TN. The harmonic mean stays close to the smaller of its two inputs. A model with precision 1 and recall 0.01 has $F_1 = 0.0198$, while the arithmetic mean would give 0.505. $F_1$ weights precision and recall equally. When a miss costs more than a false alarm, $F_2$ states that preference directly.

#### *(Moving the Threshold)* Changing the threshold moves points between the columns of the matrix and never between its rows.

Raising $t$ turns some predicted positives into predicted negatives. Each true positive that crosses becomes a false negative, and each false positive becomes a true negative. The row totals $P$ and $N$ stay fixed, so recall and FPR can only fall as $t$ rises. Precision usually rises, but it falls at any step that removes a true positive and no false positive. The cost-minimizing threshold for calibrated probabilities follows from the misclassification costs, as [[4.1. Bayes Classifier]] derives. The ROC and precision-recall curves record the split at every threshold at once.

#### *(Micro Averaging Equals Accuracy)* In a single-label multi-class problem, micro precision, micro recall and micro $F_1$ all equal accuracy.

Suppose a point of true class $j$ is predicted as class $k$. It counts as a false negative for class $j$ and a false positive for class $k$. Summed over classes, the total FP and the total FN both equal the number of errors. The total TP equals the number of correct predictions. Micro precision is then (correct)/(correct + errors), which is accuracy, and the same holds for recall and $F_1$. Micro averaging therefore weights each class by its size, and a rare class barely affects it. Macro averaging gives every class the same weight, so a badly handled rare class pulls the score down. Report macro $F_1$ when rare classes matter as much as common ones. Libraries differ on its definition. Some average the per-class $F_1$ scores, and others take the $F_1$ of macro precision and macro recall.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Scoring a Spam Filter
>A spam filter screens 1,000 emails, of which 200 are spam. It flags 190 emails, and 170 of them are spam.
>
>>[!example]- Fill in the confusion matrix and compute accuracy, precision, recall, specificity and $F_1$.
>>The filter has $TP = 170$ and $FP = 190 - 170 = 20$. It misses $FN = 200 - 170 = 30$ spam emails, and $TN = 800 - 20 = 780$ legitimate emails pass. Accuracy is $(170 + 780)/1000 = 0.95$. Precision is $170/190 = 0.895$ and recall is $170/200 = 0.85$. Specificity is $780/800 = 0.975$, so $\text{FPR} = 0.025$. The $F_1$ score is $2 \cdot 170/(340 + 20 + 30) = 340/390 = 0.872$. The 20 false positives send one legitimate email in forty to the spam folder. The accuracy check $0.2 \cdot 0.85 + 0.8 \cdot 0.975 = 0.95$ matches the weighted-average property.

>[!example] (Medium) A Fraud Model That Loses on Accuracy
>A bank sees 100,000 card transactions, of which 500 are fraud. Model A labels every transaction legitimate. Model B flags 2,000 transactions, and 400 of them are fraud. Reviewing a flag is cheap, and a missed fraud is expensive.
>
>>[!example]- Compare the two models by accuracy, precision, recall, $F_1$ and $F_2$.
>>Model A has accuracy $99{,}500/100{,}000 = 0.995$ and recall 0. Its precision is undefined because it flags nothing, and its $F_1$ is 0 by convention. Model B has $TP = 400$, $FP = 1{,}600$, $FN = 100$ and $TN = 97{,}900$. Its accuracy is $98{,}300/100{,}000 = 0.983$, lower than Model A's. Its precision is $400/2000 = 0.2$ and its recall is $400/500 = 0.8$. Its $F_1$ is $2 \cdot 0.2 \cdot 0.8/(0.2 + 0.8) = 0.32$, and its $F_2$ is $5 \cdot 0.2 \cdot 0.8/(4 \cdot 0.2 + 0.8) = 0.5$. Model B catches four frauds in five and raises four false flags per real fraud. Accuracy ranks the useless model first. $F_2$ encodes the bank's preference for recall and rates Model B higher than $F_1$ does.

>[!example] (Hard) Macro and Micro Averages for Ticket Routing
>A support system routes tickets into three queues: billing (100 tickets), technical (50) and cancellation (10). Rows give the true queue and columns the predicted queue, in that order:
>$$\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 90 & 8 & 2 \\ 5 & 40 & 5 \\ 4 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}.$$
>
>>[!example]- Compute per-class precision, recall and $F_1$, then the macro and micro $F_1$.
>>The column sums give the number of predictions per queue: 99, 50 and 11. Precision is $90/99 = 0.909$ for billing, $40/50 = 0.8$ for technical and $4/11 = 0.364$ for cancellation. Recall divides by the row sums, giving $90/100 = 0.9$, $40/50 = 0.8$ and $4/10 = 0.4$. The per-class $F_1$ scores are 0.905, 0.8 and 0.381, and their mean, the macro $F_1$, is 0.695. The micro $F_1$ equals accuracy, $(90 + 40 + 4)/160 = 0.8375$. The cancellation queue explains the gap. It holds 6% of the tickets, so micro averaging barely registers that the system catches only 4 of 10 cancellations. Macro averaging gives that queue a third of the weight.
