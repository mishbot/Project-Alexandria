> [!note] Variable Elimination
>**Variable Elimination** is an exact inference algorithm that computes posterior probabilities more efficiently than inference by enumeration by **systematically eliminating hidden variables** one at a time. It applies to **Bayes Nets** and any model that factorizes a joint distribution into local conditional probability tables (CPTs), while avoiding construction of the full joint distribution  .
>
>The goal is to compute the posterior
>$$
> P(Q \mid e) = \alpha P(Q, e),
> $$
>
> where $Q$ is the query variable, $e$ is observed evidence, and $\alpha = \frac{1}{P(e)}$ is a normalization constant.   
> 
> Instead of summing over all hidden variables at once, variable elimination **reorders the computation** to reduce intermediate complexity. The joint term is implicitly represented as a product of **factors**:
>$$
> P(Q, e) = \sum_{h} \prod_i f_i,
> $$
>
> where each factor $f_i$ is an **unnormalized probability** derived from a CPT and evidence.
> 
> **Eliminating a variable** X consists of two steps:
> 1. **Join** (multiply) all factors that contain $X$.
> 2. **Sum out** $X$ from the resulting factor.
> 
> Formally, if $\mathcal{F}_X$ is the set of factors containing $X$,
>$$
> f_{\text{new}}(\mathbf{Y}) = \sum_{x} \prod_{f \in \mathcal{F}_X} f(x, \mathbf{Y}),
> $$
>
> where $\mathbf{Y}$ are the remaining variables in those factors.
> 
> This process is repeated for each hidden variable in a chosen **elimination order**. After all hidden variables are eliminated, the remaining factors are multiplied together and normalized to produce $P(Q \mid e)$.
>
> The key advantage over inference by enumeration is that **irrelevant terms are summed out early**, keeping intermediate factor sizes small. However, the efficiency of variable elimination critically depends on the elimination order: it only outperforms enumeration if the largest intermediate factor remains manageable.
