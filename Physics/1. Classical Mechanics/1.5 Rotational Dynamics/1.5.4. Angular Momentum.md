>[!note] Angular Momentum
>**Angular momentum** describes the rotational analog of [[1.4.1. Linear Momentum|linear momentum]] and measures how much rotational motion an object has about a given point or axis.
>
>For a **single particle**, angular momentum about a point (often the origin) is defined as
>$$\vec{L} = \vec{r} \cdot \vec{p} = \vec{r} \cdot (m\vec{v}),$$
>where $\vec{r}$ is the particle's position vector, and $\vec{p} =m \vec{v}$ is its linear momentum. The direction of $\vec{L}$ is perpendicular to the plane formed by $\vec{r}$ and $\vec{v}$, following the **right-hand rule**.
>
>For a **rigid body rotating** about a fixed axis (so that every point shares the same angular velocity $\vec{\omega}$, the total angular momentum becomes
>$$\vec{L} = I \boldsymbol{\omega},$$
>where $I$ is the [[1.5.2. Rotational Inertia|rotational inertia]] of the body about the axis of rotation, and $\omega$ is the [[1.5.1. Rotational Kinematics|angular velocity]].
>
>Just as linear momentum $\vec{p} = m\vec{v}$ is conserved in the absence of net external forces, angular momentum is conserved in the absence of net external torque, such that
>$$\frac{dL}{dt}=\tau_{\text{net}}.$$
>If $\tau_{\text{net}}=0$, then $\vec{L}=C$ is constant.

>[!example] (Easy) Two Disks
>A disk with moment of inertia $I_{1}$ is attached to a light, rigid rod and rotates counterclockwise with an angular velocity $\omega_{0}$. Another disk of rotational inertia $I_{2}$, which is not rotating, slides down along the rod. We assume there is no friction between the rod and the second disk. Once the second disk lands on the first disk, the two eventually rotate with the same angular velocity $\omega_{f}$ due to friction.
>
>![[Screenshot 2025-11-03 at 22.06.16.png|200]]
>
>>[!example]- Find the final angular velocity $\omega_{f}$.
>>Since momentum is conserved in this closed system, and the two disks end up rotating at the same speed, it follows that
>>$$I_{1}\omega_{0}=(I_{1}+I_{2})\omega_{f} \implies \omega_{f}=\frac{I_{1}}{I_{1}+I_{2}}\omega_{0}.$$
>
>>[!example]- Find the fractional change of kinetic energy, $\Delta K / K_{i}$. Assume gravity is not acting on the upper disk.
>>We have that
>>$$\frac{K_{f}-K_{i}}{K_{i}}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}(I_{1}+I_{2})\omega_{f}^2-\frac{1}{2}I_{1}\omega_{0}^2}{\frac{1}{2}I_{1}\omega_{0}^2}=-\frac{I_{2}}{I_{1}+I_{2}}$$

>[!example] (Hard) Orbiting Satellite
>A satellite of mass $m$ is in an elliptic orbit around the Earth of mass $M$. The center of the Earth is at distance $d_{E}$ from the center of the ellipse, and both point $A$ and point $C$ are at distance $d_{C}$ from the center of the ellipse. The speed of the satellite at point $A$ is given to be $v_{A}$.
>
>![[Screenshot 2025-11-04 at 0.22.19.png|250]]
>
>>[!example]- Determine the net torque acting on the satellite with respect to the axis going through the center of mass of the Earth, perpendicular to the plane of the orbit.
>>Since the gravitational force $F_{G}$ is parallel to the distance from Earth to the satellite, the net torque is zero (i.e., $\tau_{\text{net}}=0$).
>
>>[!example]- Determine the speed of the satellite at point $C$, based on your conclusion about net torque.
>>Since $\tau_{\text{net}}=0$, it follows that $\int \tau_{\text{net}}=L = C$. As such, we conclude that angular momentum is conserved. It follows that
>>$$mv_{C}(d_{C}+d_{E})=mv_{A}(d_{C}-d_{E}) \implies v_{C}=\frac{{d_{C}-d_{E}}}{d_{C}+d_{E}}v_{A}.$$

>[!example] (Hard) Potter's Wheel
>A potter's wheel is a solid disk of mass $M$ and radius $R$, mounted on a frictionless vertical axle. The wheel is initially at rest and the potter applies a tangential force of constant magnitude $F$ on the rim of the wheel during two full rotations as shown in the figure below.
>
>![[Screenshot 2025-11-04 at 12.49.42.png|250]]
>
>>[!example]- Determine the angular speed $\omega_{1}$ of the wheel after two rotations.
>>The torque of the wheel is given by $\tau=FR=I \alpha=\frac{1}{2}MR^2 \alpha$. As such, $\alpha=\frac{2F}{MR}$. Since velocity-displacement states that $\omega^2=2\alpha \Delta \theta$, it follows that
>>$$\omega_{1}=\sqrt{ \frac{16\pi F}{MR} }.$$
>
>Now, a wad of clay of negligible size and mass $m_{c}=M/9$ is tossed onto the wheel, spinning at the angular speed determined in the pervious part, with its velocity $v_{1}$ at right angle to the wheel's radius, and in the same direction as the wheel's rotation, like in the figure below. It sticks to the wheel at a distance $d=3R/4$ from the rotation axis.
>
>![[Screenshot 2025-11-04 at 13.14.48.png|250]]
>
>>[!example]- Explain which physical quantity is conserved before and after the clay hits the wheel, and why.
>>Total angular momentum is conserved, because the clay is included in the system. There are no net acting forces in the horizontal direction, and both gravity and the normal force are perpendicular to the axis of rotation.
>
>>[!example]- Determine the angular velocity $\omega_{2}$ of the wheel-clay system once the clay is stuck onto the wheel.
>>Using conservation of angular momentum, we have
>>$$\frac{3}{4}R \cdot \frac{M}{9}v_{1}+I\omega_{1}=\left( (\frac{3}{4}R )^2 \cdot \frac{M}{9} + I \right)\omega_{2},$$
>>where the rotational inertia of the disk is $I=\frac{1}{2}MR^2$. As such, we get
>>$$\omega_{2}=\frac{32}{9}\sqrt{ \frac{\pi F}{MR} } + \frac{4}{27} \frac{v_{1}}{R}.$$
>
>>[!example]- Determine under which condition the angular speed would remain the same before and after the clay hits the wheel.  
>>The angular speed will remain the same under $\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}$. Equating these two angular velocities and solving for $v_{1}$, we obtain
>>$$v_{1}=3 \sqrt{ \frac{\pi FR}{M} }.$$
>
>Now the clay can be thrown in any direction.  
>
>>[!example]- Imagine a scenario where the wheel would start spinning in the opposite direction after the clay hits the wheel. Explain without any calculations.
>>The clay must be thrown from a direction that has a tangential to the axis of rotation component in the opposite direction of the disk's rotation at a large enough velocity, such that the clay's angular momentum exceeds the wheel's angular momentum.
