>[!note] Behavioral Finance
>Behavioral finance studies how predictable deviations from the rational benchmark shape individual decisions and, when combined with real-world trading constraints, can move prices and generate return patterns.
>- In **neoclassical finance**, agents are rational, and markets are perfectly competitive. 
>	- Beliefs are updated via Bayes' rule, and decisions under uncertainty follow expected utility maximization (i.e., $\max\limits_{x}\mathbb{E}[u(x)]$ for some action $x$).
>	- The rationality of agents and the assumption of perfect markets makes arbitrage impossible.
>- In **institutional finance**, rational agents operate within frictional markets.
>	- Frictions include transaction costs, regulatory constraints, etc.
>	- Short selling is restricted due to institutional mandate rules and high lending fees.
>	- Imperfect arbitrage (finite capital, leverage constraints, transaction costs, career risk).
>
>Behavioral finance removes the assumption that agents are rational by incorporating physical biases in investor decision-making:
>- **Irrational/biased expectations:** agents are assumed to form biased expectations (overconfident, trend-extrapolating, and inattentive), so beliefs depart from Bayesian updating.
>- **Behavioral preferences:** agents are assumed to have reference-dependent and time-inconsistent preferences (loss averse with present bias and self-control problems), so choices depart from expected utility.
>- **Limits to arbitrage:** agents are assumed to face binding trading constraints (short-sale frictions and funding/liquidity limits), so mispricing is not instantly arbitraged away.
>
>Behavioral finance has experienced its fair share of critiques. Two of the most famous ones incluide:
>- **The Arbitrage Critique**: Milton Friedman argued that although irrational investors can exist, they are swept away by rational arbitrageurs who trade against them and eliminate mispricings.

>- **The Lack of Discipline Critique**: Behavioral finance can be post-hoc storytelling, so explanations should be judged by new, testable predictions, and not just by fitting an anomaly.
