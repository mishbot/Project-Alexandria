>[!note] Carnot Engine
>The **Carnot cycle** is the cycle of an ideal engine running between two reservoirs at $T_H$ and $T_L$. It has four [[2.7.3 Reversible Process|reversible]] legs. The first is an [[2.5.3d Adiabatic Process|adiabatic]] compression $A \to B$ that warms the gas from $T_L$ to $T_H$. The second is an [[2.5.3c Isothermal Process|isothermal]] expansion $B \to C$ at $T_H$, during which the gas absorbs $Q_H$ from the hot reservoir. The third is an adiabatic expansion $C \to D$ that cools the gas back to $T_L$. The fourth is an isothermal compression $D \to A$ at $T_L$, during which the gas rejects $Q_L$ to the cold reservoir. Heat flows only on the two isotherms, and on an isotherm the heat equals the work, so
>$$Q_H = NkT_H \ln\frac{V_C}{V_B}, \qquad Q_L = NkT_L \ln\frac{V_D}{V_A}.$$
>The two adiabats force $V_C / V_B = V_D / V_A$, the logarithms cancel, and the efficiency depends only on the two temperatures,
>$$e_C = 1 - \frac{T_L}{T_H},$$
>the **Carnot efficiency**, with both temperatures in kelvins. **Carnot's theorem** states that no engine operating between the same two reservoirs can be more efficient than a Carnot engine. Every reversible engine between them has exactly this efficiency, whatever its working substance. The theorem follows from the [[2.7.2 Second Law of Thermodynamics|second law]]: an engine that beat the Carnot efficiency could drive a reversed Carnot engine and move heat from cold to hot with no net work.
>
>The efficiency reaches $1$ only for $T_L = 0$ or $T_H \to \infty$, neither of which is available, so every engine rejects heat. A real engine between the same temperatures has $e < e_C$ because its processes are not reversible. A steam turbine between $500\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $30\ ^\circ\text{C}$ has $e_C = 0.61$ and reaches about $0.40$.

# Intuition

Why these four legs? An engine takes in heat and gives out work, and the [[2.5.3c Isothermal Process|isothermal expansion]] is the process that converts heat to work completely, with no change in internal energy. So the intake of $Q_H$ should be an isotherm at the hottest available temperature. The rejection of $Q_L$ should likewise be an isotherm at the coldest available temperature, so that as little heat as possible leaves. Between the two isotherms the gas has to change temperature, and the only way to do that without exchanging heat is an [[2.5.3d Adiabatic Process|adiabat]]. Two isotherms and two adiabats are the only choice that takes in heat at $T_H$ alone, rejects it at $T_L$ alone, and exchanges no heat anywhere else. The loop closes because adiabats are steeper than isotherms. The diagram shows the cycle.

Carnot published the cycle in 1824, before the first law was known, to answer a practical question: how much work can a given amount of heat produce? His answer was that the limit depends only on the two temperatures. That is the reason power plants run their boilers as hot as the metal allows and cool their condensers with the coldest water they can find.

# Properties

#### *(Temperatures Only)* The Carnot efficiency depends on $T_H$ and $T_L$ and on nothing else about the engine.

The working gas, the volumes, the pressures, and the amount of gas all cancel out of $e_C$. The only way to improve a Carnot engine is to raise $T_H$ or lower $T_L$. Doubling the absolute hot temperature with $T_L$ fixed takes the efficiency from $1 - T_L/T_H$ to $1 - T_L/2T_H$. Lowering $T_L$ is usually not an option, since the cold reservoir is the environment, so engineering effort goes into raising $T_H$.

#### *(A Ceiling for Every Engine)* Any engine between the same reservoirs has $e \leq e_C$, with equality only if it is reversible.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture stated Carnot's theorem but did not prove it. The coupled-engine proof in this property follows Giancoli section 20.3.

Suppose an engine $X$ had $e_X > e_C$. Run $X$ forward and use its work to drive a Carnot engine backward as a refrigerator. Because $X$ is more efficient it needs less heat from the hot reservoir to produce the work that the Carnot refrigerator uses to return a larger amount of heat there. The net effect is heat flowing from cold to hot with no outside work, which the Clausius statement of the [[2.7.2 Second Law of Thermodynamics|second law]] forbids. So no such $X$ exists. Applying the argument to two reversible engines in both directions shows that all reversible engines have the same efficiency.

#### *(Where Real Cycles Lose)* A cycle falls short of Carnot whenever it takes in heat below $T_H$ or rejects heat above $T_L$.

The rectangular cycle worked in the [[2.7.1 Heat Engine|heat engine]] note reaches $e = 0.22$ between extreme temperatures whose ratio is $9$, where the Carnot value is $1 - 1/9 = 0.89$. It gets a quarter of the way. Most of its $Q_H$ enters on an isobaric leg at temperatures well below the maximum, and most of its $Q_L$ leaves on an isobaric leg well above the minimum, so the effective temperatures it works between are much closer together than the extremes suggest. The Carnot cycle avoids this by exchanging heat only at $T_H$ and only at $T_L$. The [[2.7.4a Otto Cycle|Otto cycle]] falls short for the same reason. Its heat enters over a range of temperatures on the ignition leg and leaves over a range on the exhaust leg. A useful ratio $Q_L / Q_H = T_L / T_H$ holds for the Carnot cycle and for no other, and the entropy notes at the end of the section show what that ratio means.

#### *(Slow and Useless in Practice)* A Carnot engine delivers its work infinitely slowly, so no real engine is built as one.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not discuss the speed or power of a Carnot engine. This property follows Giancoli section 20.3.

Reversibility requires isothermal legs that exchange heat across a vanishing temperature difference, which takes forever. A real engine trades efficiency for power by running hotter than its reservoir on intake and colder on exhaust. The Carnot cycle is a standard of comparison, not a design. Its value is the number $1 - T_L/T_H$, which tells an engineer how far a real machine is from the limit.

# Derivation

We want to show that $Q_L / Q_H = T_L / T_H$. On the isothermal leg $B \to C$ the internal energy does not change, so the heat absorbed equals the work done, $Q_H = NkT_H \ln(V_C / V_B)$. On the isothermal leg $D \to A$ the gas is compressed from $V_D$ to $V_A < V_D$, work is done on it, and the same amount leaves as heat: $Q_L = NkT_L \ln(V_D / V_A)$, positive because $V_D > V_A$. The ratio is
$$\frac{Q_L}{Q_H} = \frac{T_L \ln(V_D / V_A)}{T_H \ln(V_C / V_B)}.$$
The volumes are not independent, because the four states lie on one cycle. Along the adiabat $C \to D$, $TV^{\gamma - 1}$ is constant, so $T_H V_C^{\gamma - 1} = T_L V_D^{\gamma - 1}$. Along the adiabat $A \to B$, $T_H V_B^{\gamma - 1} = T_L V_A^{\gamma - 1}$. Dividing the first equation by the second,
$$\left( \frac{V_C}{V_B} \right)^{\gamma - 1} = \left( \frac{V_D}{V_A} \right)^{\gamma - 1}, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \frac{V_C}{V_B} = \frac{V_D}{V_A}.$$
The two logarithms are equal and cancel, leaving $Q_L / Q_H = T_L / T_H$ and $e_C = 1 - T_L / T_H$, the headline result. The step that closes the cycle, using both adiabats to relate the four volumes, is the same step that every cycle problem needs. Solved for the volumes on the cold isotherm, the two adiabat conditions give
$$V_A = V_B \left( \frac{T_H}{T_L} \right)^{1/(\gamma - 1)}, \qquad V_D = V_C \left( \frac{T_H}{T_L} \right)^{1/(\gamma - 1)},$$
with $1/(\gamma - 1) = d/2$. Given $V_B$, $V_C$, and the two temperatures, these fix the whole cycle.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Steam Turbine
>A power plant runs steam between a boiler at $500\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and a condenser at $30\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the maximum possible efficiency.
>>Convert to kelvins, $773\ \text{K}$ and $303\ \text{K}$, then
>>$$e_C = 1 - \frac{303}{773} = 0.61.$$
>>No turbine between these temperatures can convert more than $61$ percent of the boiler's heat into work. Actual plants reach about $40$ percent. The rest of the boiler's heat warms the river or the cooling tower.

>[!example] (Medium) Checking a Claim
>An engine is claimed to deliver $400\ \text{J}$ of work per cycle from $1000\ \text{J}$ of heat taken at $400\ \text{K}$, rejecting the rest at $300\ \text{K}$.
>
>>[!example]- Is the claim possible, and what is the most work the engine could deliver?
>>The Carnot efficiency between these reservoirs is $1 - 300/400 = 0.25$, so the most work from $1000\ \text{J}$ is $250\ \text{J}$. The claim of $400\ \text{J}$, a $40$ percent efficiency, exceeds the ceiling and is impossible. It would require rejecting only $600\ \text{J}$ at $300\ \text{K}$, less than the $750\ \text{J}$ that a Carnot engine must reject, and Carnot's theorem says no engine rejects less. The [[2.7.7 Entropy Statement of the Second Law|entropy statement]] reaches the same verdict in one line: the two reservoirs would change by $-1000/400 + 600/300 = -0.50\ \text{J/K}$, a decrease.

>[!example] (Hard) A Carnot Cycle in Numbers
>One mole of helium runs a Carnot cycle between $T_H = 500\ \text{K}$ and $T_L = 300\ \text{K}$. The isothermal expansion at $T_H$ takes the gas from $V_B = 1.00\ \text{L}$ to $V_C = 3.00\ \text{L}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find $Q_H$, the work per cycle, $Q_L$, and the volumes $V_D$ and $V_A$.
>>The heat in is the isothermal work,
>>$$Q_H = nRT_H \ln\frac{V_C}{V_B} = (1)(8.314)(500)\ln 3 = 4.57\ \text{kJ}.$$
>>The efficiency is $1 - 300/500 = 0.400$, so $W = 1.83\ \text{kJ}$ and $Q_L = Q_H - W = 2.74\ \text{kJ}$. Helium has $\gamma = 5/3$, so $\gamma - 1 = 2/3$ and the adiabat $C \to D$ gives
>>$$V_D = V_C \left( \frac{T_H}{T_L} \right)^{1/(\gamma - 1)} = (3.00\ \text{L})\left( \frac{500}{300} \right)^{3/2} = 6.45\ \text{L}.$$
>>The adiabat $A \to B$ gives $V_A = V_B (T_H/T_L)^{3/2} = 2.15\ \text{L}$. The ratio $V_D / V_A = 3.00$ equals $V_C / V_B$, as the derivation requires, and $Q_L = nRT_L \ln 3 = 2.74\ \text{kJ}$ checks against the first law.
