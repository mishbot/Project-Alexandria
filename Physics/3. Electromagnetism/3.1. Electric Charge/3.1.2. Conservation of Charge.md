>[!note] Conservation of Charge
>The **conservation of charge** states that the net charge of an isolated system never changes. Charge can move from one object to another inside the system. No process creates or destroys net charge. For an isolated system of objects with charges $Q_1, Q_2, \ldots$,
>$$Q_{\text{total}} = \sum_i Q_i = \text{constant}.$$
>When two objects exchange charge and nothing else is involved, one gains exactly what the other loses:
>$$\Delta Q_1 = -\Delta Q_2.$$
>Here each $\Delta Q$ is final minus initial. In ordinary charging the rule follows from counting electrons. Electrons move between objects, and none appear or disappear along the way. Two consequences follow:
>- Rubbing two neutral objects together leaves them with charges $+q$ and $-q$ of exactly equal size.
>- A neutral object stays neutral overall when nearby charges rearrange its electrons. One region turns positive and another turns negative by the same amount.
>
>Conservation holds together with [[3.1.1. Electric Charge|quantization]]. The total charge is always a whole-number multiple of $e$, and it stays the same number.

# Intuition

Track the electrons. When fur rubs a plastic rod, some electrons leave the fur and land on the rod. The fur is now short of electrons and the rod has extra, by the same count. Nothing else changed, so the total charge of fur plus rod is still zero. Every charging process in this course works the same way. Charge moves from place to place, and the total stays the same.

Conservation can be used to find unknown charges. If the total is known and every piece but one is known, the last piece follows from subtraction. Forces and fields are often not needed at all.

# Properties

#### *(Isolated Systems Only)* The total stays fixed only when no charge can enter or leave the system.

A wire to the ground, a hand, or a generator lets charge flow in or out. Then the total of the objects under study can change. The total of everything connected to them still stays fixed. A classroom demonstration hangs a metal-coated ping-pong ball between two charged plates. One plate is connected to a generator and the other to the ground. The ball carries charge across, and the generator keeps resupplying the plates. The plates alone are therefore not an isolated system. With both plates disconnected, the total of the plates and the ball would stay fixed. The note [[3.1.6. Induced Charge]] explains why the ball moves.

#### *(Counting Electrons)* In ordinary charging, every change in charge comes from a whole number of electrons gained or lost.

An object that gains $N$ electrons changes its charge by $\Delta Q = -Ne$. An object that loses $N$ electrons changes by $\Delta Q = +Ne$. Conservation then says that the electrons gained by some objects equal the electrons lost by the others. Counting electrons and adding charges give the same answer. Counting is often easier when several transfers happen one after another.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Fur and Rod
>A plastic rod rubbed with fur ends with a charge of $-40\ \text{nC}$. Both objects started neutral.
>
>>[!example]- What is the charge on the fur, and how many electrons moved?
>>Conservation gives the fur $+40\ \text{nC}$. The rod gained the electrons the fur lost, so the number moved is
>>$$N = \frac{40 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{C}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}} = 2.5 \times 10^{11}.$$

>[!example] (Medium) The Missing Charge
>Three objects form an isolated system. They start with charges $Q_A = +5.0\ \text{nC}$, $Q_B = -3.0\ \text{nC}$, and $Q_C = 0$. After they are rubbed against each other, A carries $+1.0\ \text{nC}$ and B carries $-6.0\ \text{nC}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the final charge on C, and say which objects gained electrons.
>>The total is fixed at $5.0 - 3.0 + 0 = +2.0\ \text{nC}$. The final charges must add to the same total:
>>$$1.0 + (-6.0) + Q_C = 2.0,$$
>>so $Q_C = +7.0\ \text{nC}$. The changes are $\Delta Q_A = -4.0$, $\Delta Q_B = -3.0$, and $\Delta Q_C = +7.0\ \text{nC}$. Objects A and B became more negative, so they gained electrons. Object C lost them. The changes add to zero, as conservation requires.

>[!example] (Hard) Two Transfers in a Row
>A neutral plastic rod is rubbed with neutral fur and gains $2.0 \times 10^{11}$ electrons. The rod is then rubbed with a neutral cloth, which takes $5.0 \times 10^{10}$ electrons from it.
>
>>[!example]- Find the final charges on the rod, the fur, and the cloth.
>>Count electrons first. The rod gained $2.0 \times 10^{11}$ and lost $5.0 \times 10^{10}$, a net gain of $1.5 \times 10^{11}$. Its charge is
>>$$Q_{\text{rod}} = -(1.5 \times 10^{11})(1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}) = -24\ \text{nC}.$$
>>The fur lost $2.0 \times 10^{11}$ electrons, so $Q_{\text{fur}} = +32\ \text{nC}$. The cloth gained $5.0 \times 10^{10}$ electrons, so $Q_{\text{cloth}} = -8.0\ \text{nC}$. The three charges add to $-24 + 32 - 8.0 = 0$, the starting total.
