>[!note] Exchange-Traded Funds
>An **exchange-traded fund (ETF)** is an investment fund that holds a diversified portfolio of underlying assets, and can be bought and sold on stock exchanges (secondary markets), just like individual equities (stocks).
>
>ETFs can be structured to track anything from the price of a commodity to a large and diverse collection of stocks, which may be done either using passive or active investment strategies. **Leveraged ETFs** seek to deliver multiples of daily index returns by leveraging derivatives and shorting. However, leveraged ETFs typically underperform relative to objectives in the long run.
>
>As with [[2.3.2. Mutual Funds|mutual funds]], dividends, interest, and capital gains (although rare for ETFs) from the pool are distributed to ETF shareholders. However, since ETFs trade intraday, the supply of shares is regulated through a **creation and redemption** mechanism, which keeps market price close to the **Net Asset Value (NAV)** to prevent inefficiencies that create arbitrage opportunities. It involves a two-step process:
>1. When an ETF manager wants to issue more shares, an authorized participant (AP) buys shares of the assets associated with the ETF.
>2. Then, the AP sells or exchanges the assets to the ETF for new shares of the ETF at equal value. 
>
>In turn, the AP sells the ETF shares in the market for a profit if the selling price is greater than the ETF's NAV, creating an arbitrage opportunity.
>
>The top 3 ETFs based on Assets under Management (AUM) are the **SPDR S&P 500 ETF (SPY)**, the iShares S&5 500 ETF (IVY), and the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO).
