>[!note] Waves
>A wave is an [[1.6.1. Simple Harmonic Motion|oscillation]] that transfers energy from one point to another **without transferring matter**. There are two types of simple waves:
>- In a **transverse wave**, the particles of the medium move perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation (e.g. light waves, water waves). A series of crests and troughs travel along a transverse wave, such that crests represent positive peaks and vice-versa. A transverse wave's vertical displacement at time $t$ and displacement $x$ can be modeled as
>$$D(x, t)=A\sin(kx \pm  \omega_{n} t + \phi),$$
>where $k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$ is the wave number, which represents how “tightly packed” the wave crests are in space, such that $\lambda$ is the wavelength (the horizontal distance between two equal vertical points). The speed of a wave is therefore $v=\lambda f$. Here, $\lambda$ represents the distance traveled in one period, and $f$ is the number of oscillations per second of the wave.
>‎ 
>- A **longitudinal wave** is a wave in which particles of the medium move parallel to the direction of wave propagation (e.g., sound waves, movement of a slinky). A series of compressions and expansions travel along a longitudinal wave, such that compressions indicate areas where the coils are tightly packed and vice-versa. The speed of a longitudinal wave, given a solid with an elastic modulus or a liquid with a bulk modulus $M$ and density $\rho$ is defined as
>$$v=\sqrt{ \frac{M}{\rho} }.$$
>This equation helps model longitudinal waves as transverse waves, such that $v=\lambda f$, from which one can find the period, frequency and wavelength of the wave.

# Examples
>[!example] Bug on a Rope
>A large bug is standing on a circus tightrope that is stretched with tension $F_{T}$. The rope has mass per unit length $\mu$. The tightrope walker, wanting to shake the bug off the rope, moves her foot up and down near the end of the rope, generating a sinusoidal transverse wave of wavelength $\lambda$ and amplitude $A$. You may assume that the mass of the bug is too small to have any effect on the wave propagation. The acceleration due to gravity has magnitude $g$ and air resistance is negligible.
>
>>[!example]- What is the displacement $y(x,t)$ of the rope in the vertical direction, assuming a propagation in the $+x$ direction, and an arbitrary phase shift $\Phi$?
>>The wave number is always $k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$. In a rope, we have that $v=\sqrt{ F_{T} / \mu }$. Since $T=2\pi / \omega$ and $v=\lambda / T$, we have that $\omega = 2\pi\frac{v}{\lambda}$. Since we are propagating in the $+x$ direction, we have that
>>$$y(x, t) = A\sin\left( \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}x - 2\pi \sqrt{ \frac{F_{T}}{\lambda^2 \mu} }t + \Phi \right).$$
>
>>[!example]- Calculate the acceleration of the rope in its up-down motion as a function of $x$ and $t$.
>>The acceleration is the second derivative of displacement with respect to time. As such,
>>$$a(x, t) = -A 4\pi^2 \frac{F_{T}}{\lambda^2 \mu} \sin\left( \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}x - 2\pi \sqrt{ \frac{F_{T}}{\lambda^2 \mu} }t + \Phi \right).$$
>
>>[!example]- Determine the minimum amplitude $A_{\text{min}}$ that would cause the bug to become momentarily "weightless" at some point as the wave passes underneath it.  
>>For the bug to become "weightless", there must be an acceleration that counteracts gravity. We conveniently set the point to be at the amplitude of the acceleration, such that
>>$$g=A_{\text{min}} \frac{4\pi^2F_{T}}{\lambda^2 \mu} \implies A_{\text{min}} = \frac{\lambda^2\mu g}{4\pi^2F_{T}}.$$
>
