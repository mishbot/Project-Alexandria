>[!note] Digital Images
>An **image** can be modeled as a function $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$, where $f(x, y)$ gives the intensity at position $(x, y)$. A real image is defined only over a rectangle and has a finite range, $f: [a, b] \times [c, d] \to [0, 1]$. A color image is three such functions pasted together, written as a vector-valued function
>$$f(x, y) = \begin{bmatrix} r(x, y) \\ g(x, y) \\ b(x, y) \end{bmatrix},$$
>where $r$, $g$ and $b$ give the red, green and blue intensities. A **digital image** comes from **sampling** $f$ on a grid of pixel positions and **quantizing** each sample to a finite set of levels. With 8 bits per channel, the levels are the $256$ integers $0, \dots, 255$.
>
>A pixel value measures light. A source emits light, a fraction of it reflects off a surface into the camera, and the lens focuses it onto the sensor. A simple model of the result is
>$$f(x, y) = \text{reflectance}(x, y) \cdot \text{illumination}(x, y),$$
>with reflectance in $[0, 1]$ and illumination in $[0, \infty)$. The **acquisition pipeline** turns scene radiance (in W/sr/m²) into sensor irradiance through the lens, then into sensor exposure through the shutter (open for a time $\Delta t$). The sensor (a CCD in the slides) turns exposure into analog voltages. An ADC (analog-to-digital converter) turns the voltages into digital values. A final remapping curve gives the stored pixel values. Real scenes span a **dynamic range** of about $10^{-6}$ to $10^{6}$ in brightness, while an 8-bit picture stores $0$ to $255$. An exposure records only a window of the scene's range.
>
>In code, an image is an array of shape $(H, W, C)$ for height (rows), width (columns) and channels, called channel-last or HWC. PyTorch uses channel-first, CHW. The pixel at column $x$ and row $y$ is `img[y, x]`. Two dtypes are common: `uint8` with values $0$–$255$, and float with values $0.0$–$1.0$. Convert with `img.astype(np.float32) / 255`. OpenCV loads color images in BGR order. Each channel shown alone is a grayscale map of one primary color. The standard grayscale (luminance) conversion weights the channels by the eye's sensitivity:
>$$Y = 0.299\,R + 0.587\,G + 0.114\,B.$$
>Here $R$, $G$ and $B$ are the channel values of one pixel, and $Y$ is its gray value on the same scale.

>[!warning] Casting to `uint8`
>Casting floats to `uint8` truncates instead of rounding: $255.2$ becomes $255$ and $0.1$ becomes $0$. A float image in $[0, 1]$ cast directly becomes almost all zeros. Out-of-range values wrap around: the discussion shows $256.0$ becoming $0$, though NumPy does not promise any particular result for out-of-range casts. Arithmetic on `uint8` arrays also wraps modulo $256$. Do arithmetic in float, then clip to $[0, 255]$ and round before casting back.

# Intuition

Treating an image as a function lets us use the mathematics of functions on it. Filtering changes the values that $f$ takes, and warping changes where the values sit, as in [[3.1. Affine Transformations|affine transformations]]. A grayscale image can even be drawn as a surface, with brightness as height. A digital image is that function after two losses. Sampling keeps values only at grid points, as in [[2.10a Sampling|sampling]] of signals, and the space between them must later be filled by guessing ([[2.3. Resampling|resampling]]). Quantization rounds each kept value to the nearest allowed level. The figure shows both steps on one row of an image.

Pixel values measure light, so they mix two causes: the surface (reflectance) and the lighting (illumination). A gray shirt in sunlight and a white shirt in shade can produce the same pixel value. The sensor also stores only a narrow window of the brightness range found in the world. Choosing the exposure chooses which window. Tone adjustments that remap pixel values one at a time are the subject of [[2.8. Point Processing|point processing]].

# Properties

#### *(Array Layout)* NumPy stores an image as `img[row, column, channel]`, and the row index grows downward.

So the first index is $y$, the second is $x$, and the origin is the top-left corner. Converting HWC to CHW for PyTorch is `img.transpose(2, 0, 1)`. A list of images becomes a batch with a new leading axis, `np.stack(images, axis=0)`, of shape $(N, H, W, C)$, and its CHW version is `batch.transpose(0, 3, 1, 2)`. Because $y$ points down, a rotation matrix that turns counterclockwise in a $y$-up frame turns clockwise on screen, as noted in [[3.1. Affine Transformations]].

#### *(Dtype Ranges)* The same picture is stored as `uint8` in $[0, 255]$ or as float in $[0, 1]$, and mixing the two scales breaks results silently.

JPG files load as `uint8`. TIFF files may load as `uint16`, with values up to $65{,}535$. Dividing a `uint16` image by $255$ gives values up to $257$, which display as almost pure white. Divide by the maximum of the actual dtype, or use a helper such as `skimage.img_as_float`, before combining images. Image writers expect floats in $[0, 1]$ or `uint8` in $[0, 255]$, so clip before saving.

#### *(Channel Order)* OpenCV returns BGR, while most other libraries expect RGB.

Showing a BGR array with an RGB viewer swaps red and blue: skies turn orange and red signs turn blue. Reverse the last axis with `img[:, :, ::-1]` or `np.flip(img, axis=2)`. In a BGR array the red channel is index $2$, so zeroing the red component is `img[:, :, 2] = 0`. The Prokudin-Gorskii glass plates used for color alignment with [[2.1.5. Template Matching|template matching]] are also stacked as B, G, R from top to bottom.

#### *(Luminance Weights)* Equal weights would make pure blue look as bright as pure green.

The human eye is most sensitive to green light, less to red, and least to blue. The weights $0.299$, $0.587$ and $0.114$ follow this response. Pure green $(0, 255, 0)$ gets $Y = 149.7$ and pure blue $(0, 0, 255)$ gets $Y = 29.1$, while the plain average $(R + G + B)/3$ gives both $85$. The weights sum to $1$, so white $(255, 255, 255)$ stays $255$.

#### *(Exposure Window)* An 8-bit image stores a brightness ratio of at most $255 : 1$, far less than a real scene contains.

The slides list relative scene brightnesses from $1$ up to $2{,}000{,}000{,}000$, from a dim room to the disk of the sun. A long exposure maps the dark part of the scene into $0$–$255$ and clips everything brighter to $255$. A short exposure keeps the bright part and crushes the shadows to $0$. The final remapping step of the pipeline is a nonlinear curve, so stored values are not proportional to the light that hit the sensor. Pixel arithmetic that assumes proportionality, such as averaging two exposures, is therefore only approximate.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Averaging Two Photos Stored as `uint8`
>Two aligned photos `a` and `b` are `uint8` arrays. At one pixel, `a` is $200$ and `b` is $100$. The code computes `(a + b) / 2`.
>
>>[!example]- What value comes out, and how should the average be computed?
>>The sum `a + b` is computed in `uint8`, so $300$ wraps to $300 - 256 = 44$. Dividing by $2$ then gives $22.0$ instead of $150$. Casting first fixes it: `(a.astype(np.float32) + b) / 2` gives $150.0$. The discussion's motion-trail one-liner, `np.mean(video, axis=0)`, is safe, because `np.mean` accumulates `uint8` input in `float64` and returns $150.0$ here.

>[!example] (Easy) The Channels of a Stop Sign
>A photo of a red stop sign has the RGB value $(200, 30, 40)$ on the sign. The same photo loaded with OpenCV stores the pixel as `[40, 30, 200]`.
>
>>[!example]- How does the sign look in each channel? What gray value does it get? What goes wrong if the OpenCV array is treated as RGB?
>>Shown as grayscale maps, the sign is bright in the R channel ($200$) and dark in the G and B channels ($30$ and $40$). Its luminance is $0.299 \cdot 200 + 0.587 \cdot 30 + 0.114 \cdot 40 = 81.97$, about $82$. The plain average is $90$.
>>
>>If the BGR array is read as RGB, channel $0$ is taken as red and gives $40$. The sign then looks dark in the "red" channel and bright in the "blue" one. The grayscale value becomes $0.299 \cdot 40 + 0.587 \cdot 30 + 0.114 \cdot 200 = 52.4$, which is wrong by about $30$ levels.

>[!example] (Medium) Splitting a Prokudin-Gorskii Scan
>A scanned glass plate loads as a `uint16` array of shape $(3209, 1100)$. The three exposures are stacked vertically in the order B, G, R.
>
>>[!example]- Split the plates, build an RGB float image in $[0, 1]$, and give the shapes in HWC and CHW. How much memory does the float32 result use?
>>The height is not a multiple of $3$, so each plate gets $H = \lfloor 3209/3 \rfloor = 1069$ rows and the last $2$ rows are dropped. The plates are `b = im[:1069]`, `g = im[1069:2138]` and `r = im[2138:3207]`, each of shape $(1069, 1100)$. The RGB image is `np.dstack([r, g, b]) / 65535`, of shape $(1069, 1100, 3)$ in HWC. Dividing by $255$ instead would give values up to $257$. The CHW version is `rgb.transpose(2, 0, 1)`, of shape $(3, 1069, 1100)$. As float32 it uses $1069 \cdot 1100 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 = 14{,}110{,}800$ bytes, about $14.1$ MB. The `uint16` original needs half that, and float64 needs twice that.
