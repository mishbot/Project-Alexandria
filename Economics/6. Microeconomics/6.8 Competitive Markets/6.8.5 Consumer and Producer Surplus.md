>[!note] Consumer and Producer Surplus
>**Surplus** measures in dollars what buyers and sellers gain from a market. Dollars can be added across people, so surplus lets us compare policies that help some people and hurt others.
>- **Producer surplus** of a price-taking firm that sells $y_0$ at price $p$ is its profit, $\pi(p, y_0) = py_0 - c(y_0)$. It has two pictures:
>$$\pi(p, y_0) = y_0\left[p - \frac{c(y_0)}{y_0}\right] = \int_0^{y_0}\big(p - c'_y(y)\big)\,dy - c(0).$$
>- The first is a rectangle, quantity times the gap between price and average cost. The second is the area between the price line and the MC curve from $0$ to $y_0$, minus the fixed cost $c(0)$. So the area above MC is profit plus fixed cost, and it equals profit when $c(0) = 0$.
>- For a consumer, the welfare effect of a price change from $p_0$ to $p_1$ is the change in the money needed to reach a fixed utility $u$, measured with the expenditure function $e(p, u)$ of [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]]:
>$$e(p_0, u) - e(p_1, u) = \int_{p_1}^{p_0} h(p, u)\,dp.$$
>- The step uses $\partial e(p, u)/\partial p = h(p, u)$, the Hicksian (compensated) demand, from the [[7.3 Envelope Theorem|envelope theorem]]. The measure is the area to the left of the Hicksian demand curve between the two prices. It is positive for a price fall and negative for a price rise.
>- **Consumer surplus** at price $p$ is this measure for a fall from the **choke price** $\bar p$, where demand reaches zero, down to $p$. It is the area under the demand curve and above the price.
>- Market diagrams use ordinary market demand $X^D(p, M)$ instead of $h$. The two areas agree when the good has no income effect. Otherwise, for a normal good, the ordinary area lies between the Hicksian areas at the old and the new utility (not taught).
>- Market demand and supply are horizontal sums ([[6.8.1 Aggregation]]), so market CS is the sum of individual CS. The area to the left of market supply is total profit plus total fixed cost $\sum_j c_j(0)$ (not taught).
>- **Total surplus** is $W = CS + PS$, plus tax revenue when there is a tax. A competitive equilibrium maximizes it (not proved in lecture). [[6.8.6 Welfare Applications]] uses $W$ to rank taxes, trade, and price controls.

# Intuition

A policy that changes a price helps some people and hurts others. To weigh the two, we need one unit for both. Utility does not work, because it is only defined up to an increasing transformation ([[6.1.3 Utility Functions]]), and one person's utility cannot be added to another's. Dollar amounts can be added. For a firm, profit is already in dollars. For a consumer, the expenditure function $e(p, u)$ gives the least money that reaches utility $u$ at price $p$. If a price fall cuts this amount by $\$50$, the price fall is worth $\$50$ to her.

In a market diagram, both surpluses are areas. A point on the demand curve shows what the buyer of that unit is willing to pay for it. A buyer who would pay up to $2$ and pays $4/3$ gains $2/3$ on that unit. Adding these gains over all units bought gives the area under demand and above the price. A point on the supply curve shows the marginal cost of that unit. Adding $p - MC$ over all units sold gives the area above supply and below the price. The diagram uses the market of the Easy example, with $X^D = 100 - 50p$ and $Y^S = 25p$.

# Properties

#### *(Two Pictures of Profit)* The rectangle sits on the AC curve and the area sits on the MC curve, and the two differ by the fixed cost.

The rectangle $y_0\,(p - AC(y_0))$ has height equal to profit per unit. The area form adds the gain $p - c'_y(y)$ of each unit from $0$ to $y_0$. Marginal costs add up to variable cost only, so the fixed cost $c(0)$ is subtracted at the end. The area between price and MC is revenue minus variable cost. Units with $MC > p$, such as the first units of a non-convex technology, enter with a negative sign. The formula writes the fixed cost as $c(0)$, the cost of producing nothing. If the fixed cost is a setup cost paid only when $y > 0$, as for the food truck in [[6.7.5 Profit Maximization and Supply]], then $c(0) = 0$ and $c$ jumps at zero. The formula then holds with the setup cost $F$ in place of $c(0)$. The figure uses the Medium example, with $c(y) = 64 + y^2$ and $p = 24$.

#### *(Why the Hicksian Area)* A price fall of $dp$ saves the consumer $h(p, u)\,dp$ dollars, and the welfare measure adds these savings along the price path.

When the price falls by $dp$, the $h$ units she buys each cost $dp$ less, which saves $h\,dp$. She also switches toward the cheaper good. At the expenditure-minimizing bundle, both goods cost the same per unit of utility, so the switch saves nothing to first order. Hence $de = h\,dp$, and integrating from $p_1$ to $p_0$ gives the measure. In the surface below, the measure is the drop in height along the slice of fixed utility $u_0$. The slope of that slice at each price is $h(p, u_0)$. Utility stays fixed along the whole path, so the measure uses compensated demand and contains no income effect. The surface is $e = 2u\sqrt p$ from the Hard example.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture drew market surplus areas and the Hicksian area. It did not derive the three results below: how market surplus adds up, why equilibrium maximizes total surplus, and how the ordinary-demand area compares with the Hicksian one.

#### *(Market Surplus Adds Up)* Market curves are horizontal sums, so market surplus is the sum of individual surpluses.

Market demand is $X^D(p) = \sum_j x_j(p)$, so $CS = \int_p^{\bar p} X^D(q)\,dq = \sum_j \int_p^{\bar p} x_j(q)\,dq$. Supply works the same way. A firm's maximal profit $\pi^*(p)$ has slope $y^*(p)$ by the envelope theorem. So the area to the left of its supply curve, from the price where it starts producing up to $p$, is $\pi^*(p) + c(0)$. This also holds with an avoidable setup cost, where supply jumps at min AC and $c(0) = 0$. Total surplus counts a dollar the same whoever receives it, so it ignores how gains are shared.

#### *(Equilibrium Maximizes Total Surplus)* At the competitive quantity, the buyer's value of the last unit equals its marginal cost.

Let $P^D(Y)$ and $P^S(Y)$ be inverse market demand and supply. Ignoring fixed costs, total surplus from trading $Y$ units is $W(Y) = \int_0^Y \big(P^D(q) - P^S(q)\big)\,dq$, so $W'(Y) = P^D(Y) - P^S(Y)$. This is positive below the equilibrium quantity, zero at it, and negative above it. A policy that moves quantity away from $Y^*$ loses the area between the curves over the units no longer traded. This is the deadweight loss of [[6.8.6 Welfare Applications]]. The formula assumes that the units go to the buyers with the highest values and come from the lowest-cost firms. A market price ensures this. Rent control does not.

#### *(Ordinary Demand Approximates)* For a normal good, the area under ordinary demand lies between the Hicksian areas at the old and the new utility.

The measure depends on which $u$ is held fixed. At the old utility $u_0$ it is the money that could be taken from her after a price fall, leaving her as well off as before (the **compensating variation**). At the new utility $u_1$ it is the money she would need at the old price to be as well off as after (the **equivalent variation**). At each price, ordinary demand equals $h(p, v(p, M))$, where the indirect utility $v(p, M)$ lies between $u_0$ and $u_1$. A normal good has $h$ rising in $u$, so the ordinary curve lies between the two Hicksian curves. By the [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]], the three curves coincide when the good has no income effect, as with quasilinear utility. The gaps are small when the good takes a small share of spending. The figure shows the Hard example.

# Derivation

We want both formulas in the callout. Each uses $f(x) = f(0) + \int_0^x f'(s)\,ds$. For the firm, apply it to revenue and to cost:
$$\pi(p, y_0) = \left[p \cdot 0 + p\int_0^{y_0} 1\,dy\right] - \left[c(0) + \int_0^{y_0} c'_y(y)\,dy\right] = \int_0^{y_0}\big(p - c'_y(y)\big)\,dy - c(0).$$
The rectangle form follows from factoring $y_0$ out of $py_0 - c(y_0)$. For the consumer, apply the same identity to $e(\cdot, u)$ at $p_0$ and at $p_1$:
$$e(p_0, u) - e(p_1, u) = \left[e(0, u) + \int_0^{p_0} \frac{\partial e(p, u)}{\partial p}\,dp\right] - \left[e(0, u) + \int_0^{p_1} \frac{\partial e(p, u)}{\partial p}\,dp\right] = \int_{p_1}^{p_0} \frac{\partial e(p, u)}{\partial p}\,dp.$$
The term $e(0, u)$ cancels. For the integrand, take the Lagrangian of expenditure minimization with the other price $p_2$ fixed, $\mathcal{L} = p\,x_1 + p_2x_2 - \lambda\,(u(x_1, x_2) - u)$. By the envelope theorem, $\partial e/\partial p$ equals $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial p = x_1$ at the optimum, which is $h(p, u)$. Substituting gives the callout formula.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Surplus in a Small Market
>Five identical firms each supply $y_j = 5p$, and ten identical consumers each demand $x_j = 10 - 5p$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the equilibrium price, consumer surplus, and producer surplus. Check that each total equals the sum of the individual surpluses.
>>Market supply is $Y^S = 25p$ and market demand is $X^D = 100 - 50p$. Setting them equal gives $75p = 100$, so $p^* = 4/3$ and $Y^* = 100/3 \approx 33.33$. Demand reaches zero at the choke price $\bar p = 2$. CS is a triangle with base $100/3$ and height $2 - 4/3 = 2/3$, so $CS = \frac12 \cdot \frac{100}{3} \cdot \frac23 = 11.11$. Supply starts at the origin, so PS is a triangle with height $4/3$, and $PS = \frac12 \cdot \frac{100}{3} \cdot \frac43 = 22.22$. Total surplus is $33.33$. Each consumer buys $10 - 5 \cdot \frac43 = 3.33$ units and gains $\frac12 \cdot 3.33 \cdot \frac23 = 1.11$. Ten consumers give $11.11$. Each firm sells $5 \cdot \frac43 = 6.67$ units and gains $\frac12 \cdot 6.67 \cdot \frac43 = 4.44$. Five firms give $22.22$. The supply $y_j = 5p$ comes from $c(y) = y^2/10$, with $MC = y/5$ and no fixed cost. Each firm's profit is $\frac43 \cdot 6.67 - 6.67^2/10 = 8.89 - 4.44 = 4.44$, equal to its area, as the zero fixed cost requires.

>[!example] (Medium) A Copy Shop With a Lease
>A copy shop pays a lease of $\$64$ per day whether or not it opens, so $c(0) = 64$. Its cost is $c(y) = 64 + y^2$, with $y$ in hundreds of pages, and the price is $p = 24$. A classmate reads the area between the price and MC off the graph and reports $PS = 144$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the output and the producer surplus with both pictures. Explain the gap.
>>$MC = 2y$, so $p = MC$ gives $y_0 = 12$. Average cost is $AC(12) = 64/12 + 12 = 17.33$. The rectangle is $12 \times (24 - 17.33) = 80$. The area between price and MC is $\int_0^{12}(24 - 2y)\,dy = 288 - 144 = 144$, and subtracting $c(0) = 64$ gives $80$. Both pictures agree, and $\pi = 288 - (64 + 144) = 80$. The classmate's $144$ is revenue minus variable cost, which is profit plus the lease. The lease does not change the choice of $y_0$, since it is paid at every output. It is still a cost, so producer surplus is $80$.

>[!example] (Hard) Cheaper Coffee
>A student spends $M = \$100$ a month on coffee $x_1$ and other goods $x_2$ (price $1$), with $u = x_1^{1/2}x_2^{1/2}$. A café cuts the coffee price from $p_0 = 4$ to $p_1 = 1$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the welfare measure at her old utility, and check its meaning directly. Then compute the area under her ordinary demand and the measure at her new utility, and explain the ranking.
>>At $p_0 = 4$ she buys $x_1 = 50/4 = 12.5$ and $x_2 = 50$, so $u_0 = \sqrt{12.5 \cdot 50} = 25$. With $\alpha = 1/2$ and $p_2 = 1$, [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]] gives $h(p, u) = u/\sqrt p$ and $e(p, u) = 2u\sqrt p$. The measure is
>>$$\int_1^4 \frac{25}{\sqrt p}\,dp = 25\,\big[2\sqrt p\,\big]_1^4 = 50 = e(4, 25) - e(1, 25) = 100 - 50.$$
>>After the price cut she reaches $u_0$ with $\$50$ instead of $\$100$. With $M = 50$ and $p_1 = 1$ she buys $25$ coffees and $25$ of the other good, and $\sqrt{25 \cdot 25} = 25$. The café could charge her a $\$50$ monthly fee and leave her as well off as before.
>>
>>Her ordinary demand is $x_1^* = 50/p$, so its area is $\int_1^4 \frac{50}{p}\,dp = 50\ln 4 = 69.31$. At $p_1 = 1$ she buys $50$ of each good, so $u_1 = 50$, and the measure at $u_1$ is $e(4, 50) - e(1, 50) = 200 - 100 = 100$. Then $50 < 69.31 < 100$. Coffee is a normal good, and the price cut raises her real income, so ordinary demand grows faster than demand at fixed $u_0$. The gaps are large because coffee takes half her budget and the price falls by $75\%$.
