>[!note] Stability
>Stability of an LTI system is governed by the eigenvalues of $A$.
>
>**Internal (zero-input) stability** concerns the homogeneous response $x(t) = e^{At} x(0)$. The system is:
>- **Asymptotically stable** iff every eigenvalue satisfies $\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0$ (continuous time) or $|\lambda_i| < 1$ (discrete time). Every initial condition decays to $0$.
>- **Marginally stable** iff every eigenvalue satisfies $\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_i) \leq 0$ (CT) or $|\lambda_i| \leq 1$ (DT), with *simple* (non-repeated) roots on the boundary. Trajectories stay bounded but do not decay.
>- **Unstable** iff any eigenvalue violates the above — or a boundary eigenvalue is repeated (defective), producing $t^k e^{\lambda t}$ or $i^k \lambda^i$ growth.
>
>**Bounded-input bounded-output (BIBO) stability** concerns the forced response $y(t)$: the system is BIBO stable iff every bounded input $u$ produces a bounded output $y$. For an LTI system with transfer function $G(s)$, BIBO stability is equivalent to all poles of $G(s)$ lying in the open left half-plane (CT) or the open unit disk (DT).
>
>Asymptotic stability $\Rightarrow$ BIBO stability. The converse fails when unstable internal modes are canceled by zeros in $G(s)$ (pole-zero cancellation) and therefore do not appear in the input-output map — but they are still present internally and can blow up via disturbances or initial conditions.
>
>The **stable region** is the open left half-plane for continuous time and the open unit disk for discrete time. This is the fundamental mapping: $\lambda \mapsto e^{\lambda T}$ sends the left half of the $\lambda$-plane to the interior of the unit disk, connecting the two criteria.
>
>>[!warning] Marginal Stability Is Fragile
>>A marginally stable system (e.g., purely imaginary eigenvalues, $|\lambda| = 1$) is on the boundary between stable and unstable. Any tiny perturbation — numerical roundoff, modeling error, unmodeled dynamics — can push it to either side. In practice, controllers require strict asymptotic stability with a safety margin ($\mathrm{Re}(\lambda) < -\epsilon$ for some $\epsilon > 0$), not just $\mathrm{Re}(\lambda) \leq 0$.

# Intuition
A stable LTI system is one whose natural response decays on its own — "flick it, and it settles back down." The eigenvalues of $A$ are the growth rates of its modes: $e^{\lambda_i t} v_i$. If every $\lambda_i$ has negative real part, every mode decays, and therefore every linear combination of modes decays too. One positive-real-part eigenvalue is enough to blow up the whole system, no matter how many other modes are stable.

The left half-plane / unit disk criterion reflects the two ways to grow. In continuous time, growth is exponential: $|e^{\lambda t}| = e^{\mathrm{Re}(\lambda) t}$ decays iff $\mathrm{Re}(\lambda) < 0$. In discrete time, growth is power: $|\lambda^i|$ decays iff $|\lambda| < 1$. Sampling a CT system at rate $T$ turns eigenvalues $\lambda$ into $e^{\lambda T}$ — and $\mathrm{Re}(\lambda) < 0$ is exactly $|e^{\lambda T}| < 1$. The two pictures are the same, viewed through different time representations.

BIBO stability asks a different question: can a bounded input drive the output unbounded? Even an unstable system ($A$ with a positive eigenvalue) might be BIBO stable if that bad mode isn't reachable from $u$ or visible from $y$ — but that relies on exact cancellation of a pole by a zero, which is never exact in practice. The safe engineering standard is asymptotic (internal) stability, which implies BIBO and is robust.

# Properties

## Eigenvalue Criterion

#### *(CT asymptotic stability)* Every eigenvalue in the open left half-plane: $\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0$ for all $i$.

This is both necessary and sufficient. Each mode contributes $e^{\lambda_i t}$; the full response is a finite sum of these. The sum decays to $0$ iff every summand does. Strict inequality is needed because $\lambda = 0$ gives a constant mode $e^{0 \cdot t} = 1$ that never decays.

#### *(DT asymptotic stability)* Every eigenvalue strictly inside the unit disk: $|\lambda_i| < 1$ for all $i$.

The discrete-time analog. Modes evolve as $\lambda^i$; decay requires $|\lambda| < 1$. Eigenvalues on the unit circle give persistent oscillation ($\lambda^i$ circles around the unit circle without shrinking); eigenvalues outside give exponential growth.

#### *(CT-DT mapping)* Sampling sends $\lambda \mapsto e^{\lambda T}$: LHP maps to the open unit disk.

If a CT system with eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ is sampled at interval $T$, the discrete-time dynamics has eigenvalues $e^{\lambda_i T}$. The mapping is: LHP $\to$ open unit disk, imaginary axis $\to$ unit circle, RHP $\to$ outside the disk. Stability classifications transfer: CT-stable systems remain DT-stable under sampling (though convergence rates change).

#### *(Marginal stability)* Boundary eigenvalues give bounded but non-decaying response, only if simple.

Pure imaginary eigenvalues $\pm i\omega$ (CT) or unit-circle eigenvalues $e^{\pm i\theta}$ (DT) give oscillations that never grow or shrink. Zero eigenvalue (CT) or $\lambda = 1$ (DT) give constant modes. But if a boundary eigenvalue is *repeated* and the $A$ matrix is defective (Jordan block), the mode grows as $t^k$ (CT) or $i^k$ (DT) — that is *unstable*, not marginal.

## Internal vs BIBO

#### *(Internal $\Rightarrow$ BIBO)* Asymptotic internal stability implies BIBO stability.

If every internal mode decays, the state response to any bounded input stays bounded, and so does the output $y = Cx + Du$. The impulse response $h(t) = Ce^{At}B$ decays exponentially, so $\int_0^\infty |h(\tau)| d\tau < \infty$, which is the standard BIBO condition.

#### *(BIBO $\not\Rightarrow$ internal)* BIBO stability does not imply internal stability.

If an unstable internal mode is uncontrollable (not reachable from $u$) or unobservable (not seen in $y$), it does not appear in the transfer function $G(s) = C(sI - A)^{-1}B + D$ — it is canceled by a zero. The system looks stable from input-output measurements but still has a hidden divergent mode. Disturbances or nonzero initial conditions can excite it.

#### *(Transfer function poles)* BIBO stability iff all poles of $G(s)$ are in the open LHP (CT) / open unit disk (DT).

The poles of $G(s)$ are a subset of the eigenvalues of $A$ — specifically, the eigenvalues of the controllable-observable part. Checking pole locations is the standard frequency-domain stability test. Tools: Routh–Hurwitz criterion (algebraic test on polynomial coefficients), Nyquist criterion (contour integral on the frequency response), root locus (pole locations as a parameter varies).

## Robustness

#### *(Stability margin)* Distance of the nearest eigenvalue from the stability boundary quantifies robustness.

$\min_i |\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_i)|$ (CT) or $1 - \max_i |\lambda_i|$ (DT) measures how much perturbation the system can tolerate before becoming unstable. Control design often aims for a target margin, not just for stability.

#### *(Parameter sensitivity)* Eigenvalues move continuously in the matrix entries; stability can be lost under perturbation.

Small changes to $A$ cause small changes to eigenvalues. A system with eigenvalues near the boundary can flip from stable to unstable under unmodeled dynamics or parameter drift. This motivates robust control techniques ($H_\infty$, $\mu$-synthesis) which design for a family of plants rather than a single model.

#### *(Nonlinear implications)* Linearization stability $\Rightarrow$ local nonlinear stability (Lyapunov's first method).

If the linearization of $\dot{x} = f(x)$ at an equilibrium $x_0$ has all eigenvalues in the open LHP, then $x_0$ is a locally asymptotically stable equilibrium of the nonlinear system. If even one eigenvalue is in the open RHP, $x_0$ is unstable. Eigenvalues on the imaginary axis are inconclusive — higher-order terms decide. See [[7.2. Linearization]].

# Examples
>[!example] Stability from Eigenvalues
>Is $A = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 0 \\ 3 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ asymptotically stable?
>>[!example]- Solution
>>Lower-triangular, so eigenvalues are the diagonal entries: $\lambda = -2, -1$. Both in the open LHP.
>>
>>**Asymptotically stable.** Stability margin $|\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_\text{closest})| = 1$.

>[!example] Marginal Stability: Undamped Oscillator
>Is $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ stable?
>>[!example]- Solution
>>Characteristic polynomial $\lambda^2 + 1 = 0$, eigenvalues $\pm i$. Pure imaginary, simple, on the boundary.
>>
>>**Marginally stable.** Trajectories are closed orbits (see [[7.4. Phase Portraits|center]]); bounded but not decaying. Any dissipation (however small) moves eigenvalues into the LHP and makes the system asymptotically stable; any energy injection moves them into the RHP.

>[!example] Discrete-Time Stability
>Is the discrete-time system with $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & -0.2 \end{bmatrix}$ stable?
>>[!example]- Solution
>>$\det(\lambda I - A) = \lambda^2 - 0.3 \lambda - (0.1 + 0.12) = \lambda^2 - 0.3\lambda - 0.22 = 0$.
>>
>>$\lambda = \frac{0.3 \pm \sqrt{0.09 + 0.88}}{2} = \frac{0.3 \pm 0.985}{2}$, so $\lambda_1 \approx 0.643$, $\lambda_2 \approx -0.343$.
>>
>>Both $|\lambda_i| < 1$. **Asymptotically stable.** Stability margin $1 - \max |\lambda_i| \approx 0.357$.

>[!example] Defective Repeated Eigenvalue
>Is $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ stable?
>>[!example]- Solution
>>Both eigenvalues are $0$, both on the boundary. But $A$ has only one eigenvector $(1, 0)^T$ — it is a defective Jordan block.
>>
>>Solution: $e^{At} = I + At = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Response to $x(0) = (0, 1)^T$ is $(t, 1)^T$ — unbounded.
>>
>>**Unstable.** The repeated boundary eigenvalue with defective $A$ produces polynomial growth even though $|\lambda| = 0$.
