>[!note] Electric Dipole
>An **electric dipole** is a pair of charges $+q$ and $-q$ separated by a distance $d$. Its **dipole moment** is the vector
>$$\vec p = q\vec d,$$
>where $\vec d$ points from the negative charge to the positive charge and has length $d$. The unit of $p$ is the coulomb-meter, $\text{C}\cdot\text{m}$. A **permanent dipole** has a fixed separation, as in a water molecule. An **induced dipole** forms when a field pulls the charges of a neutral object apart, as in [[3.1.6. Induced Charge]].
>
>On the perpendicular bisector, at distance $x$ from the center, the field points opposite to $\vec p$ with size
>$$E = \frac{kqd}{\left(x^2 + d^2/4\right)^{3/2}} \approx \frac{kp}{x^3} \quad (x \gg d).$$
>Far from a dipole, its field falls off as $1/r^3$ in every direction. That is faster than the $1/r^2$ of a single charge.
>
>In a **uniform** external field $\vec E$, the two charges feel equal and opposite forces. The net force on the dipole is zero. The forces still make a **torque** about the center,
>$$\tau = pE\sin\theta, \qquad \vec\tau = \vec p \times \vec E,$$
>where $\theta$ is the angle from $\vec E$ to $\vec p$. The torque turns $\vec p$ toward $\vec E$. The dipole's potential energy in the field is
>$$U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E = -pE\cos\theta.$$
>It is lowest, $-pE$, when $\vec p$ points along $\vec E$, and highest, $+pE$, when $\vec p$ points opposite to $\vec E$. In a **nonuniform** field the two forces no longer cancel, and the dipole also feels a net force.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture stated $U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E$ without proof. The note derives it below from the torque, using the work done by a torque from [[1.5.3. Torque|rotational mechanics]].

# Intuition

Up close, a dipole is two separate charges, and their fields are easy to tell apart. From far away, the two charges are too close together to tell apart. The object then looks like what it is overall, a neutral thing. A neutral object has no $1/r^2$ field. What survives is a small leftover from the charges sitting at slightly different places, and it fades as $1/r^3$. A single charge has a net charge at every distance, which is why its field fades more slowly.

A dipole is not spherical. It has a direction, from its negative end to its positive end. Its field depends on where a point sits relative to that direction. The dipole moment vector records both the strength and the direction. Most of the human body is made of permanent dipoles, mainly water molecules.

In a uniform field, the positive end is pushed one way and the negative end the other, with equal forces. The dipole's center does not accelerate. The dipole twists instead. The torque is zero when the dipole points along the field. That aligned position has the lowest energy, and the dipole swings back to it when disturbed.

The animation releases a dipole at $75^\circ$ from a uniform field. The equal and opposite forces twist it back and forth through alignment. The dot on the graph of $U = -pE\cos\theta$ swings through the minimum at $\theta = 0$.

# Derivation

Put $+q$ at $(0, d/2)$ and $-q$ at $(0, -d/2)$, so $\vec p$ points along $+y$. Take a point P at $(x, 0)$ on the bisector. Both charges are a distance $\sqrt{x^2 + d^2/4}$ from P, so both fields have the size
$$E_+ = E_- = \frac{kq}{x^2 + d^2/4}.$$
The field of $+q$ points away from it, down and to the right. The field of $-q$ points toward it, down and to the left. Reflecting across the bisector swaps $+q$ and $-q$. The components along the bisector cancel, and the components perpendicular to it add. Let $\alpha$ be the angle between each field and the bisector. Each perpendicular component equals $E_+\sin\alpha$, with $\sin\alpha = (d/2)/\sqrt{x^2 + d^2/4}$:
$$E_y = -2\,\frac{kq}{x^2 + d^2/4}\cdot\frac{d/2}{\sqrt{x^2 + d^2/4}} = -\frac{kqd}{\left(x^2 + d^2/4\right)^{3/2}}.$$
For $x \gg d$, drop $d^2/4$ next to $x^2$, which gives $E_y \approx -kp/x^3$.

For the torque, place the dipole in a uniform field $\vec E$ at angle $\theta$ to it. The force $qE$ on $+q$ and the force $qE$ on $-q$ point in opposite directions, so they sum to zero. Each acts a distance $d/2$ from the center, at angle $\theta$ to the line of the dipole. Both turn it the same way. The total torque is
$$\tau = 2\cdot qE\cdot\frac{d}{2}\sin\theta = pE\sin\theta.$$
Turning the dipole from $\theta$ to $\theta + d\theta$ against this torque takes work $pE\sin\theta\,d\theta$. That work is stored as potential energy, so $dU = pE\sin\theta\,d\theta$ and $U = -pE\cos\theta$. The constant is chosen so that $U = 0$ at $\theta = 90^\circ$.

# Properties

#### *(Why $1/r^3$)* The dipole's field falls off faster than a point charge's because its net charge is zero.

A point charge with $Q \neq 0$ has a field that falls as $1/r^2$ at every distance. A dipole's two fields almost cancel far away, and only their difference survives. That difference comes from the charges sitting a distance $d$ apart. It equals roughly $d/r$ times a single charge's field, giving $kqd/r^3 = kp/r^3$. Doubling the distance cuts a dipole's field to an eighth. This is why the fields of neutral molecules reach so short a distance.

#### *(Direction Matters)* A dipole has symmetry only about its own axis, so its field depends on the direction to the point.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture computed the field only on the bisector. The on-axis value $2kp/r^3$ follows Giancoli section 21.11.

A point charge looks the same from every direction. A dipole looks the same only after rotations about the line through its two charges. On the bisector, the far field points opposite to $\vec p$ with size $kp/r^3$. On the axis, beyond the positive charge, it points along $\vec p$ with twice that size, $2kp/r^3$. At every distance the field depends on the direction from the dipole. That is why $\vec p$ must be a vector.

#### *(Stable and Unstable Alignment)* The aligned position is stable, and the anti-aligned position is unstable.

At $\theta = 0$ the torque is zero and $U$ has its minimum, $-pE$. A small twist creates a torque that turns the dipole back, so it oscillates about the field direction. At $\theta = 180^\circ$ the torque is also zero, but $U$ has its maximum, $+pE$. A small twist creates a torque that turns the dipole farther away. Flipping a dipole from aligned to anti-aligned takes the work $2pE$.

#### *(Net Force in a Nonuniform Field)* When the field differs at the two ends, the dipole is pushed as a whole as well as twisted.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture said only that a net force appears. The direction below follows from superposition.

In a nonuniform field the two ends sit in fields of different strength. Near a positive charge, an aligned dipole has its negative end closer, where the field is stronger. The pull on that end is larger than the push on the far end. The dipole therefore moves toward the stronger field. This is the force that pulls an induced dipole, and the neutral object carrying it, toward a charged rod.

#### *(Take the Dipole Apart)* To analyze a dipole, replace it by its two charges, solve, and then write the answer with $p$.

The torque and energy formulas came from this procedure. Put $+q$ and $-q$ a distance $d$ apart, find the force on each from $\vec F = q\vec E$, and combine. Only the product $qd$ survives in the final answer, so it can be written with $p$. A molecule is so small that the field barely changes across it. The formulas $\vec\tau = \vec p \times \vec E$ and $U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E$ therefore hold even in a nonuniform field, with $\vec E$ taken at the dipole's center.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Torque on a Water Molecule
>A water molecule has a dipole moment of $6.2 \times 10^{-30}\ \text{C}\cdot\text{m}$. It sits in a uniform field of $5.0 \times 10^{5}\ \text{N/C}$, with its dipole moment perpendicular to the field.
>
>>[!example]- Find the torque on the molecule and the net force on it.
>>At $\theta = 90^\circ$,
>>$$\tau = pE\sin 90^\circ = (6.2 \times 10^{-30})(5.0 \times 10^{5}) = 3.1 \times 10^{-24}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}.$$
>>The field is uniform, so the net force is zero. The torque turns the molecule toward alignment with the field.

>[!example] (Medium) How Good Is $kp/x^3$?
>A dipole has charges $\pm 2.0\ \text{nC}$ separated by $1.0\ \text{cm}$. Point P is $10\ \text{cm}$ from the center on the bisector.
>
>>[!example]- Compare the exact field at P with the far-field approximation.
>>The dipole moment is $p = (2.0 \times 10^{-9})(0.010) = 2.0 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{C}\cdot\text{m}$. The exact field is
>>$$E = \frac{kqd}{\left(x^2 + d^2/4\right)^{3/2}} = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(2.0 \times 10^{-11})}{(0.010025)^{3/2}} = 179.1\ \text{N/C}.$$
>>The approximation gives $kp/x^3 = 0.1798/(0.10)^3 = 179.8\ \text{N/C}$. At ten times the separation, the two differ by $0.4\%$. The field points opposite to $\vec p$.

>[!example] (Hard) Can a Field Line Up Water?
>Use the water molecule and field from the Easy example. At room temperature, $T = 300\ \text{K}$, each molecule has a random thermal energy of order $k_B T$, with $k_B = 1.381 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}$ the Boltzmann constant.
>
>>[!example]- Compare the work to flip a molecule from aligned to anti-aligned with $k_B T$. Will the field line up the molecules?
>>The work to flip is the difference in potential energy:
>>$$\Delta U = (+pE) - (-pE) = 2pE = 2(6.2 \times 10^{-30})(5.0 \times 10^{5}) = 6.2 \times 10^{-24}\ \text{J}.$$
>>The thermal energy is
>>$$k_B T = (1.381 \times 10^{-23})(300) = 4.1 \times 10^{-21}\ \text{J},$$
>>about $670$ times larger. Collisions knock the molecules around far more strongly than the field can align them. The field only slightly favors the aligned direction.
