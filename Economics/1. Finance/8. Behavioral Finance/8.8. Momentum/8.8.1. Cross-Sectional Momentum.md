>[!note] Cross-Sectional Momentum
>**Cross-sectional momentum** (Jegadeesh & Titman, 1993) is a zero-cost long-short equity strategy that ranks all stocks in a universe by their *past* $J$-month return, buys the top decile of "winners," shorts the bottom decile of "losers," and holds the resulting portfolio for $K$ months. For $J, K \in \{3, 6, 9, 12\}^2$ all sixteen combinations have historically delivered significant positive average returns.
>
>For each formation date $t$, the strategy ranks the universe on cumulative return over $[t-J,\, t]$, forms equal-weight decile portfolios $D_1, \dots, D_{10}$, and trades the long-short **Winners-Minus-Losers (WML)** portfolio
>$$\text{WML}_{t,K} \;=\; \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bigl(r_{D_{10},\,t+k} \,-\, r_{D_1,\,t+k}\bigr).$$
>
>Headline empirical results (1965–1989 NYSE/AMEX):
>- WML earns approximately $1\%$ per month ($\approx 12\%$ annualized) with $t$-statistics above $3$ (i.e., high statistical significance) for the standard $(J,K)=(6,6)$ specification.
>- The [[4.3c Capital Asset Pricing Model|CAPM]] beta of WML is $\beta_{\text{WML}} \approx -0.08$, so the strategy is *not* compensated for market risk; if anything it tilts slightly against the market.
>- Continuation persists over horizons of three to twelve months and reverses over months $13$–$31$ ([[De Bondt & Thaler 1985|De Bondt & Thaler, 1985]] long-term reversal).
>- The result has been replicated internationally (Rouwenhorst, 1998), across asset classes (Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen, 2013), and is itself one of the four [[Carhart Four-Factor Model|Carhart]] factors (UMD).
>
>Cross-sectional momentum compares assets *against each other* — its signal is *relative*, so a stock that fell less than its peers in a falling market still counts as a "winner." This is its defining contrast with [[8.8.2. Time-Series Momentum|time-series momentum]], which compares each asset against its own past. Together with [[8.8.3. Momentum Crashes|momentum crashes]], these three notes form the empirical core of the [[8.8. Momentum|momentum]] section. Cross-sectional momentum is widely regarded as the single strongest piece of evidence against the [[8.3. Challenges to Rational Pricing|efficient markets hypothesis]].

# Intuition

Stocks that have beaten their peers over the past several months tend to keep beating them for several more, then reverse. The standard story (see the [[8.9. Reaction Models|over- and under-reaction models]]) has two stages. First, investors are slow to absorb news — from [[8.6.1. Conservatism|conservatism]], slow information spread, or inattention — so prices drift in the direction of the news for months instead of jumping right away. Second, late buyers pile in and push prices past what the news justified; the overshoot corrects over the following years. Both halves are needed: drift without overshoot would not reverse, and overshoot without drift would not continue.

"Cross-sectional" means the rank is against *the rest of the universe*, not an absolute benchmark — in a market that fell $15\%$, a stock that fell only $5\%$ counts as a winner. The long and short legs cancel out market exposure by design, which is why the average market beta is near zero. That flat average hides a beta that swings sharply with the market state and drives [[8.8.3. Momentum Crashes|momentum crashes]].

# Properties

#### *(Overlapping Portfolios)* The practical implementation runs $K$ overlapping portfolios in parallel, one formed each month.

Each month one of the $K$ active portfolios is liquidated and a new one is formed; the reported strategy return is the average across the $K$ currently-held cohorts. This staggering smooths out rebalancing noise and lets the strategy be reported as a monthly time series even though each cohort is held for $K$ months. Deciles are equal-weighted within each leg, which gives small caps a disproportionate influence — value-weighting WML cuts the headline number roughly in half but leaves the alpha statistically significant.

#### *(Returns Decay Across the $(J,K)$ Grid)* Longer look-backs and shorter holds give the largest gross returns; the cell-by-cell pattern reveals smooth signal decay.

The $(12,1)$ corner of the grid produces the largest gross monthly return because a long look-back collects more signal and a short hold re-ups the signal frequently. The $(3,12)$ corner produces the smallest because it reverses both choices. The smooth monotonic pattern across the $4 \times 4$ grid is itself a robustness check: if any single $(J,K)$ cell were a fluke, neighboring cells would not also be positive. After realistic transaction costs the workhorse $(6,6)$ or $(12,6)$ implementations dominate, which is why those are the cells most cited.

#### *(Conditional-Risk Models Also Fail)* Even risk-factor models that allow loadings to vary with the business cycle cannot rationalize the WML alpha.

Static factor regressions — Fama-French three-factor, Carhart four-factor — are the first line of defense and they fail. The deeper rational-pricing defense lets risk loadings vary across states (so that a strategy's true risk only shows up in bad times). These conditional and ICAPM-style models have also been fitted to WML and also fail to absorb the alpha. Two decades of attempts to find a hidden risk story that justifies momentum have produced none, which is what makes momentum the central piece of evidence against the [[8.3. Challenges to Rational Pricing|efficient markets hypothesis]] rather than just another anomaly.

#### *(Tail Risk Hidden in the Mean)* The $\approx 1\%$/month average is generated by many small wins punctuated by rare $-50\%$ to $-75\%$ monthly losses.

The mechanism — covered in [[8.8.3. Momentum Crashes|momentum crashes]] — is that the loser leg in a bear market fills with high-beta survivors that explode on a rebound, so the strategy's conditional beta swings from $-0.08$ on average to $-2$ or more in panic states. A practitioner sizing WML from its unconditional Sharpe ratio of $\approx 0.5$ will be undercapitalized for the next crash month. The right way to read the WML mean is *not* as a steady cash flow but as a long stretch of small gains plus a small handful of catastrophic months whose timing is partly predictable from market state.

# Examples

>[!example]- A $(6,6)$ strategy walk-through
>Suppose at the start of January $t$, you rank the 1,000 largest U.S. stocks by their July–December cumulative return (the look-back $J=6$). You go long the top 100 (the winner decile) equal-weighted and short the bottom 100 (the loser decile) equal-weighted. You hold this portfolio for six months — through end of June.
>
>To smooth rebalancing you actually run six overlapping portfolios in parallel: one formed in January (held Jan–Jun), one formed in February (held Feb–Jul), and so on. Each month one of the six portfolios is liquidated and a new one is formed. The reported strategy return for month $t$ is the average return of the six currently-active overlapping portfolios.
>
>Over the 1965–1989 sample, this $(6,6)$ implementation returned approximately $0.95\%$ per month with a $t$-statistic above $3$. The CAPM beta of the strategy was approximately $-0.08$, so the positive return cannot be rationalized as compensation for market exposure under standard asset pricing.

>[!example]- Why $(12, 1)$ wins more than $(3, 12)$
>Two strategies built from the same data behave very differently. The $(12,1)$ strategy uses the longest look-back ($12$ months) and the shortest hold ($1$ month) — it captures a maximally informative signal and turns over every month. The $(3,12)$ strategy uses a short, noisy look-back and holds the resulting (potentially stale) signal for a full year.
>
>In the data, $(12,1)$ delivers higher gross average returns than $(3,12)$, consistent with the picture that the underlying alpha decays smoothly over the holding period: longer look-backs collect more signal, and shorter holds re-up that signal more frequently. The cost of $(12,1)$ is transaction costs, which is why practitioners more often run $(6,6)$ or $(12,6)$ as the workhorse parameterization. The pattern of returns across the $4 \times 4$ grid of $(J,K)$ combinations is itself a property of the data — every cell is positive, and the cells with longer look-backs and shorter holds are larger.

>[!example]- The De Bondt-Thaler reversal companion
>Take the same winner and loser deciles from the $(12,K)$ strategy, but instead of holding for $K \le 12$ months, hold for *months 13 through 60*. The sign of the strategy flips. Past winners now *underperform* past losers by approximately $25\%$ cumulative over the subsequent four years (De Bondt & Thaler, 1985).
>
>This is not a contradiction — it is the same data viewed at a different horizon. The cognitive story is that the initial underreaction-driven drift over months $1$–$12$ eventually overshoots fundamentals as late traders extrapolate, and the overshoot is then corrected over months $13$–$60$. Continuation and reversal are two halves of one trajectory, and any model that explains one must explain the other. This is exactly the empirical target of [[8.9.1. Barberis-Shleifer-Vishny Model|BSV]].
