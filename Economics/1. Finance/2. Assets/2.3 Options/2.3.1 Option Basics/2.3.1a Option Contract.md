>[!note] Option Contract
>An **option** gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to trade an underlying asset at a fixed price $K$ on or before an expiry date $T$. The price $K$ is called the strike price or exercise price. A **call option** is the right to buy, and a **put option** is the right to sell. The buyer (the holder, or long side) pays a price called the **premium** today to the seller (the writer, or short side). A **European option** can be exercised only at $T$. An **American option** can be exercised at any time $t$ with $0 < t \le T$. Let $S_T$ be the asset price at expiry. The holder exercises only when exercise pays, so the payoffs at $T$ are
>$$\text{call: } \max(S_T - K, 0), \qquad \text{put: } \max(K - S_T, 0).$$
>Calls and puts with these payoffs are called **vanilla options**. The writer receives the negative of each payoff.
>
>Let $S$ be the asset price today and $r$ the continuously compounded risk-free rate. The **intrinsic value** (IV) compares today's asset price with the [[1.2c Present Value|present value]] of the strike,
>$$\text{IV}_{\text{call}} = \max(S - Ke^{-rT}, 0), \qquad \text{IV}_{\text{put}} = \max(Ke^{-rT} - S, 0).$$
>The strike is discounted because the holder pays or receives it only at $T$. The **moneyness** of an option sorts it by this comparison. An option is in the money (ITM) when $\text{IV} > 0$ and at the money (ATM) when $S = Ke^{-rT}$. It is out of the money (OTM) when $S < Ke^{-rT}$ for a call and $S > Ke^{-rT}$ for a put. The **time value** (TV) is the rest of the premium, $\text{TV} = \text{premium} - \text{IV}$.
>
>An option is a [[2.2.1 Contingent Claim|contingent claim]] whose payoff bends at $K$, while a [[2.2.2a Forward Contract|forward]] has a straight-line payoff. [[2.3.1b Option Price Bounds]] limits option prices without any model, and [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity]] links calls and puts with the same $K$ and $T$.

# Intuition

A forward fixes the trade price for both sides. The buyer gains when the price rises and loses the same amount when it falls. Many investors want only one of these outcomes. A pension fund that must sell part of its index holdings next quarter wants protection against a fall. It also wants to keep any rise. A put on the index does both. The fund pays a premium today, and its sale price can no longer fall below $K$. An option keeps the gains of one side of a forward and removes its losses. The premium is the price of removing the losses.

The idea is old. Aristotle's *Politics* tells how Thales of Miletus expected a large olive harvest. In winter he paid small deposits to hire every olive press in Miletus and Chios. When the harvest came, demand for presses was high, and he rented them out on his own terms. His small deposits gave him the right to use the presses, and the large harvest made that right valuable.

# Properties

#### *(Discounted Strike)* With the strike discounted, a European option on a stock with no dividends never has negative time value.

>[!warning] Other Conventions for Intrinsic Value
>Many textbooks define intrinsic value as $\max(S - K, 0)$ for a call and $\max(K - S, 0)$ for a put, and set moneyness by comparing $S$ with $K$. Some use $Se^{rT} - K$ for a call. Check which convention a problem uses before classifying an option.

Let $c$ and $p$ be the prices of a European call and put. The [[2.3.1b Option Price Bounds|lower bounds]] $c \ge S - Ke^{-rT}$ and $p \ge Ke^{-rT} - S$ hold on a stock with no dividends, and option prices are never negative. So every European premium is at least its intrinsic value, and its time value is at least zero. The undiscounted convention breaks this for puts. A deep in-the-money European put can trade below $K - S$, because its holder must wait until $T$ to receive $K$. Its time value under that convention is negative. The forward-based convention gives the same moneyness as the discounted strike, since $Se^{rT} - K = e^{rT}(S - Ke^{-rT})$. Only the size of the intrinsic value changes.

#### *(Long and Short)* A holder can lose at most the premium, while a writer can lose much more than the premium received.

Ignore interest on the premium. A long call costs $c$ and gains without limit as $S_T$ rises. A long put costs $p$ and gains at most $K - p$, at $S_T = 0$. The writer's results are the reverse. A short call keeps at most $c$ and can lose without limit. A short put keeps at most $p$ and can lose up to $K - p$. Exchanges therefore require writers to hold a margin account, which holds collateral against these losses. Holders pay the premium up front and need no margin. Long calls and short puts gain when the price rises, so they form the bullish side of the market. Long puts and short calls form the bearish side.

#### *(Leverage and Notional)* The premium is small next to the position notional, so option returns magnify the move of the asset.

The **position notional** is the strike value of all the units the contracts cover,
$$\text{notional} = \text{number of contracts} \times \text{contract size} \times K.$$
The contract size is the number of units of the asset in one contract. The notional is the cash that would change hands if every contract were exercised. Ten US equity call contracts with strike $\$150$ have a notional of $10 \times 100 \times 150 = \$150{,}000$. If each call costs $\$4$, the premium is $10 \times 100 \times 4 = \$4{,}000$, under $3\%$ of the notional. A small move in the stock is then a large move relative to the premium. The holder also loses $100\%$ of the premium whenever the option expires out of the money. The (Medium) example below shows both effects.

#### *(Listed Contracts)* Exchanges fix the expiries, contract sizes, and strikes, so listed options come in standard forms.

In the US, one equity option contract covers 100 shares. Most listed options on single stocks are American, and most listed options on stock indices are European. Listed options generally have no dividend protection. The strike is not adjusted when the stock pays a cash dividend. A call holder therefore bears the price drop on the ex-dividend date, the first day the stock trades without the right to the next dividend. LEAPS are listed options with more than one year to expiry. FLEX options are exchange-traded options on stocks or stock indices with nonstandard strikes and expiries. Most FLEX options are short-dated options on very liquid assets.

Zero-days-to-expiration (0DTE) options expire on the day they trade, and they have grown popular in recent years. Five underlyings lead this market: the SPX and NDX indices and the SPY, QQQ, and IWM exchange-traded funds (ETFs). Traders favor the SPX and NDX options. They are European and settle in cash after the close, so no shares change hands. An index carries less risk from news about one company. US tax rules also treat gains on these index options as $60\%$ long-term and $40\%$ short-term, whatever the holding period.

#### *(Limits)* Exchanges cap the number of contracts one entity may hold or exercise on one side of the market.

A **position limit** is the maximum number of contracts an entity can hold or write on the same side of the market for one underlying. An **exercise limit** is the maximum number it can exercise within a set period, usually five consecutive business days. Both limits add up every position on one side. A trader with 6,000 long calls and 5,000 short puts on one stock counts 11,000 contracts on the bullish side. The two sides do not net, so 5,000 long puts would not reduce that count. The detailed rules are complex, especially for firms that trade for many clients.

#### *(Uses)* Investors buy options to hedge or to speculate, and write options to collect premium.

A pension fund that expects a cash inflow can buy calls on an index. The calls cap the price it pays when the cash arrives and it invests. A fund that expects a cash outflow can buy index puts to set a floor on its sale price. Deep out-of-the-money puts are cheap and protect a portfolio against a market crash. Speculators buy calls or puts as a leveraged bet on direction. An investor who owns a stock can write calls on it, a position called a **covered call**. The investor collects premium and gives up any gain above $K$. An investor who wants to buy a stock below today's price can write puts. If the stock ends below $K$, the investor buys it at $K$ and keeps the premium.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Sorting Four Options
>A stock trades at $\$80$ and pays no dividends. The risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding. Four six-month European options are quoted: a call with strike $\$70$ at $\$13.54$, a call with strike $\$80$ at $\$7.51$, a put with strike $\$80$ at $\$5.93$, and a put with strike $\$90$ at $\$11.95$.
>
>>[!example]- Classify each option as ITM, ATM, or OTM, and split each premium into intrinsic value and time value.
>>Discount each strike by $e^{-0.04 \times 0.5} = e^{-0.02}$. The discounted strikes are $70e^{-0.02} = \$68.61$, $80e^{-0.02} = \$78.42$, and $90e^{-0.02} = \$88.22$. Compare each with $S = \$80$.
>>
>>| Option | $Ke^{-rT}$ | Moneyness | IV | TV |
>>|---|---|---|---|---|
>>| Call, $K = 70$ | $68.61$ | ITM | $11.39$ | $2.15$ |
>>| Call, $K = 80$ | $78.42$ | ITM | $1.58$ | $5.93$ |
>>| Put, $K = 80$ | $78.42$ | OTM | $0$ | $5.93$ |
>>| Put, $K = 90$ | $88.22$ | ITM | $8.22$ | $3.73$ |
>>
>>The call with $K = S = \$80$ is slightly in the money, because its strike is paid only in six months. Under the undiscounted convention it would be at the money, with intrinsic value $0$ and time value $\$7.51$. That convention would also give the $\$90$ put an intrinsic value of $\$10$ and a time value of only $\$1.95$. The two options with strike $\$80$ have the largest time values, since they are closest to the money. Their time values are equal. [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity|Put-call parity]] gives $c - (S - Ke^{-rT}) = p$, so the time value of the in-the-money call equals the price of the out-of-the-money put.

>[!example] (Medium) Stock, Forward, or Call
>A stock trades at $\$50$ and pays no dividends. The risk-free rate is $5\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A trader compares three ways to bet on a rise over three months: buy one share, enter a long forward on one share, or buy one call with strike $\$52$ for $\$3.40$. Ignore interest on the cash paid today.
>
>>[!example]- Find the profit of each position at expiry for price moves of $-20\%$, $0\%$, $+5\%$, $+10\%$, $+20\%$, and $+40\%$, the return on the cash paid today, and the stock price at which the call breaks even.
>>The forward price is $F_0 = 50e^{0.05 \times 0.25} = \$50.63$. The share earns $S_T - 50$, the forward earns $S_T - 50.63$, and the call earns $\max(S_T - 52, 0) - 3.40$. The forward costs nothing today, so it has no percentage return.
>>
>>| Move | $S_T$ | Share | Forward | Call | Share return | Call return |
>>|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
>>| $-20\%$ | $40.00$ | $-10.00$ | $-10.63$ | $-3.40$ | $-20.0\%$ | $-100.0\%$ |
>>| $0\%$ | $50.00$ | $0.00$ | $-0.63$ | $-3.40$ | $0.0\%$ | $-100.0\%$ |
>>| $+5\%$ | $52.50$ | $2.50$ | $1.87$ | $-2.90$ | $5.0\%$ | $-85.3\%$ |
>>| $+10\%$ | $55.00$ | $5.00$ | $4.37$ | $-0.40$ | $10.0\%$ | $-11.8\%$ |
>>| $+20\%$ | $60.00$ | $10.00$ | $9.37$ | $4.60$ | $20.0\%$ | $135.3\%$ |
>>| $+40\%$ | $70.00$ | $20.00$ | $19.37$ | $14.60$ | $40.0\%$ | $429.4\%$ |
>>
>>The call breaks even at $S_T = 52 + 3.40 = \$55.40$, a rise of $10.8\%$. Below that the call loses money even when the stock rises. Above it the call return grows far faster than the share return. The call never loses more than $\$3.40$, while the share and the forward lose about $\$10$ in a $20\%$ fall. The forward trails the share by $\$0.63$ in every row. That gap is the interest on $\$50$ for three months, which the share buyer would also pay if interest were counted.

>[!example] (Hard) A Covered Call to Expiry
>An investor buys 300 shares of a stock at $\$48$ and writes 3 call contracts on it. Each contract covers 100 shares, has strike $\$52$, expires in three months, and pays a premium of $\$1.60$ per share. Ignore interest and dividends.
>
>>[!example]- Find the position notional, the net profit at expiry for $S_T = \$40, \$46, \$50, \$52, \$56, \$60$, the break-even price, the maximum profit, and the price above which the investor would have done better without the calls.
>>The position notional is $3 \times 100 \times 52 = \$15{,}600$, and the premium received is $300 \times 1.60 = \$480$. Per share, the profit at expiry is
>>$$(S_T - 48) + 1.60 - \max(S_T - 52, 0).$$
>>The first term is the stock gain, the second the premium kept, and the third the payoff owed on the calls. Multiply by 300 shares.
>>
>>| $S_T$ | Covered call | Stock alone |
>>|---|---|---|
>>| $40$ | $-\$1{,}920$ | $-\$2{,}400$ |
>>| $46$ | $-\$120$ | $-\$600$ |
>>| $50$ | $\$1{,}080$ | $\$600$ |
>>| $52$ | $\$1{,}680$ | $\$1{,}200$ |
>>| $56$ | $\$1{,}680$ | $\$2{,}400$ |
>>| $60$ | $\$1{,}680$ | $\$3{,}600$ |
>>
>>Below the strike the profit is $S_T - 46.40$ per share, so the break-even price is $\$46.40$. At or above the strike the calls are exercised, the shares are sold at $\$52$, and the profit stays at $300 \times (52 - 48 + 1.60) = \$1{,}680$. The stock alone earns more once $S_T - 48 > 5.60$, that is above $S_T = \$53.60$. The investor has traded every gain above $\$52$ for a premium of $\$1.60$ per share.
>>
>>The covered call pays $\min(S_T, 52)$ per share at expiry. Writing a put with strike $\$52$ and holding a bond that pays $\$52$ per share at expiry gives the same payoff, $52 - \max(52 - S_T, 0)$. If the options are European, [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity]] makes the two positions cost the same today.
