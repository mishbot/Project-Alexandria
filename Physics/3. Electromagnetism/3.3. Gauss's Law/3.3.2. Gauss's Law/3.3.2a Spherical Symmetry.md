>[!note] Spherical Symmetry
>A charge distribution has **spherical symmetry** when its density depends only on the distance $r$ from a center. The field then points radially, $\vec E = E(r)\,\hat r$. A positive $E(r)$ points outward, and a negative one points inward. The Gaussian surface is a sphere of radius $r$ with the same center. [[3.3.2. Gauss's Law|Gauss's law]] gives
>$$E(r)\,4\pi r^2 = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}(r)}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad E(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q_{\text{enc}}(r)}{r^2},$$
>where $Q_{\text{enc}}(r)$ is the charge within radius $r$. The standard cases:
>- A **point charge** $Q$ gives $E = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$ at every $r$.
>- A **thin spherical shell** of radius $R$, with charge $Q$ spread evenly, gives $E = 0$ inside and $E = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$ outside.
>- A ball whose density $\rho(r)$ depends on $r$ has $Q_{\text{enc}}(r) = \int_0^r \rho(r')\,4\pi r'^2\,dr'$. Each thin layer of radius $r'$ and thickness $dr'$ has volume $4\pi r'^2\,dr'$.
>
>A **uniformly charged ball** is an insulating ball of radius $a$ with constant volume density $\rho$. Its total charge is $Q = \tfrac43\pi a^3\rho$. Its field is
>$$E = \frac{\rho r}{3\varepsilon_0} = \frac{Q\,r}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^3} \quad (r \le a), \qquad E = \frac{\rho a^3}{3\varepsilon_0 r^2} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} \quad (r \ge a).$$
>The field grows linearly inside, is largest at the surface, and falls as $1/r^2$ outside. Both forms give $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^2)$ at $r = a$, so the field is continuous there.
>
>Outside any spherically symmetric distribution, the field equals that of a point charge at the center carrying the total charge. Charge farther out than $r$ makes no field at $r$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture wrote $Q_{\text{enc}}$ as a volume integral of $\rho$ but evaluated it only for a constant density. The layer-by-layer integral for a density that depends on $r$ follows Giancoli chapter 22.

# Intuition

Inside a uniform ball, a Gaussian sphere of radius $r$ encloses the fraction $(r/a)^3$ of the charge. Its area grows only as $r^2$. The field is proportional to the enclosed charge divided by the area, so it grows as $r^3/r^2 = r$. At the center the sphere encloses nothing, and the field is zero. Outside the ball the sphere encloses all the charge, and only the area keeps growing. The field then falls as $1/r^2$.

The field at radius $r$ depends only on the charge closer to the center than $r$. The first property shows why the charge farther out makes no field there.

A Gaussian sphere grows from a quarter of the ball's radius to twice the radius. Charge inside the sphere turns red. The graph traces the field, which rises in a straight line inside the ball and falls as $1/r^2$ outside.

# Derivation

Take the uniform ball of radius $a$ and density $\rho$. Symmetry makes the field radial, with a size $E(r)$ that depends only on $r$. For a point inside, choose a Gaussian sphere of radius $r < a$. On it, $d\vec A$ points radially outward along $\vec E$, and $E(r)$ is constant. The flux is
$$\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A = E(r)\,4\pi r^2.$$
The sphere encloses only the charge inside radius $r$. The density is constant, so the enclosed charge is density times volume:
$$Q_{\text{enc}} = \rho\cdot\tfrac43\pi r^3.$$
Gauss's law gives $E(r)\,4\pi r^2 = \rho\,\tfrac43\pi r^3/\varepsilon_0$, so $E = \rho r/(3\varepsilon_0)$.

For a point outside, choose a sphere of radius $r > a$. The flux has the same form, $E(r)\,4\pi r^2$, because the symmetry is the same. The enclosed charge stops growing at $r = a$, since there is no charge between $a$ and $r$:
$$Q_{\text{enc}} = \rho\cdot\tfrac43\pi a^3 = Q.$$
Gauss's law gives $E = \rho a^3/(3\varepsilon_0 r^2)$. Writing $\rho a^3 = 3Q/(4\pi)$ turns this into $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$, the field of a point charge $Q$ at the center.

# Properties

#### *(Outer Layers Make No Field)* The charge outside radius $r$ makes zero field at $r$, by zero flux combined with symmetry.

Split the charge beyond $r$ into thin uniform shells. Each shell lies outside the Gaussian sphere, so it adds zero net flux through it. The property *Total Field, Enclosed Charge* in [[3.3.2. Gauss's Law]] gives the reason. Zero flux alone would not give zero field. Each shell is spherically symmetric, though, so its own field on the Gaussian sphere is radial with one size $E_s$. Its flux is then $4\pi r^2 E_s = 0$, which forces $E_s = 0$. A uniform shell makes no field anywhere inside itself.

#### *(Agreement With Slicing)* Gauss's law gives the same shell field as the ring-by-ring integral, with no integration.

The Hard example in [[3.2.4b Ring of Charge]] cuts a uniform shell into rings and integrates. It needs a substitution and a derivative taken outside the integral. With $r$ the distance from the center, written $z$ there, it finds $kQ/r^2$ outside and $0$ inside. Gauss's law gives both results from $E\,4\pi r^2 = Q_{\text{enc}}/\varepsilon_0$, with $Q_{\text{enc}} = Q$ outside and $0$ inside. The two methods differ in what they can handle. Slicing still works for a shell with uneven charge. Gauss's law then still holds, but it no longer gives the field.

#### *(Continuous at a Ball, Jump at a Shell)* The field of a ball is continuous at its surface, while a thin shell makes the field jump.

The enclosed charge changes smoothly as a Gaussian sphere grows through a volume charge. Let $\delta$ be a small distance. Moving the sphere from radius $a - \delta$ to $a + \delta$ adds only the thin layer between them. That layer's charge goes to zero as $\delta$ does, so $E$ has no jump at $r = a$. A thin shell puts a finite charge $Q$ into zero thickness. Crossing it adds $Q$ to $Q_{\text{enc}}$ at once. The field jumps from $0$ to $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2)$. With $\sigma = Q/(4\pi R^2)$, the jump equals $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$. The same jump of $|\sigma|/\varepsilon_0$ appears at every charged surface of [[3.3.3a Conducting Shells|a metal shell]].

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Charged Plastic Ball
>A solid plastic ball of radius $a = 5.0\ \text{cm}$ carries $Q = 8.0\ \text{nC}$ spread evenly through its volume.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field $2.0\ \text{cm}$, $5.0\ \text{cm}$, and $10\ \text{cm}$ from its center.
>>The combination $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = (8.99 \times 10^{9})(8.0 \times 10^{-9}) = 71.9\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}$ appears in every case. Inside, at $r = 2.0\ \text{cm}$, the field is
>>$$E = \frac{Q\,r}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^3} = \frac{(71.9)(0.020)}{(0.050)^3} = 1.15 \times 10^{4}\ \text{N/C}.$$
>>At the surface, $E = 71.9/(0.050)^2 = 2.88 \times 10^{4}\ \text{N/C}$. Outside, at $10\ \text{cm}$, $E = 71.9/(0.10)^2 = 7.19 \times 10^{3}\ \text{N/C}$. All three fields point outward. The field at $2.0\ \text{cm}$ is $2/5$ of the surface value. At twice the radius it is $1/4$ of that value.

>[!example] (Medium) A Charge Inside a Shell
>A point charge of $+2.0\ \text{nC}$ sits at the center of a thin plastic shell of radius $20\ \text{cm}$. The shell carries $-6.0\ \text{nC}$ spread evenly.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field $10\ \text{cm}$ and $40\ \text{cm}$ from the center.
>>A Gaussian sphere of radius $10\ \text{cm}$ encloses only the point charge, so
>>$$E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{2.0 \times 10^{-9}}{(0.10)^2} = 1.80 \times 10^{3}\ \text{N/C},$$
>>pointing outward. A sphere of radius $40\ \text{cm}$ encloses $2.0 - 6.0 = -4.0\ \text{nC}$, so
>>$$E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{-4.0 \times 10^{-9}}{(0.40)^2} = -225\ \text{N/C}.$$
>>The minus sign means the field points inward. The shell's charge makes no field inside the shell.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture evaluated $Q_{\text{enc}}$ only for a constant density. The next example uses a density that grows with $r$, as in Giancoli chapter 22.

>[!example] (Medium) Density Growing Outward
>A ball of radius $a = 4.0\ \text{cm}$ carries a total charge $Q = 5.0\ \text{nC}$. Its density grows linearly from the center, $\rho = \rho_0 r/a$.
>
>>[!example]- Find $E$ inside and outside, and evaluate it at $r = 2.0\ \text{cm}$ and at the surface.
>>Adding thin layers of volume $4\pi r'^2\,dr'$ gives the enclosed charge:
>>$$Q_{\text{enc}}(r) = \int_0^r \frac{\rho_0 r'}{a}\,4\pi r'^2\,dr' = \frac{\pi\rho_0 r^4}{a}.$$
>>At $r = a$ this gives the total, $Q = \pi\rho_0 a^3$, so $Q_{\text{enc}} = Q\,r^4/a^4$. Inside, Gauss's law then gives
>>$$E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q\,r^2}{a^4} \quad (r \le a).$$
>>Outside, $E = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$, as for any spherical distribution. With the numbers, $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 45\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}$. At $2.0\ \text{cm}$ the field is $45\,(0.020)^2/(0.040)^4 = 7.0 \times 10^{3}\ \text{N/C}$. At the surface it is $45/(0.040)^2 = 2.8 \times 10^{4}\ \text{N/C}$. Halfway out, the field is $1/4$ of its surface value. A uniform ball would give $1/2$, because less of this ball's charge lies near the center.

>[!example] (Hard) An Electron Inside a Ball of Charge
>An early model of the atom pictured a ball of positive charge with electrons embedded in it. Take a ball of radius $a = 1.0 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ carrying $+e$ spread evenly. One electron, of mass $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$, is free to move inside it.
>
>>[!example]- Show that the electron oscillates about the center, and find the frequency.
>>Inside the ball the field points outward with size $E = e\,r/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^3)$. The electron's charge is $-e$, so the force on it points toward the center:
>>$$\vec F = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^3}\,\vec r.$$
>>The force is proportional to the displacement $\vec r$ from the center and opposite to it. This is the force law of a spring, so the electron performs [[1.6.1. Simple Harmonic Motion|simple harmonic motion]]. Its spring constant is
>>$$k_s = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^3} = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^{9})(1.602 \times 10^{-19})^2}{(1.0 \times 10^{-10})^3} = 231\ \text{N/m}.$$
>>The angular frequency is $\omega = \sqrt{k_s/m_e} = \sqrt{231/(9.11 \times 10^{-31})} = 1.59 \times 10^{16}\ \text{rad/s}$. The frequency is $f = \omega/2\pi = 2.5 \times 10^{15}\ \text{Hz}$, in the ultraviolet.
