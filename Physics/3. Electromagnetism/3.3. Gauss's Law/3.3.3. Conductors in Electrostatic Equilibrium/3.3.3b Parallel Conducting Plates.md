>[!note] Parallel Conducting Plates
>Two flat metal plates, each of area $A$, face each other a distance $d$ apart. One carries $+Q$ and the other $-Q$. The plates count as **large** when $\sqrt A \gg d$, so the gap is much smaller than their width. Large plates behave as follows:
>- The opposite charges attract each other across the gap, so all the charge moves to the two inner faces. The positive inner face has $\sigma = Q/A$, the negative inner face has $-\sigma$, and the outer faces carry nothing.
>- Outside the plates the field is zero.
>- Near the edges the field lines curve outward, an effect called **fringing**. It shrinks as the ratio $d/\sqrt A$ shrinks.
>
>Between the plates, away from the edges, the field is uniform. It points straight across from the positive plate to the negative plate, and its size is
>$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A}.$$
>Here $\sigma = Q/A$ is the charge density on the inner faces. This result treats the plates as infinite and ignores fringing. Two metal plates with opposite charges form a **parallel-plate capacitor**, the subject of [[3.5.1a Parallel-Plate Capacitor]].

# Intuition

Each plate's charge is pulled toward the other plate. It moves through the metal as close to the other plate as it can. That places it on the inner face. The charges then sit on two facing layers. Field lines run straight across the gap from the positive face to the negative face. No lines start on the outer faces, since they carry no charge.

Away from the edges, a point between the plates has plate extending far beyond it in every direction. Its field matches the field of infinite plates. Near an edge, a point has plate charge on one side and none on the other. The field there gains a sideways component, and the lines curve outward. Halving the gap or doubling the plate width shrinks this edge region compared with the whole plate. The ratio $d/\sqrt A$ measures how much it matters.

Each plate's charge moves through the metal to its inner face, the face toward the other plate. Field lines then run straight across the gap, and the field outside is zero. The last frame shows the lines bending outward near the ends of real plates.

# Derivation

Assume $d/\sqrt A \ll 1$ and treat the plates as infinite. Shifting along the plates then changes nothing, so the field between them is uniform. It is perpendicular to the plates and points from positive to negative. The inner face of the positive plate is a metal surface with density $\sigma = Q/A$. The [[3.3.3. Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium|surface rule]] gives $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$ right outside it, which is the field in the gap.

Outside the plates, any field would also be uniform and perpendicular, by the same symmetry. Take a pillbox with one face inside a plate and one face right outside its outer surface. The face in the metal has zero flux, since the field there is zero. The side wall has zero flux, since any field there runs along it. The pillbox encloses no charge, because the outer faces carry none. Gauss's law gives $EA = 0$, so $E = 0$ outside. The same fields follow from two charged sheets in [[3.3.2c Planar Symmetry]].

# Properties

#### *(Field Independent of the Gap)* At fixed charge, the field between large plates stays the same when the plates move apart or together.

The formula $E = Q/(\varepsilon_0 A)$ contains no $d$. Moving the plates leaves the charge density on each inner face unchanged, and the field depends only on that density. The result holds while the gap stays small compared with the plates' width. Once $d$ approaches $\sqrt A$, fringing spreads the lines, and the field in the middle drops. [[3.5.1a Parallel-Plate Capacitor]] combines this uniform field with the gap to find the capacitance.

#### *(How Large Is Large)* For square plates, fringing affects roughly the fraction $4d/\sqrt A$ of the plate area.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture said only that the ratio $d/\sqrt A$ must be small. The estimate below is a rough geometric argument added for these notes.

A word such as "large" has meaning only next to another length, here the gap $d$. The field differs noticeably from its uniform value only within a distance of about $d$ from the edges. For a square plate of side $L = \sqrt A$, a strip of width $d$ along the four edges covers about $4dL$. That is the fraction $4d/L$ of the plate. Plates $15\ \text{cm}$ wide and $2.0\ \text{mm}$ apart have $d/L = 0.013$, so the edge strip covers about $5\%$. The uniform-field result then holds over the remaining $95\%$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Field Between Two Plates
>Two square metal plates $15\ \text{cm}$ on a side sit $2.0\ \text{mm}$ apart. They carry $+12\ \text{nC}$ and $-12\ \text{nC}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field between the plates and outside them.
>>The ratio $d/\sqrt A = 0.0020/0.15 = 0.013$ is small, so the plates count as large. The charge density on the inner faces is
>>$$\sigma = \frac{Q}{A} = \frac{12 \times 10^{-9}}{(0.15)^2} = 5.3 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{C/m}^2.$$
>>Between the plates the field is
>>$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{5.3 \times 10^{-7}}{8.85 \times 10^{-12}} = 6.0 \times 10^{4}\ \text{N/C},$$
>>pointing from the positive plate to the negative one. Outside the plates the field is zero, apart from fringing near the edges.

>[!example] (Medium) Charging for a Target Field
>Two neutral square metal plates $20\ \text{cm}$ on a side sit $3.0\ \text{mm}$ apart. Electrons are moved from one plate to the other until the field between them is $1.5 \times 10^{5}\ \text{N/C}$.
>
>>[!example]- How much charge does each plate carry, and how many electrons were moved?
>>The ratio $d/\sqrt A = 0.0030/0.20 = 0.015$ is small, so the uniform-field result applies. Solving $E = Q/(\varepsilon_0 A)$ for $Q$ gives
>>$$Q = \varepsilon_0 E A = (8.85 \times 10^{-12})(1.5 \times 10^{5})(0.040) = 5.3 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{C}.$$
>>The plates carry $+53\ \text{nC}$ and $-53\ \text{nC}$. The number of electrons moved is $N = Q/e = 3.3 \times 10^{11}$. The gap does not enter the answer.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture treated only equal and opposite charges. The next example uses the sheet fields of [[3.3.2c Planar Symmetry]], as in Giancoli chapter 22.

>[!example] (Hard) Unequal Charges
>Two square metal plates $10\ \text{cm}$ on a side sit $1.5\ \text{mm}$ apart. The left plate carries $Q_L = +9.0\ \text{nC}$ and the right plate $Q_R = -3.0\ \text{nC}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the charge on each of the four faces, and the field in each region.
>>Label the faces $1$ to $4$ from left to right, with densities $\sigma_1$ to $\sigma_4$. Each face acts as a charged sheet with the field $\sigma_i/(2\varepsilon_0)$. Take right as positive. Inside the left plate, face 1 lies to the left and faces 2, 3, and 4 lie to the right. The field there must be zero:
>>$$\sigma_1 - \sigma_2 - \sigma_3 - \sigma_4 = 0.$$
>>Inside the right plate, faces 1, 2, and 3 lie to the left and face 4 to the right. The same condition gives
>>$$\sigma_1 + \sigma_2 + \sigma_3 - \sigma_4 = 0.$$
>>Subtracting the first equation from the second gives $\sigma_2 = -\sigma_3$, and adding them gives $\sigma_1 = \sigma_4$. Each plate's total charge fixes the rest. With $A = 0.010\ \text{m}^2$, $\sigma_1 + \sigma_2 = Q_L/A$ and $\sigma_3 + \sigma_4 = Q_R/A$. The solution is
>>$$\sigma_1 = \sigma_4 = \frac{Q_L + Q_R}{2A} = 3.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{C/m}^2, \qquad \sigma_2 = -\sigma_3 = \frac{Q_L - Q_R}{2A} = 6.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{C/m}^2.$$
>>The inner faces carry $\pm 6.0\ \text{nC}$, and each outer face carries $+3.0\ \text{nC}$. The surface rule then gives the fields. In the gap, $E = \sigma_2/\varepsilon_0 = 6.8 \times 10^{4}\ \text{N/C}$, pointing right. Outside, $E = \sigma_1/\varepsilon_0 = 3.4 \times 10^{4}\ \text{N/C}$, pointing away from the plates on both sides. With $Q_R = -Q_L$, the outer faces carry nothing, as for equal and opposite charges.
