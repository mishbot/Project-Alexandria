>[!note] Implicit Function Theorem
>A function is **explicit** when one variable is written as a formula in the others, as in $x = g(p)$. It is **implicit** when the relation is given only as an equation, $f(p, x) = 0$. Every explicit function can be written implicitly as $x - g(p) = 0$. The **implicit function theorem**, also called Dini's theorem, says when the converse works near a known solution. It also gives the derivative of the implicit function without solving the equation.
>
>In the univariate case, let $(p_0, x_0)$ solve $f(p, x) = 0$. Assume that $f$ is continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of $(p_0, x_0)$ and that $f'_x(p_0, x_0) \neq 0$. Then there is exactly one function $x = g(p)$, defined in a neighborhood of $p_0$ and taking values near $x_0$, with $f(p, g(p)) = 0$ and $g(p_0) = x_0$. Its derivative is
>$$g'(p) = -\frac{f'_p(p, g(p))}{f'_x(p, g(p))},$$
>where $f'_p$ and $f'_x$ are the partial derivatives of $f$, evaluated on the curve.
>
>In the multivariate case, $s$ equations $f_1(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{p}) = 0, \dots, f_s(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{p}) = 0$ link $s$ unknowns $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_s)$ to $n$ parameters $\mathbf{p} = (p_1, \dots, p_n)$. Let $(\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{p}_0)$ solve the system. Assume every $f_j$ is continuously differentiable near $(\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{p}_0)$. Assume also that the Jacobian matrix $\partial \mathbf{f}/\partial \mathbf{x}$ has a nonzero determinant at that point. This is the $s \times s$ matrix with $\partial f_j/\partial x_i$ in row $j$ and column $i$. Then there is exactly one set of functions $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{p})$, defined near $\mathbf{p}_0$ and taking values near $\mathbf{x}_0$, with $\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{p}), \mathbf{p}) = \mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{p}_0) = \mathbf{x}_0$. Its partial derivatives follow from Cramer's rule:
>$$\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial p_k} = -\frac{\det \dfrac{\partial(f_1, \dots, f_s)}{\partial(x_1, \dots, x_{i-1}, p_k, x_{i+1}, \dots, x_s)}}{\det \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{f}}{\partial \mathbf{x}}}.$$
>The numerator is the Jacobian with column $i$ replaced by $(\partial f_1/\partial p_k, \dots, \partial f_s/\partial p_k)$. The denominator is the same for every $i$ and $k$.
>
>Both conclusions are local. They hold only in some neighborhood of the starting point. When $f'_x = 0$, or the Jacobian determinant is zero, the theorem gives no conclusion. In economics the equations are usually first-order conditions or constraints. The theorem then gives the slope of an [[6.1.4 Indifference Curves and MRS|indifference curve]] and the [[6.3.1 Comparative Statics|comparative statics]] of an optimal choice.

# Intuition

Economic models rarely give an explicit formula for the choice. A consumer's demand or a firm's output is determined by an equation, such as a first-order condition, that often has no closed-form solution. We still want to know how the solution moves when a price or an income moves. The implicit function theorem answers this with the partial derivatives of the equation at one known solution.

Look at the level set $f(p, x) = 0$ in a small neighborhood of a known point. In a small enough neighborhood the curve is close to its tangent line. A tangent line that is not vertical can be solved for $x$ as a function of $p$, and its slope is $-f'_p/f'_x$. A vertical tangent cannot be solved that way. In the diagram the zero set is the circle $p^2 + x^2 = 1$. Near $(p_0, x_0) = (0.6, 0.8)$ the upper arc is the graph of $x = g(p) = \sqrt{1 - p^2}$, with slope $-0.6/0.8 = -0.75$. At $(1, 0)$ the tangent is vertical and $f'_x = 2x = 0$. Immediately to the left of $p = 1$ each $p$ has two values of $x$, and to the right of it none. No function $x = g(p)$ exists there.

Microeconomics uses the theorem wherever an equation defines a choice. An indifference curve $u(x_1, x_2) = \bar u$ is an implicit function, and its slope is the MRS $-u'_{x_1}/u'_{x_2}$ from [[6.1.4 Indifference Curves and MRS]]. The first-order conditions of an optimization problem define the optimal choice implicitly. The theorem then gives the response of that choice to prices, income, or interest rates, as in [[6.3.1 Comparative Statics]] and [[6.5.3 Intertemporal Choice]]. The method of [[7.2 Lagrange Multipliers]] also comes from applying the theorem to a constraint.

# Properties

#### *(Reuse the Denominator)* The Jacobian determinant is computed once and serves every derivative $\partial g_i/\partial p_k$.

Every derivative in the Cramer formula has the same denominator, $\det(\partial \mathbf{f}/\partial \mathbf{x})$. That determinant was already needed to check the second hypothesis. When the equations are the first-order conditions $\partial h/\partial x_i = 0$ of a function $h(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{p})$, the Jacobian of the system is the Hessian of $h$. Its determinant is then $|H|_s$, the last leading principal minor in the second-order conditions of [[7.1 Unconstrained Optimality Conditions]]. At a point that passes that second-order test, the determinant is nonzero and its sign is known in advance. With two choice variables at a maximum it is positive. The sign of $\partial x_i^*/\partial p_k$ then depends only on the numerator.

#### *(One-Equation Sign Rule)* For a single first-order condition with $h''_{x,x} < 0$, $dx^*/dp$ has the sign of the cross partial $h''_{x,p}$.

Let $x^*(p)$ solve $h'_x(x, p) = 0$, and suppose $h''_{x,x}(x^*, p) < 0$, the second-order condition for a strict maximum. Applying the univariate theorem to the equation $h'_x = 0$ gives
$$\frac{dx^*}{dp} = -\frac{h''_{x,p}(x^*, p)}{h''_{x,x}(x^*, p)}.$$
The denominator is negative, and it cancels the minus sign. So the choice rises with $p$ exactly when a higher $p$ raises the marginal return to $x$. Many signed results in consumer theory come from this check. For several equations the same logic holds, but the numerator determinant can mix several cross partials.

#### *(Swapping Roles)* When $f'_x = 0$ but $f'_p \neq 0$, the equation defines $p$ as a function of $x$ instead.

The theorem treats the two variables alike. At the rightmost point $(1, 0)$ of the circle, $f'_x = 0$ but $f'_p = 2p = 2$. The theorem then gives $p = \sqrt{1 - x^2}$ near $x = 0$, with $dp/dx = -f'_x/f'_p = 0$. Where both derivatives exist, they are reciprocals: $dx/dp = 1/(dp/dx)$. The hypothesis $f'_x \neq 0$ is a sufficient condition, and a function $x = g(p)$ can exist without it. The equation $p - x^3 = 0$ has $f'_x = 0$ at $(0, 0)$, yet $x = p^{1/3}$ is a function of $p$. That function has an infinite slope at $p = 0$, so the derivative formula still fails there.

#### *(Local Only)* The function $g$ depends on the starting point, so the derivative must be evaluated at that point.

The circle has two solutions $x$ for every $p$ in $(-1, 1)$. Starting from $(0.6, 0.8)$, the theorem picks the upper arc, with slope $-0.75$. Starting from $(0.6, -0.8)$, it picks the lower arc, with slope $+0.75$. The formula $-f'_p/f'_x = -p/x$ gives both answers because it uses the $x$ of the starting point. The requirement that $g$ take values near $x_0$ rules out the other arc. When an economic problem has several optima, each one has its own comparative statics.

# Derivation

We want the derivative formula from the total differential. The total differential of $f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ is
$$df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}\,dx_1 + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}\,dx_2 + \dots + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\,dx_n.$$
It gives the first-order change in $f$ when each argument $x_i$ changes by $dx_i$. Along the curve $f(p, g(p)) = 0$ the value of $f$ stays at zero, so $df = 0$:
$$f'_p\,dp + f'_x\,dx = 0.$$
Solving for the ratio gives $dx/dp = -f'_p/f'_x$, which requires $f'_x \neq 0$. This calculation finds the slope once $g$ is known to exist and to be differentiable. Those two facts are the content of the theorem.

For a system, take the total differential of each equation with only $p_k$ changing:
$$\sum_{i=1}^s \frac{\partial f_j}{\partial x_i}\,dx_i = -\frac{\partial f_j}{\partial p_k}\,dp_k, \qquad j = 1, \dots, s.$$
This is a linear system $J\,d\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, with $J = \partial\mathbf{f}/\partial\mathbf{x}$ and $b_j = -(\partial f_j/\partial p_k)\,dp_k$. Cramer's rule solves it when $\det J \neq 0$. It gives $dx_i = \det J_i/\det J$, where $J_i$ is $J$ with column $i$ replaced by $\mathbf{b}$. A determinant is linear in each column, so the factor $-dp_k$ comes out of column $i$. Dividing by $dp_k$ gives the formula in the callout.

>[!warning] Sign and Order
>The formula carries a minus sign, and the derivative with respect to the variable being solved for goes in the denominator. Dropping the minus sign reverses every comparative-statics result.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Curve That Solves Only One Way
>The equation $x_1 e^{x_2} + x_2 - 2 = 0$ passes through the point $(x_1, x_2) = (2, 0)$.
>
>>[!example]- Can the equation be solved explicitly for $x_1$? For $x_2$? Find $dx_2/dx_1$ at $(2, 0)$.
>>Solving for $x_1$ gives $x_1 = (2 - x_2)e^{-x_2}$. Solving for $x_2$ is not possible with elementary functions, because $x_2$ appears both inside and outside the exponential. The implicit function theorem gives the slope anyway.
>>
>>Let $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1 e^{x_2} + x_2 - 2$. It is continuously differentiable everywhere. Its partial derivatives are $f'_{x_1} = e^{x_2}$ and $f'_{x_2} = x_1 e^{x_2} + 1$, which equal $1$ and $3$ at $(2, 0)$. The second hypothesis holds because $f'_{x_2} = 3 \neq 0$. So near $x_1 = 2$ there is a unique function $x_2 = g(x_1)$ with $g(2) = 0$, and
>>$$g'(2) = -\frac{f'_{x_1}}{f'_{x_2}} = -\frac{1}{3}.$$
>>The explicit function in the other direction gives a check. From $x_1 = (2 - x_2)e^{-x_2}$, the derivative is $dx_1/dx_2 = -(3 - x_2)e^{-x_2}$, which equals $-3$ at $x_2 = 0$. Its reciprocal is $-1/3$.

>[!example] (Medium) Keeping Output Fixed at a Café
>A café makes $q = 4L^{1/2}K^{1/2}$ coffees per hour from $L$ barista hours and $K$ espresso-machine hours. It currently uses $L = 9$ and $K = 4$. The set of input pairs that give the same output is an **isoquant**, the production version of an indifference curve.
>
>>[!example]- What is the slope $dK/dL$ of the isoquant at $(9, 4)$? If the café cuts barista time by $0.9$ hours, about how many more machine hours keep output unchanged? Compare with the exact answer.
>>Output is $q = 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 24$, so the isoquant is the implicit function $f(L, K) = 4L^{1/2}K^{1/2} - 24 = 0$. The partial derivatives are the marginal products $f'_L = 2K^{1/2}/L^{1/2} = 4/3$ and $f'_K = 2L^{1/2}/K^{1/2} = 3$. Since $f'_K \neq 0$, the theorem applies:
>>$$\frac{dK}{dL} = -\frac{f'_L}{f'_K} = -\frac{4/3}{3} = -\frac{4}{9}.$$
>>A change $dL = -0.9$ therefore needs about $dK = -\frac{4}{9}(-0.9) = 0.4$ extra machine hours.
>>
>>For the exact answer, output $24$ means $LK = 36$ on this isoquant. At $L = 8.1$ this gives $K = 36/8.1 \approx 4.444$, so the café needs $0.444$ extra hours. The derivative is the slope at $(9, 4)$ only. The isoquant $K = 36/L$ is convex and lies above its tangent line, so the linear estimate falls short for a finite change.

>[!example] (Hard) Shared Oven at a Bakery
>A bakery sells bread $x_1$ at price $10$ and pastries $x_2$ at price $8$. Its daily cost is $a x_1^2 + x_1 x_2 + x_2^2$. The term $x_1 x_2$ appears because both products share one oven. The parameter $a$ rises with the price of a flour used only for bread. Profit is
>$$\pi(x_1, x_2; a) = 10x_1 + 8x_2 - a x_1^2 - x_1x_2 - x_2^2.$$
>
>>[!example]- At $a = 1$, use the implicit function theorem on the first-order conditions to find $\partial x_1^*/\partial a$ and $\partial x_2^*/\partial a$. Check against the explicit solution.
>>The first-order conditions are two equations in $(x_1, x_2)$ with parameter $a$:
>>$$f_1 = 10 - 2a x_1 - x_2 = 0, \qquad f_2 = 8 - x_1 - 2x_2 = 0.$$
>>At $a = 1$ they give $x_1^* = 4$ and $x_2^* = 2$. The Jacobian with respect to $(x_1, x_2)$ is
>>$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{f}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} = \begin{pmatrix} -2a & -1 \\ -1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det = 4a - 1 = 3 \neq 0,$$
>>so the theorem applies. This matrix is also the Hessian of $\pi$, and [[7.1 Unconstrained Optimality Conditions]] uses it to confirm that $(4, 2)$ is a maximum.
>>
>>The derivatives with respect to $a$ are $\partial f_1/\partial a = -2x_1 = -8$ and $\partial f_2/\partial a = 0$. Replacing column $1$, and then column $2$, by $(-8, 0)$ gives
>>$$\frac{\partial x_1^*}{\partial a} = -\frac{1}{3}\det\begin{pmatrix} -8 & -1 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix} = -\frac{16}{3}, \qquad \frac{\partial x_2^*}{\partial a} = -\frac{1}{3}\det\begin{pmatrix} -2 & -8 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{8}{3}.$$
>>Both derivatives reuse the determinant $3$. Dearer flour cuts bread output. Less bread lowers the marginal cost of pastries, $x_1 + 2x_2$, so pastry output rises.
>>
>>The explicit solution confirms this. Solving the two conditions gives $x_1^* = 12/(4a - 1)$ and $x_2^* = (16a - 10)/(4a - 1)$. Their derivatives are $-48/(4a - 1)^2$ and $24/(4a - 1)^2$, which equal $-16/3$ and $8/3$ at $a = 1$.
