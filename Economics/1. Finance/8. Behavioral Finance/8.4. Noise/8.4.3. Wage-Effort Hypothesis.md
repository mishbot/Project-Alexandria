> [!note] Akerlof-Yellen Wage-Effort Hypothesis
> The **wage-effort hypothesis** (Akerlof & Yellen) says workers' **effort depends on perceived wage fairness**, so firms may pay above the market-clearing wage because "fair" pay elicits higher effort, while perceived unfair pay reduces effort.
> 
> The model formalizes fairness with a **fair wage** $w_i^*$ and an **effort rule**
>$$e_i=\min\!\left(\frac{w_i}{w_i^*},\,1\right),$$
>where $w_i$ is worker $i$’s wage. If $w_i<w_i^*$, effort falls proportionally; if $w_i\ge w_i^*$, effort caps at 1.
>
>The fair wage is a weighted average of **coworker pay** and the **market-clearing wage**:
>$$w_i^*=\beta\,w_{-i}+(1-\beta)\,w_i^{c},$$
> where $w_{-i}$ is a reference coworker wage and $w_i^{c}$ is the competitive (market-clearing) wage. The parameter $\beta\in[0,1]$ measures how much fairness is driven by **internal comparisons** vs. the outside market.
> 
> **Implication:** raising one worker’s wage can raise the reference point $w_{-i}$, making others feel underpaid and reducing their effort unless their wages adjust too. However, the performance of the worker whose wage was raised does not improve.
> - Note that fairness violations are only triggered when the rationale for pay differences is unclear to workers.

  

**Exercise (from the slide)**

- Assume \beta=1 and all workers initially earn the same wage w. Then w_i^*=w_{-i}=w, so e_i=\min(w/w,1)=1.
    
- If there are 100 workers and only worker 1 gets a raise to w_1'>w:
    
    - For every other worker j\neq 1, the reference wage rises above w (coworker comparison), so w_j^*>w and e_j=\min(w/w_j^*,1)<1.
        
    - For worker 1, w_1'>w_1^* so e_1=\min(w_1'/w_1^*,1)=1 (still capped).
