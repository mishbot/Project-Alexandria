>[!note] Principal Component Analysis
>**Principal component analysis** (PCA) finds the orthogonal directions along which a data set varies most. It describes each sample by its coordinates along the top $k$ of them. Stack $n$ samples $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ as the rows of $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, and subtract the mean sample $\bar{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \mathbf{x}_i$ from every row. From here on, $\mathbf{X}$ denotes this centered matrix. Its sample covariance and its [[13.1 Singular Value Decomposition|SVD]] are
>$$\mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{n-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}, \qquad \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^T,$$
>with singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0$, left singular vectors $\mathbf{u}_j \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and right singular vectors $\mathbf{v}_j \in \mathbb{R}^d$. PCA reads everything off these two factorizations:
>
>- **Principal directions** (loadings): the columns $\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots$ of $\mathbf{V}$. They are orthonormal eigenvectors of $\mathbf{S}$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_j = \sigma_j^2/(n-1)$.
>- **Variance along $\mathbf{v}_j$**: $\lambda_j$. The total variance is $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{S} = \sum_j \lambda_j$.
>- **Scores**: $\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{V}_k = \mathbf{U}_k\boldsymbol\Sigma_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$, where $\mathbf{V}_k = [\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k]$. Row $i$ is the code $\mathbf{z}_i$ of sample $i$, with entries $z_{ij} = \mathbf{v}_j^T(\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})$.
>- **Reconstruction**: $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i = \bar{\mathbf{x}} + \mathbf{V}_k\mathbf{z}_i$ for one sample. For the centered matrix it is $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{V}_k\mathbf{V}_k^T = \sum_{j \le k}\sigma_j\mathbf{u}_j\mathbf{v}_j^T$.
>- **Variance explained** by component $j$: $\sigma_j^2 / \sum_i \sigma_i^2$. The first $k$ components explain the sum of their shares.
>
>Two views lead to the same subspace, spanned by $\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k$:
>
>- **Maximum variance**: $\mathbf{v}_1 = \arg\max_{\|\mathbf{v}\| = 1}\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{v}$. Each later $\mathbf{v}_j$ maximizes the same quantity among unit vectors orthogonal to $\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_{j-1}$.
>- **Minimum reconstruction error**: $\mathbf{V}_k$ minimizes $\sum_i\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{x}_i\|^2$ over all $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ with orthonormal columns. By the [[13.3 Low-Rank Approximation|Eckart-Young theorem]], the minimum is $\sum_{j > k}\sigma_j^2$.
>
>There are three common rules for choosing $k$. The first looks for the elbow of a **scree plot** of $\lambda_j$ against $j$. The second keeps enough components for a target share of variance, such as $90\%$ or $95\%$. The third validates $k$ on a later task. Features in different units should be standardized first, which makes $\mathbf{S}$ the correlation matrix. Each $\mathbf{v}_j$ is unique up to sign when the $\sigma_j$ are distinct. A linear [[6.2. Autoencoders|autoencoder]] with a $k$-dimensional code and squared loss learns the same subspace, possibly in a different basis.

>[!warning] Rows Versus Columns
>The Mathematics notes [[13.1 Singular Value Decomposition]] and [[13.3 Low-Rank Approximation]] state their results for a general matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$. They write plain capitals and arrow vectors such as $\vec{u}_i$. To apply them here, set $A = \mathbf{X}$. Their $m$ is then the sample count $n$, and their $n$ is the feature count $d$. Many linear algebra texts instead store data points as the columns of $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$. Then $A = \mathbf{X}^T$, the principal directions are the left singular vectors of the centered $A$, and the covariance is $\frac{1}{n-1}AA^T$. Some texts also divide by $n$ instead of $n - 1$. The directions stay the same, and every $\lambda_j$ scales by $(n-1)/n$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture 14 defined PCA as projection onto the top $k$ right singular vectors of $\mathbf{X}$. It linked PCA to the Eckart-Young theorem and to linear autoencoders. The covariance and maximum-variance view, variance explained, choosing $k$ and standardizing are not from the lecture. Neither are the properties and the derivation below. They follow ISLR section 12.2 (James, Witten, Hastie and Tibshirani) and Bishop's PRML section 12.1.

# Intuition

The name describes the output. The **principal components** are the main directions in which the data vary. Picture a cloud of points in $d$ dimensions. Draw the line through its center along which the points spread the most. That line is the first principal direction. The second is the direction of greatest spread perpendicular to the first, and so on. Projecting each point onto the first few directions keeps most of its position with far fewer numbers. The dropped directions carry little spread, so little is lost when most of the variance sits in a few directions.

Each centered point splits into a part inside the kept subspace and a residual perpendicular to it. By Pythagoras, the squared length of the point is the sum of the squared lengths of the two parts. The data fix the total. Keeping more variance in the subspace therefore leaves less in the residuals. This is why the maximum-variance view and the minimum-error view pick the same directions.

The animation projects 30 points onto the plane of the top two principal directions. The sticks are the residuals, and their squared lengths sum to $\sigma_3^2$. The bars show each direction's share of the variance.

# Properties

#### *(Centering Matters)* Without centering, the first direction points toward the mean instead of along the spread.

An uncentered SVD finds the best subspace through the origin. When the data sit far from the origin, the best single direction points at the cloud, close to $\bar{\mathbf{x}}/\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|$. Its squared singular value is then close to $n\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^2$, which measures the distance of the cloud from the origin. In the Hard example below, a direction $7.3°$ away from the main axis of spread reports a share of $99.87\%$. Centering also lowers the rank. The rows of a centered matrix sum to zero, so its rank is at most $\min(n - 1, d)$.

#### *(Scale Dependence)* Changing the units of one feature changes the principal directions.

A feature measured in grams has $10^6$ times the variance of the same feature in kilograms. That feature then dominates $\mathbf{S}$, and $\mathbf{v}_1$ points almost along its axis. Standardizing each feature to unit variance gives the PCA of the correlation matrix. With two standardized features of correlation $r$, the directions are always $(1, 1)/\sqrt{2}$ and $(1, -1)/\sqrt{2}$. The first of them explains $(1 + |r|)/2$ of the variance. Standardizing is the wrong choice when all features share a unit and their variances carry meaning. Image pixels are an example. It would inflate pixels that barely vary, such as a plain background.

#### *(Uncorrelated Scores)* The scores along different principal directions are uncorrelated, and their variances are the $\lambda_j$.

The sample covariance of the scores is
$$\frac{1}{n-1}\mathbf{Z}^T\mathbf{Z} = \frac{1}{n-1}\boldsymbol\Sigma_k\mathbf{U}_k^T\mathbf{U}_k\boldsymbol\Sigma_k = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k),$$
because the columns of $\mathbf{U}_k$ are orthonormal. With $k = d$, PCA rotates the axes, possibly with a reflection, so that the covariance becomes diagonal. Dividing each score by $\sqrt{\lambda_j}$ gives **whitened** data with identity covariance. Uncorrelated scores are guaranteed to be independent only when the data are jointly Gaussian.

#### *(Compute With the SVD)* Forming $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ squares the condition number, so computing the SVD of $\mathbf{X}$ directly is more accurate.

The eigenvalues of $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ are the squares $\sigma_j^2$. A ratio $\sigma_1/\sigma_d = 10^8$ becomes $10^{16}$, beyond the relative precision of double-precision numbers. The small components are then lost to rounding. A full SVD of $\mathbf{X}$ costs $O(nd\min(n, d))$. Randomized SVD (Halko, Martinsson and Tropp 2011) finds the top $k$ components in about $O(ndk)$. When $d > n$, as with images or gene data, the $n \times n$ matrix $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^T$ is smaller. Its eigenvectors are the $\mathbf{u}_j$, and $\mathbf{v}_j = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{u}_j/\sigma_j$.

#### *(Variance Is Not Relevance)* PCA ignores labels, so it can drop the direction that matters for prediction.

PCA ranks directions by spread alone. Take two classes that form thin parallel clouds and differ only across their short side. The first principal direction runs along the clouds and mixes the classes. The dropped direction is the one that separates them. Linear discriminant analysis in [[4.2. Linear Discriminants]] uses the labels and finds it. Principal component regression, which regresses the target on the top components, has the same risk. PCA is also linear, so it cannot flatten data that lie near a curved surface. A nonlinear [[6.2. Autoencoders|autoencoder]] can.

# Derivation

We derive the maximum-variance view and show that it agrees with the minimum-error view. Project the centered samples onto a unit vector $\mathbf{v}$. The projections $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}$ have mean zero, so their sample variance is
$$\frac{1}{n-1}\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{v}.$$
We maximize this subject to $\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{v} = 1$. The Lagrangian $\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{v} - \lambda(\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{v} - 1)$ has gradient $2\mathbf{S}\mathbf{v} - 2\lambda\mathbf{v}$. Setting it to zero gives the eigenvalue equation $\mathbf{S}\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$. At such a $\mathbf{v}$ the variance is $\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{v} = \lambda$, so the best choice is the eigenvector of the largest eigenvalue. Repeating the argument among vectors orthogonal to $\mathbf{v}_1$ gives the second eigenvector, and so on.

The SVD connects these eigenvectors to $\mathbf{V}$. Substituting $\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^T$ and using $\mathbf{U}^T\mathbf{U} = \mathbf{I}$ gives
$$\mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{n-1}\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol\Sigma^T\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^T,$$
an eigendecomposition with eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_j$ and eigenvalues $\sigma_j^2/(n-1)$. The total variance is $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{S} = \sum_j\sigma_j^2/(n-1)$, which justifies the variance-explained ratio.

For the error view, let $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ have orthonormal columns. Each sample splits into its projection $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{x}_i$ and a perpendicular residual. Pythagoras then gives
$$\|\mathbf{X}\|_F^2 = \|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\|_F^2 + \|\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^T\|_F^2,$$
where $\|\cdot\|_F$ is the [[4.9a Frobenius Norm|Frobenius norm]]. The left side does not depend on $\mathbf{W}$. Maximizing the kept variance $\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\|_F^2$ is therefore the same as minimizing the error. The choice $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{V}_k$ keeps $\sum_{j \le k}\sigma_j^2$ and leaves the error $\sum_{j > k}\sigma_j^2$. By Eckart-Young, no matrix of rank $k$ comes closer to $\mathbf{X}$, so $\mathbf{V}_k$ solves both problems.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Two Judges, Four Divers
>Two judges score four divers. Judge A gives $76$, $64$, $66$ and $74$. Judge B gives $80$, $64$, $75$ and $69$. Each row of the data matrix holds one diver's two scores.
>
>>[!example]- Find the principal directions, the scores, the share of variance of the first component and the rank-1 reconstruction.
>>The mean scores are $70$ and $72$. Subtracting them gives the centered rows $(6, 8)$, $(-6, -8)$, $(-4, 3)$ and $(4, -3)$. Then
>>$$\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 104 & 72 \\ 72 & 146 \end{bmatrix},$$
>>with eigenvalues $200$ and $50$ and eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_1 = (0.6, 0.8)$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = (-0.8, 0.6)$. The singular values are $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{200} \approx 14.14$ and $\sigma_2 = \sqrt{50} \approx 7.07$, and $\mathbf{S}$ has eigenvalues $66.7$ and $16.7$. The first scores are $10$, $-10$, $0$ and $0$, and the second scores are $0$, $0$, $5$ and $-5$. The first component explains $200/250 = 80\%$ of the variance. It measures how well a diver did overall, with judge B weighted a little more. The second component records where the judges disagreed. The rank-1 reconstruction $\bar{\mathbf{x}} + z_{i1}\mathbf{v}_1$ gives $(76, 80)$, $(64, 64)$, $(70, 72)$ and $(70, 72)$. The last two divers lose their disagreement, an error of $25$ each. The total error, $50$, equals $\sigma_2^2$.

>[!example] (Medium) Square Meters Versus Bedrooms
>Five houses have floor areas of $60$, $80$, $100$, $120$ and $140$ m². They have $2$, $2$, $3$, $3$ and $5$ bedrooms.
>
>>[!example]- Compare the first principal direction and its share of variance before and after standardizing.
>>The sample variance is $1000$ for area and $1.5$ for bedrooms, and the covariance is $35$. The first eigenvector of $\mathbf{S}$ is $(0.9994, 0.035)$, only $2.0°$ from the area axis. It explains $99.97\%$ of the variance, because area is measured in larger units. The correlation is $r = 35/\sqrt{1000 \cdot 1.5} \approx 0.904$. After standardizing, the first direction is $(1, 1)/\sqrt{2}$, and it explains $(1 + 0.904)/2 \approx 95.2\%$. Raw PCA reports area alone. Standardized PCA reports overall house size, which both features measure.

>[!example] (Medium) Compressing a Sensor Log
>A factory logs $1{,}000$ readings from $6$ sensors. After centering, the singular values of the data matrix are $40$, $30$, $10$, $4$, $2$ and $1$.
>
>>[!example]- How many components keep at least $95\%$ of the variance? What error and storage result?
>>The squared singular values are $1600$, $900$, $100$, $16$, $4$ and $1$, with total $2621$. Their shares are $61.0\%$, $34.3\%$, $3.8\%$, $0.6\%$, $0.2\%$ and $0.04\%$. The cumulative shares are $61.0\%$, $95.4\%$ and $99.2\%$ for $k = 1, 2, 3$, so $k = 2$ is the smallest that passes $95\%$. The scree plot also bends after the second value, where $\sigma_j$ drops from $30$ to $10$. The squared reconstruction error is $100 + 16 + 4 + 1 = 121$. That is a root-mean-square error of $\sqrt{121/6000} \approx 0.14$ per entry, in the sensors' units. Storing the scores, two directions and the mean takes $1000 \cdot 2 + 6 \cdot 2 + 6 = 2018$ numbers instead of $6000$.

>[!example] (Hard) Forgetting to Center
>An analyst runs the SVD on the divers' raw scores from the Easy example, without subtracting the means.
>
>>[!example]- What is the first direction, what share does it report, and why?
>>The raw matrix $\mathbf{R}$ gives
>>$$\mathbf{R}^T\mathbf{R} = \begin{bmatrix} 19704 & 20232 \\ 20232 & 20882 \end{bmatrix},$$
>>with eigenvalues $40533.6$ and $52.4$. The first direction is $(0.697, 0.717)$. It lies within $0.03°$ of the mean direction $(70, 72)/\|(70, 72)\| = (0.697, 0.717)$. It reports $40533.6/40586.0 = 99.87\%$ of the squared norm. Almost all of that is $n\|\bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^2 = 4 \cdot 10084 = 40336$, which measures how far the cloud sits from the origin. The true axis of spread, $(0.6, 0.8)$, is $7.3°$ away. A rank-1 fit without the mean leaves an error of $52.4$. Centered PCA stores one extra vector, the mean, and leaves an error of $50$. Its direction also describes the spread of the scores.
