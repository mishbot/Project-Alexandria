>[!note] Time Consistency
>Preferences are **time consistent** when the plan a person makes for a future date coincides with the action she takes when that date arrives. The setting extends [[6.5.3 Intertemporal Choice]] to three periods, $t = 0, 1, 2$. At each date the person has resources $M_t'$, equal to income $M_t$ plus savings (or minus debts) from the previous period. She consumes $c_t$ and saves or borrows $M_t' - c_t$ at interest rate $r$. She cannot borrow in the last period, so $c_2 = M_2'$. From $t = 1$ on, the budget is
>$$c_1 + \frac{c_2}{1+r} \le M_1' + \frac{M_2}{1+r}.$$
>- *Exponential discounting:* utility is $U(c_0) + \frac{1}{1+\delta}U(c_1) + \frac{1}{(1+\delta)^2}U(c_2)$ at $t = 0$ and $U(c_1) + \frac{1}{1+\delta}U(c_2)$ at $t = 1$, with $U' > 0$ and $U'' < 0$. Both selves want $U'(c_1)/U'(c_2) = (1+r)/(1+\delta)$, so these preferences are time consistent.
>- **$\beta$-$\delta$ preferences** (quasi-hyperbolic discounting; Akerlof 1991, Laibson 1997, O'Donoghue and Rabin 1999): utility at date $t$ is
>$$U(c_t) + \frac{\beta}{1+\delta}\,U(c_{t+1}) + \frac{\beta}{(1+\delta)^2}\,U(c_{t+2}) + \dots$$
>The discount factors are $1, \frac{\beta}{1+\delta}, \frac{\beta}{(1+\delta)^2}, \dots$ in place of $1, \frac{1}{1+\delta}, \frac{1}{(1+\delta)^2}, \dots$. A value $\beta < 1$ captures **immediate gratification**, also called present bias. $\beta = 1$ gives back exponential discounting.
>- *Choice versus plan:* the self at $t = 1$ chooses $(c_1^*, c_2^*)$ with $\dfrac{U'(c_1^*)}{U'(c_2^*)} = \beta\,\dfrac{1+r}{1+\delta}$. The self at $t = 0$, if she can commit, plans $(c_1^{*,c}, c_2^{*,c})$ with $\dfrac{U'(c_1^{*,c})}{U'(c_2^{*,c})} = \dfrac{1+r}{1+\delta}$.
>- *Time inconsistency:* for $\beta < 1$ and any $U$ with $U'' < 0$, $c_1^{*,c} < c_1^*$ and $c_2^{*,c} > c_2^*$. The person allows herself more immediate consumption than she planned. The self at $t = 0$ is strictly worse off under the later choice, so she values **commitment**, any device that fixes $c_1$ in advance (not taught).
>- *Log utility* (not taught): with $W_1 = M_1' + M_2/(1+r)$, the choice is $c_1^* = \frac{1+\delta}{1+\delta+\beta}\,W_1$ and the plan is $c_1^{*,c} = \frac{1+\delta}{2+\delta}\,W_1$.
>- *One activity:* utility $b$ at $t = 1$ and $B$ at $t = 2$ is worth $b + \frac{\beta}{1+\delta}B$ to the self at $t = 1$ and $\frac{\beta}{1+\delta}b + \frac{\beta}{(1+\delta)^2}B$ to the self at $t = 0$.

>[!warning] Two Meanings of $\delta$
>Here $\delta$ is a discount rate, and one period of delay multiplies utility by $1/(1+\delta)$. [[8.10.6. Hyperbolic Discounting]] writes the same model as $u(c_t) + \beta\sum_{k \ge 1}\delta^k u(c_{t+k})$, where $\delta$ is a discount factor. The two match when the factor equals $1/(1 + \text{rate})$.

# Intuition

People plan to start exercising tomorrow, to save from next month's paycheck, and to skip dessert next time. When the day comes, many of these plans change. Exponential discounting cannot produce such reversals. It discounts every one-period delay by the same factor $1/(1+\delta)$. So the trade-off between dates $1$ and $2$ looks the same from date $0$ as from date $1$. $\beta$-$\delta$ preferences make the present special. Every future date gets an extra factor $\beta < 1$ relative to today, while two future dates are still compared at the rate $1 + \delta$. Seen from $t = 0$, dates $1$ and $2$ both lie in the future, so the weight on $c_1$ is $1 + \delta$ times the weight on $c_2$. At $t = 1$, date $1$ becomes the present and the ratio jumps to $(1+\delta)/\beta$. The person at $t = 0$ and the person at $t = 1$ act like two selves who disagree about the same trade-off.

The diagram shows the weights for $\beta = 0.5$ and $\delta = 0.25$, with the present in red. Several facts about American households fit this model. Americans hold about one trillion dollars of credit card debt, most of it at teaser rates. Two thirds of Americans are overweight or obese, and health clubs are an 11 billion dollar industry. Credit card debt and weight gain fit a present self who overrides an earlier plan, and paid gym memberships fit a demand for commitment.

# Properties

#### *(Why Exponential Is Consistent)* Exponential utility at $t = 0$ contains the utility at $t = 1$ as a block.

Factoring out $1/(1+\delta)$ gives
$$U(c_0) + \frac{1}{1+\delta}U(c_1) + \frac{1}{(1+\delta)^2}U(c_2) = U(c_0) + \frac{1}{1+\delta}\left[U(c_1) + \frac{1}{1+\delta}U(c_2)\right].$$
The bracket is the utility at $t = 1$. The self at $t = 0$ ranks every pair $(c_1, c_2)$ exactly as the self at $t = 1$ does, so her plan is what the later self picks anyway. With $\beta$-$\delta$ preferences the same step gives $U(c_0) + \frac{\beta}{1+\delta}\left[U(c_1) + \frac{1}{1+\delta}U(c_2)\right]$. The bracket weights $c_2$ by $1/(1+\delta)$, while the self at $t = 1$ weights it by $\beta/(1+\delta)$. For $\beta < 1$ the two selves rank pairs $(c_1, c_2)$ differently, and the factor $\beta$ alone causes it.

#### *(Temptations and Chores)* Present bias makes the later self more willing to give in to temptations and less willing to do chores.

Take an activity with utility $b$ at $t = 1$ and $B$ at $t = 2$. The self at $t = 0$ approves of it when $b + B/(1+\delta) > 0$, since the common factor $\beta/(1+\delta)$ does not change the sign. The self at $t = 1$ does it when $b + \beta B/(1+\delta) > 0$. For a **temptation**, with $b > 0$ and $B < 0$, such as alcohol, shopping, or credit card borrowing, the later self shrinks the future cost and is more willing. For a **chore**, with $b < 0$ and $B > 0$, such as exercising or helping a friend, the later self shrinks the future reward and is less willing. She puts the chore off although she planned to do it. An exponential discounter, with $\beta = 1$, applies the same condition at both dates. *(The lecture computed both values for one example and listed these activities. The general sign argument was not taught.)*

#### *(Direction of the Bias)* The later self consumes more at $t = 1$ than planned, whatever the shape of $U$.

Along the budget line, raising $c_1$ lowers $c_2$. Then $U'(c_1)$ falls and $U'(c_2)$ rises, so the ratio $U'(c_1)/U'(c_2)$ strictly falls as $c_1$ rises. The self at $t = 1$ targets the ratio $\beta(1+r)/(1+\delta)$, below the target $(1+r)/(1+\delta)$ of the plan. A lower target needs a higher $c_1$, and the budget then forces a lower $c_2$. The size of the gap depends on $U$. With log utility $c_1^*/c_1^{*,c} = (2+\delta)/(1+\delta+\beta)$, which grows as $\beta$ falls. In the animated diagram $r = \delta = 0$, $\beta = 0.5$, and resources are $12$. The plan $P = (6, 6)$ is tangent to an indifference curve of the self at $t = 0$. When date $1$ arrives, the choice moves to $Q = (8, 4)$. The indifference curves of the self at $t = 1$ are steeper at every point, because she values $c_1$ more relative to $c_2$, so her tangency lies further down the budget line.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture solved the problem of the self at $t = 0$ with commitment and stopped there. The value of commitment and the naive versus sophisticated distinction below are extensions.

#### *(Value of Commitment)* The self at $t = 0$ would pay to take the choice away from the self at $t = 1$.

The objective of the self at $t = 0$ is strictly concave in $(c_1, c_2)$ and the budget is linear, so her plan is the unique best point on the budget line. For $\beta < 1$ the choice of the self at $t = 1$ is a different point on the same line, so it gives the self at $t = 0$ strictly less utility. She gains from any **commitment device** that fixes $c_1$ in advance, and she would accept a cost for it. Examples are savings accounts with withdrawal penalties, prepaid gym memberships, and not keeping sweets at home. An exponential discounter gains nothing from commitment, since her later self follows the plan anyway. Without commitment, the outcome depends on what the self at $t = 0$ expects. A **sophisticated** person foresees her later bias and chooses $c_0$ by backward induction, as in [[3.2b Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibrium]]. A **naive** person expects her later self to follow the plan and is surprised.

# Derivation

We want the two first-order conditions. The choice of $c_0$ fixes $M_1'$, which both problems below take as given. Following [[7.2 Lagrange Multipliers]], the self at $t = 1$ maximizes $U(c_1) + \frac{\beta}{1+\delta}U(c_2)$ with Lagrangian
$$\mathcal{L}_1 = U(c_1) + \frac{\beta}{1+\delta}\,U(c_2) - \lambda\left(c_1 + \frac{c_2}{1+r} - M_1' - \frac{M_2}{1+r}\right).$$
The first-order conditions are $U'(c_1) = \lambda$ and $\frac{\beta}{1+\delta}U'(c_2) = \frac{\lambda}{1+r}$. Dividing the first by the second gives $U'(c_1^*)/U'(c_2^*) = \beta(1+r)/(1+\delta)$.

The self at $t = 0$ with commitment maximizes $U(c_0) + \frac{\beta}{1+\delta}U(c_1) + \frac{\beta}{(1+\delta)^2}U(c_2)$ over $c_1$ and $c_2$, subject to the same budget, with multiplier $\mu$. The first-order conditions are $\frac{\beta}{1+\delta}U'(c_1) = \mu$ and $\frac{\beta}{(1+\delta)^2}U'(c_2) = \frac{\mu}{1+r}$. The ratio cancels $\beta$ and one factor $1/(1+\delta)$, which leaves $U'(c_1^{*,c})/U'(c_2^{*,c}) = (1+r)/(1+\delta)$. Setting $\beta = 1$ in the problem at $t = 1$ gives the same condition, which is the time consistency of exponential discounting. Both objectives are strictly concave and the budget is linear, so each condition has one solution on the budget line.

For log utility, $U'(c) = 1/c$ turns the condition at $t = 1$ into $c_2 = \beta\frac{1+r}{1+\delta}\,c_1$. Substituting into the budget gives $c_1\big(1 + \frac{\beta}{1+\delta}\big) = W_1$, so $c_1^* = \frac{1+\delta}{1+\delta+\beta}W_1$. Setting $\beta = 1$ gives the plan $c_1^{*,c} = \frac{1+\delta}{2+\delta}W_1$. *(The log closed forms were not taught. The lecture stopped at the two ratio conditions.)*

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Ice Cream
>A person with $\beta$-$\delta$ preferences, $\delta = 0$ and $\beta = 0.5$, loves ice cream. Eating it at $t = 1$ gives utility $+10$ at $t = 1$ and a stomach ache worth $-16$ at $t = 2$.
>
>>[!example]- What is eating ice cream at $t = 1$ worth to the self at $t = 0$ and to the self at $t = 1$? Does a plan made at $t = 0$ survive? For which $\beta$ would it survive?
>>The self at $t = 0$ discounts both dates by $\beta = 0.5$, so the value is $0.5 \times 10 + 0.5 \times (-16) = -3$. She plans to skip the ice cream. The self at $t = 1$ gets the $+10$ now and discounts only the ache, so the value is $10 + 0.5 \times (-16) = +2$. She eats it, and the plan fails. An exponential discounter, $\beta = 1$, values the ice cream at $10 - 16 = -6$ at both dates and skips it both times. The self at $t = 1$ skips it when $10 - 16\beta \le 0$, that is, when $\beta \ge 0.625$.

>[!example] (Medium) A Bonus Spent Too Fast
>At $t = 1$ a worker has resources worth $W_1 = M_1' + M_2/(1+r) = 100$, in thousands of dollars. Her utility has $U = \ln c$, $r = \delta = 0.1$, and $\beta = 0.6$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the plan of the self at $t = 0$ and the choice of the self at $t = 1$. How much extra does she consume at $t = 1$?
>>The plan has $U'(c_1)/U'(c_2) = c_2/c_1 = (1+r)/(1+\delta) = 1$, so $c_1^{*,c} = c_2^{*,c}$. The log formula gives $c_1^{*,c} = \frac{1.1}{2.1}\times 100 = 52.38 = c_2^{*,c}$. Check: $52.38 + 52.38/1.1 = 100$.
>>
>>The self at $t = 1$ wants $c_2/c_1 = \beta(1+r)/(1+\delta) = 0.6$. The log formula gives $c_1^* = \frac{1.1}{1.7}\times 100 = 64.71$ and $c_2^* = 0.6 \times 64.71 = 38.82$. Check: $64.71 + 38.82/1.1 = 100$. She consumes $12.32$ more at $t = 1$ than planned and $13.56$ less at $t = 2$. The loss at $t = 2$ is larger because each unit saved at $t = 1$ would have grown by the factor $1 + r = 1.1$.

>[!example] (Hard) Paying for Commitment
>A person has resources $W_1 = 120$ at $t = 1$, with $U = \ln c$, $r = \delta = 0$, and $\beta = 0.5$. At $t = 0$ she can sign a contract that fixes her consumption at $t = 1$. The contract charges a fee $F$ at $t = 1$, which leaves $120 - F$ to split.
>
>>[!example]- What is the largest fee the self at $t = 0$ accepts? Would the self at $t = 1$ sign the same contract?
>>Without the contract, the self at $t = 1$ chooses $c_1^* = 120/1.5 = 80$ and $c_2^* = 40$. With $\delta = 0$ the self at $t = 0$ weights $c_1$ and $c_2$ equally, so she ranks plans by $\ln c_1 + \ln c_2$, and her plan splits what is left equally. With the contract she gets $2\ln\big((120 - F)/2\big)$. Without it she gets $\ln 80 + \ln 40 = \ln 3200$. She is indifferent when $(120 - F)/2 = \sqrt{3200} = 56.57$, that is, when $F = 6.86$. She pays up to $6.86$, about $5.7\%$ of her resources, to bind her later self.
>>
>>The self at $t = 1$ ranks plans by $\ln c_1 + 0.5\ln c_2$. Even a free contract gives her $1.5\ln 60 = 6.14$, less than $\ln 80 + 0.5\ln 40 = 6.23$ from her own choice. She would never sign. Commitment must be bought at $t = 0$, before the temptation arrives. A gym membership paid in advance works the same way.
