>[!note] Buoyancy
>**Buoyancy** is the upward force exerted by a fluid on an object that is partially or completely submerged. This force arises from the pressure difference between the top and bottom surfaces of the object, as pressure increases with depth in a fluid at [[1.7.2. Hydrostatic Equilibrium|hydrostatic equilibrium]]. The bottom of the object experiences a slightly higher pressure than the top, producing a net upward force.
>
>The magnitude of this upward force, called the buoyant force, depends on the density of the fluid and the volume of fluid displaced by the object, such that
>$$F_B = \rho_\text{f} V_\text{dis} g,$$
>where $V_\text{dis}$ is the displaced volume of the fluid by the solid, and $\rho_\text{f}$ is the density of the fluid.
>
>**Archimedes’ Principle** restates this result in conceptual form: the buoyant force on an immersed object equals the weight of the fluid displaced by that object. Therefore, for an object of mass $m_\text{obj}$ **in static equilibrium** within the fluid,
>$$F_B = W = m_\text{obj} g.$$
>If the object is floating, only part of it is submerged. Equating the object’s weight to the weight of displaced fluid gives
>$$\rho_\text{object} V_\text{sub} = \rho_\text{fluid} V_\text{disp} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{V_\text{sub}}{V_\text{total}} = \frac{\rho_\text{object}}{\rho_\text{fluid}}.$$
>
>This relationship determines the fraction of an object that remains below the surface and explains why less dense materials float more easily: 
>- When $F_B > W$, the object accelerates upward and floats. 
>- When $F_B < W$, the object accelerates downward and sinks. 
>- When $F_B = W,$ it remains suspended in static equilibrium, known as neutral buoyancy.
>

>[!example] (Easy) Brick and Styrofoam
>Consider the following situations:
>1. A 1.00 kg brick and a 1.00 kg styrofoam cube are placed side by side in a tank of water. (The brick sinks and the styrofoam floats).
>2. The 1.00 kg brick is placed on top of the 1.00 kg styrofoam cube.
>
>>[!example]- In which situation will the water level rise more? Or will it rise the same amount in either case?
>>In the first scenario, the brick sinks while the styrofoam stays afloat. We have that the displaced water volume is $V_{\text{dis}}=V_{\text{br}}+V_{\text{sty,dis}}$ since the whole brick is submerged, and only $V_{\text{sty,dis}}$ of the styrofoam's volume is submerged. To find the submerged part of the styrofoam, we act as if there is an imaginary chunk of water added, such that
>>$$F_{B}=\rho_{w}V_{\text{sty,dis}}g = \rho_{\text{sty}}V_{\text{sty}}g=W_{\text{sty}} \implies V_{\text{sty,dis}}=\frac{\rho_{\text{sty}}}{\rho_{w}}V_{\text{sty}}.$$
>>This is because the styrofoam is in static equilibrium (i.e., floating). As such, 
>>$$V_{\text{dis}}^{(a)}=V_{\text{br}}+\frac{\rho_{\text{sty}}}{\rho_{w}}V_{\text{sty}}.$$
>>
>>In the second scenario, we have that
>>$$\rho_{w}V_{\text{dis}}^{(b)}=\rho_{\text{br}}V_{\text{br}}+\rho_{\text{sty}}V_{\text{sty}} \implies V_{\text{dis}}^{(b)}=\frac{\rho_{\text{br}}V_{\text{br}}+\rho_{\text{sty}}V_{\text{sty}}}{\rho_{w}}.$$
>>Since $\rho_{\text{br}} > \rho_{w}$, it follows that $V_{\text{dis}}^{(b)}>V_{\text{dis}}^{(a)}$.

>>[!example] Find the ratio $y_{3} / y_{1}$.  
>>We can express the area of the block as $A=A_{1}+A_{3}=y_{1}x+y_{3}x$. Since buoyant forces are always upward and it is given that the block is in static equilibrium, we have by Archimedes' principle that
>>$$\rho_{1}y_{1}xg + \rho_{3}y_{3}xg=(y_{1}+y_{3})xg \implies \frac{y_{3}}{y_{1}}=\frac{\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}}{\rho_{3}-\rho_{2}}.$$
