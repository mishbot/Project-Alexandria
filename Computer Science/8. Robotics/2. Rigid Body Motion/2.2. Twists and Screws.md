> [!note] Twists and Screws
>**Chasles’ theorem** states that any rigid body displacement in three-dimensional space can be represented as a rotation about some axis combined with a translation along that same axis. In other words, every rigid motion is a **screw motion**.
>
>This geometric fact motivates the structure of the Lie algebra of $SE(3)$. The infinitesimal counterpart of $SE(3)$ is its Lie algebra $\mathfrak{se}(3)$, whose elements are called **twists**. A twist represents the instantaneous version of a screw motion and is written as a six-dimensional vector
>$$\xi = \begin{bmatrix} v\\ \omega \end{bmatrix},$$
>where $\omega\in\mathbb{R}^3$ represents angular velocity and $v\in\mathbb{R}^3$ represents linear velocity. These two components together describe a **screw motion**, meaning that at any instant a rigid body’s motion can be viewed as rotation about some axis combined with translation along that axis.
>
>Every twist can be converted into a $4\times4$ matrix using the “hat” operator,
>$$\hat{\xi} = \begin{bmatrix} [\omega]_\times & v\\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \in se(3),$$
> where $[\omega]_\times$ is the skew-symmetric matrix representing the cross product with $\omega$. The matrix exponential of this object produces a [[2.1. Rigid Motion|finite rigid motion]]:
>$$g(\theta) = \exp(\hat{\xi}\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}R(\theta) & p(\theta) \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \in SE(3),$$
> where $\theta$ is a scalar displacement parameter (an angle, distance, or just a scalar depending on the type of joint). Thus, the six numbers in $\xi$ define the **direction of motion**, while $\theta$ specifies how far to move along that direction.
> 
> Geometrically, a twist may correspond to pure rotation (zero pitch), pure translation (infinite pitch), or a general screw motion with finite pitch, defined as the translation per unit rotation. Because rigid motions are fundamentally screw-like, twists provide a unified way to describe all joint motions in robotics, which naturally leads to the classification of joint types.
> 
>Twists are not absolute objects: the same physical instantaneous motion has different coordinate representations depending on the reference frame. If a rigid transform $g\in SE(3)$ relates two frames, then the twist coordinates change by the **adjoint representation** of SE(3):
>
>$$\xi' = \mathrm{Ad}_g\,\xi, \qquad \mathrm{Ad}_g = \begin{bmatrix} R & [p]_\times R\\ 0 & R \end{bmatrix},$$
>which mixes the linear and angular components because translating the reference frame changes the linear velocity induced by a given angular velocity. This distinction also motivates the two standard conventions for expressing twists: a **spatial twist** is the instantaneous velocity of the body written in the fixed (space) frame, whereas a **body twist** is the same instantaneous velocity written in the moving (body) frame. If g(t) is the body pose, these are defined by
>$$\hat{\xi}_s = \dot g\,g^{-1},\qquad \hat{\xi}_b = g^{-1}\dot g,$$
> and they are related by the same adjoint map,
>$$\xi_s = \mathrm{Ad}_g\,\xi_b.$$
> Choosing spatial versus body coordinates does not change the underlying motion; it changes only the frame in which the 6D velocity is reported, and the adjoint provides the exact conversion between the two.

# Intuition
A twist is the infinitesimal generator of a screw motion, just as a skew-symmetric matrix $\hat{\omega} \in \mathfrak{so}(3)$ is the infinitesimal generator of a rotation. The six components of $\xi = (v, \omega)$ encode two things: the direction of the screw axis (determined by $\omega$ and $v$) and the type of motion (rotation, translation, or helical). The scalar $\theta$ says how far along that screw to move.

The classification by pitch $h = \omega^Tv/\|\omega\|^2$ captures the ratio of translation to rotation along the screw axis. A door hinge has $h = 0$ (pure rotation); a sliding drawer has $h = \infty$ (pure translation); a corkscrew or bolt has finite $h$ (helical motion). Every possible rigid body motion falls into one of these three categories (Chasles' theorem), so twists provide a universal description of joint motions.

# Properties

## Twist Classification

#### (*Revolute Twist*) Pitch $h = 0$, $\|\omega\| = 1$. The twist has the form $\xi = \begin{bmatrix} -\omega \times q \\ \omega \end{bmatrix}$ where $q$ is any point on the rotation axis. The linear component $v = -\omega \times q$ encodes the axis location, not a translational velocity.

#### (*Prismatic Twist*) $\omega = 0$, $\|v\| = 1$. The twist $\xi = \begin{bmatrix} v \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ represents pure translation along $v$. The exponential is $e^{\hat{\xi}\theta} = \begin{bmatrix} I & v\theta \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

#### (*Helical Twist*) Pitch $h = \omega^Tv / \|\omega\|^2 \neq 0$. The motion combines rotation by $\theta$ about an axis with translation by $h\theta$ along that axis.

## Key Identities

#### (*Twist Exponential Conjugation*) For $g \in SE(3)$ and twist $\xi$:
$$g\,e^{\hat{\xi}\theta}\,g^{-1} = e^{\widehat{\mathrm{Ad}_g\,\xi}\,\theta}.$$
This says that conjugating a screw motion by a rigid transform is the same as transforming the twist by the adjoint. It is the $SE(3)$ analogue of the [[1.4. Rodrigues' Formula#Hat conjugation identity|hat conjugation identity]] for $SO(3)$.

#### (*Adjoint Properties*) The adjoint satisfies: $\mathrm{Ad}_{g_1 g_2} = \mathrm{Ad}_{g_1}\mathrm{Ad}_{g_2}$ (composition), $\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}} = (\mathrm{Ad}_g)^{-1}$ (inverse), and $\det(\mathrm{Ad}_g) = 1$ (volume-preserving). See [[5.1. Rigid Body Velocity#The Adjoint Map|adjoint map]] for the explicit formula and its role in velocity kinematics.

#### (*Recovering the Axis*) For a revolute twist $\xi = (-\omega \times q,\, \omega)$, the axis direction is $\omega$ and a point on the axis is $q = \omega \times v / \|\omega\|^2$ (when $\omega^T v = 0$, i.e., zero pitch).

# Examples
>[!example] Constructing a Revolute Twist
>A joint rotates about the $z$-axis through the point $q = (a, 0, 0)$:
>>[!example]- Twist computation
>>$\omega = (0, 0, 1)$, $v = -\omega \times q = -(0, 0, 1) \times (a, 0, 0) = (0, a, 0)$.
>>
>>So $\xi = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ a \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Exponentiating: $e^{\hat{\xi}\theta}$ gives a rotation by $\theta$ about the $z$-axis through $(a, 0, 0)$.
>>
>>If instead the axis passes through the origin ($q = 0$), then $v = 0$ and $\xi = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$—a pure rotation about $z$.
