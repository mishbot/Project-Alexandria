>[!note] N-Gram Language Models
>An $n$-gram model is a [[2.1. Language Models|language model]] that estimates the probability of a token based on the previous $n-1$ tokens using frequency statistics from a corpus. It assumes a [[4.3a Markov Chains| finite Markov dependency]] structure over sequences. Formally, for a token sequence $w_1, w_2, \dots, w_T$,
>$$\mathbb{P}(w_1,\dots,w_T)=\prod_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb{P}(w_t \mid w_{t-n+1},\dots,w_{t-1}),$$
>giving an $n$-step approximation of the full distribution $\mathbb{P}(w_t \mid w_1,\dots,w_{t-1})$. In turn, this results in loss of long-range dependencies in exchange for reduced complexity. 
>
>Parameter estimation in n-gram models is grounded in empirical frequency counts from a corpus. Using maximum likelihood estimation, conditional probabilities are computed as normalized counts, such that
>$$P(w_t \mid h)=\frac{C(h,w_t) + \alpha}{C(h) + \alpha|V|},$$
>where $h=\{ w_{t-n+1},\dots,w_{t-1} \}$ denotes the **context**, $|V|$ is the size of the vocabulary, and $\alpha\geq0$ is a Laplace smoothing constant that alleviates the **sparsity problem**, where many plausible $n$-grams receive zero probability as $N \to \infty$, simply because they were not observed in the training data.
