>[!note] Itô's Lemma
>**Itô's lemma** is the chain rule for a function of a randomly moving quantity, such as an option price that depends on a stock price. Let $x$ follow the [[7.3 Ito Process|Itô process]] $dx = a(x, t)\,dt + b(x, t)\,dW_t$. Let $G(x, t)$ have continuous [[2.3. Partial Derivatives|partial derivatives]] $\partial G/\partial t$, $\partial G/\partial x$, and $\partial^2 G/\partial x^2$. Then $G$ follows the Itô process
>$$dG = \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial t} + a\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{2}b^2\,\frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial x^2}\right)dt + b\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial x}\,dW_t,$$
>with all derivatives evaluated at $(x_t, t)$. Written with $dx$, the same result is
>$$dG = \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}b^2\,\frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial x^2}\right)dt + \frac{\partial G}{\partial x}\,dx.$$
>Ordinary calculus gives every term except $\frac{1}{2}b^2\,(\partial^2 G/\partial x^2)\,dt$. This extra term appears because $(\Delta W)^2$ behaves like $\Delta t$ in sums ([[7.2 Quadratic Variation]]), so a second-order term survives. The same $dW_t$ drives both $x$ and $G$. For $x = W$, so that $a = 0$ and $b = 1$, the lemma gives $d(W_t^2) = 2W_t\,dW_t + dt$ and $d(tW_t) = t\,dW_t + W_t\,dt$. For a stock with $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t$ it gives $d(\ln S) = (\mu - \sigma^2/2)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t$. The lemma evaluates [[7.4 Ito Integral|Itô integrals]], solves [[7.6 Geometric Brownian Motion|geometric Brownian motion]], and leads to the [[2.3.3e Black-Scholes-Merton Equation]].

# Intuition

A trader holds a call option on a stock. She knows how the stock moves, $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t$. Her profit depends on the call price $c(S, t)$, so she needs to know how $c$ moves. Ordinary calculus offers the chain rule, $dc = (\partial c/\partial t)\,dt + (\partial c/\partial S)\,dS$.

She tests the chain rule on one day. A one-month call with strike $\$100$ is written on a stock at $\$100$ with volatility $20\%$, and the interest rate is $0$. The [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]] prices it at $\$2.30$. Suppose the stock moves to $\$98$ or $\$102$ with equal probability. Ignore the day of time decay, which the $\partial c/\partial t$ term handles. The call is then worth $\$1.42$ or $\$3.46$. The two stock moves cancel on average, so the chain rule predicts no average change in $c$. But the up move raises the call by $\$1.16$, and the down move lowers it by only $\$0.88$. On average the call gains $\$0.14$, a drift that the chain rule misses.

The call price curves upward as a function of $S$. The chain rule follows the tangent line, which treats an up move and a down move as mirror images. A curve that bends upward lies above its tangent on both sides. So both outcomes beat the tangent, and the average beats it too.

The figure shows a convex function $G$ of $x$, such as the call price as a function of $S$. Write $G''$ for $\partial^2 G/\partial x^2$. Suppose $x$ moves from $x_0$ to $x_0 - h$ or to $x_0 + h$ with equal probability. The tangent line predicts an average of $G(x_0)$, since the two first-order changes cancel. The average of $G(x_0 - h)$ and $G(x_0 + h)$ is higher, by about $\frac{1}{2}G''h^2$. For the call at $\$100$, $\partial^2 c/\partial S^2 = 0.0691$ and $h = 2$, so the gap is $\frac{1}{2}(0.0691)(2^2) = \$0.14$, the drift found above.

In ordinary calculus this second-order gap vanishes as the step shrinks. A smooth path moves by an amount proportional to $\Delta t$, so $h^2$ is proportional to $(\Delta t)^2$, and the gaps add up to $0$. A stock moves by about $\sigma S\sqrt{\Delta t}$ in a step of length $\Delta t$. So $h^2$ is about $\sigma^2S^2\,\Delta t$, the same order as the drift term. [[7.2 Quadratic Variation]] shows that squared Wiener moves add up to the elapsed time, so these gaps do not vanish in the limit. In a short step of a general Itô process, $h^2$ is on average $b^2\,\Delta t$, and the extra drift is $\frac{1}{2}b^2G''\,\Delta t$.

Itô's lemma is the chain rule with this extra drift added. In the callout, the terms $\partial G/\partial t$, $a\,\partial G/\partial x$, and $b\,(\partial G/\partial x)\,dW_t$ come from the ordinary chain rule applied to $dx = a\,dt + b\,dW_t$. The curvature step adds $\frac{1}{2}b^2\,\partial^2 G/\partial x^2$ to the drift. It adds nothing to the $dW_t$ term, because a sum of squared moves is close to its mean.

# Properties

#### *(Replacing $(\Delta W)^2$ by $\Delta t$)* The lemma may replace each squared step by $\Delta t$ because the squared steps enter only through a weighted sum.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules justify the replacement for one step. The weighted-sum argument and the shortcut $(dx)^2 = b^2\,dt$ are not stated.

One squared step is not close to $\Delta t$, since its standard deviation $\sqrt{2}\,\Delta t$ exceeds its mean. Write $G_t$, $G_x$, and $G_{xx}$ for the partial derivatives. Over an interval, the change in $G$ is a sum over many steps, and its second-order part is $\sum_j \frac{1}{2}G_{xx}b^2\,(\Delta W_j)^2$. Here $G_{xx}$ and $b$ are taken at the start of each step, and the weight $\frac{1}{2}G_{xx}b^2$ changes little over a short stretch of time. On such a stretch the sum is close to the weight times $\sum_j (\Delta W_j)^2$. By [[7.2 Quadratic Variation]], that sum is close to the length of the stretch. Adding up the stretches gives $\int \frac{1}{2}G_{xx}b^2\,dt$, the extra drift in the callout. In practice the lemma is applied in one line. Expand $dG = G_t\,dt + G_x\,dx + \frac{1}{2}G_{xx}\,(dx)^2$, and use the rules $(dt)^2 = 0$, $dt\,dW_t = 0$, and $(dW_t)^2 = dt$ from that note to get $(dx)^2 = b^2\,dt$.

#### *(Evaluating Itô Integrals)* To compute $\int_0^t f(W_s, s)\,dW_s$, choose $G$ with $\partial G/\partial x = f$ and apply the lemma.

With $x = W$ the lemma reads $dG = (G_t + \frac{1}{2}G_{xx})\,dt + G_x\,dW_t$, where subscripts denote partial derivatives. Integrating from $0$ to $t$ and solving for the $dW$ part gives
$$\int_0^t G_x(W_s, s)\,dW_s = G(W_t, t) - G(W_0, 0) - \int_0^t \left(G_t + \frac{1}{2}G_{xx}\right)(W_s, s)\,ds.$$
The right side has no stochastic integral. For $G = x^2$, the lemma gives $d(W_t^2) = 2W_t\,dW_t + dt$. With $W_0 = 0$ this yields $\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s = \frac{1}{2}W_t^2 - \frac{1}{2}t$, the limit found from sums in [[7.4 Ito Integral]]. For $G = tx$, the derivatives are $G_t = x$, $G_x = t$, and $G_{xx} = 0$. So $d(tW_t) = t\,dW_t + W_t\,dt$, and $\int_0^t s\,dW_s = tW_t - \int_0^t W_s\,ds$. This second case has no extra term, because $G$ is linear in $x$.

#### *(Stock Price Form)* For $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t$, the lemma takes the form used in option pricing.

Substitute $a = \mu S$ and $b = \sigma S$:
$$dG = \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial t} + \mu S\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\,\frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial S^2}\right)dt + \sigma S\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial S}\,dW_t.$$
For $G = \ln S$, the derivatives are $\partial G/\partial S = 1/S$ and $\partial^2 G/\partial S^2 = -1/S^2$, so
$$d(\ln S) = \left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)dt + \sigma\,dW_t.$$
The log price is a generalized Wiener process with constant coefficients. [[7.6 Geometric Brownian Motion]] uses this result to find the distribution of $S_t$.

#### *(Convexity Term)* The extra drift has the sign of $\partial^2 G/\partial x^2$.

For convex $G$ the extra term is positive, so $G$ drifts up faster than ordinary calculus predicts. For concave $G$ it is negative. The log function is concave. For a stock, the previous property gives $\mathbb{E}[\ln S_t] = \ln S_0 + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)t$. The mean price gives $\ln\mathbb{E}[S_t] = \ln S_0 + \mu t$. The gap of $\sigma^2t/2$ is the extra term $\frac{1}{2}b^2\,\partial^2 G/\partial S^2 = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2(-1/S^2) = -\sigma^2/2$, accumulated over time $t$. For linear $G$ the second derivative is $0$, and ordinary calculus is exact.

#### *(Hedging)* A position long $G$ and short $\partial G/\partial x$ units of $x$ has no $dW_t$ term.

The random part of $dG$ is $b\,(\partial G/\partial x)\,dW_t$, and the random part of $dx$ is $b\,dW_t$. Both come from the same $dW_t$. Hold $\partial G/\partial x$ fixed over a short step. The position $G - (\partial G/\partial x)\,x$ then changes by
$$dG - \frac{\partial G}{\partial x}\,dx = \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}b^2\,\frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial x^2}\right)dt,$$
which has no random term over that step. With $x = S$ and $G$ an option price $f(S, t)$, the position is long one option and short $\partial f/\partial S$ shares. The option writer in [[2.3.3d Delta Hedging]] holds the opposite position. A riskless portfolio must earn the risk-free rate, and this condition gives the [[2.3.3e Black-Scholes-Merton Equation]].

# Derivation

We want the change in $G(x, t)$ over a short step $\Delta t$. A second-order Taylor expansion gives
$$\Delta G = \frac{\partial G}{\partial t}\Delta t + \frac{\partial G}{\partial x}\Delta x + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial t^2}(\Delta t)^2 + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial x^2}(\Delta x)^2 + \frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial t\,\partial x}\Delta t\,\Delta x + \cdots$$
Here $\Delta x = a\,\Delta t + b\,\Delta W$ with $\Delta W = \varepsilon\sqrt{\Delta t}$. We keep terms up to order $\Delta t$. The term in $(\Delta t)^2$ has order $(\Delta t)^2$, and $\Delta t\,\Delta x$ has order $(\Delta t)^{3/2}$, so both drop. In $(\Delta x)^2 = a^2(\Delta t)^2 + 2ab\,\Delta t\,\Delta W + b^2(\Delta W)^2$, only the last term has order $\Delta t$. This leaves
$$\Delta G = \frac{\partial G}{\partial t}\Delta t + \frac{\partial G}{\partial x}\big(a\,\Delta t + b\,\Delta W\big) + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial x^2}\,b^2(\Delta W)^2.$$
The square $(\Delta W)^2$ is random, with mean $\Delta t$ and variance $2(\Delta t)^2$ ([[7.2 Quadratic Variation]]). Its spread is of order $\Delta t$, much smaller than the size $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ of $\Delta W$ itself. Over many steps the squares add up to the elapsed time, so we replace $(\Delta W)^2$ by $\Delta t$. Collecting the $\Delta t$ terms gives
$$\Delta G = \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial t} + a\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{2}b^2\,\frac{\partial^2 G}{\partial x^2}\right)\Delta t + b\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial x}\,\Delta W.$$
Letting $\Delta t \to 0$ gives the lemma in the callout.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) An Exponential of $W$
>A toy index starts at $1$ and equals $Y_t = e^{W_t}$, with time in years.
>
>>[!example]- Find $dY$, and check that its drift agrees with the lognormal mean of $Y_1$.
>>Take $G(x, t) = e^x$ with $x = W$, so $a = 0$ and $b = 1$. The derivatives are $G_t = 0$, $G_x = e^x$, and $G_{xx} = e^x$. The lemma gives
>>$$dY = \frac{1}{2}e^{W_t}\,dt + e^{W_t}\,dW_t = \frac{1}{2}Y\,dt + Y\,dW_t.$$
>>So $Y$ is a geometric Brownian motion with drift $1/2$ and volatility $1$. Ordinary calculus would give drift $0$. A geometric Brownian motion with drift $\mu$ has mean $Y_0e^{\mu t}$ ([[7.6 Geometric Brownian Motion]]). Here this gives $\mathbb{E}[Y_1] = e^{1/2} = 1.6487$. Directly, $Y_1 = e^{W_1}$ is [[2.2c.11 Lognormal Distribution|lognormal]] with parameters $(0, 1)$, so its mean is $e^{0 + 1/2} = 1.6487$. The two answers agree.

>[!example] (Medium) The Cube of $W$
>The value of a contract, in dollars, is $V_t = W_t^3$, where $W_t$ is a Wiener process with time in years. Today $W_t = 0.5$.
>
>>[!example]- Find $d(W_t^3)$, and write $\int_0^t W_s^2\,dW_s$ without a stochastic integral. Then find the mean and the standard deviation of the change in $V$ over the next day.
>>Take $G = x^3$ with $x = W$, so $a = 0$ and $b = 1$. The derivatives are $G_t = 0$, $G_x = 3x^2$, and $G_{xx} = 6x$. The lemma gives
>>$$d(W_t^3) = 3W_t\,dt + 3W_t^2\,dW_t.$$
>>Integrate from $0$ to $t$ with $W_0 = 0$, and divide by $3$:
>>$$\int_0^t W_s^2\,dW_s = \frac{1}{3}W_t^3 - \int_0^t W_s\,ds.$$
>>Over one day, $\Delta t = 1/250 = 0.004$. At $W = 0.5$ the mean change is $3 \times 0.5 \times 0.004 = \$0.006$. The standard deviation is $3 \times 0.5^2 \times \sqrt{0.004} = \$0.0474$. Ordinary calculus would give a mean change of $0$, since $dW_t$ has mean $0$. The drift $3W_t$ is positive here because $x^3$ is convex for $x > 0$.

>[!example] (Hard) The Square of a Stock Price
>A stock trades at $S_0 = \$50$ and follows $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t$ with $\mu = 0.08$ and $\sigma = 0.25$. A contract pays $S_1^2$ dollars in one year.
>
>>[!example]- Find $d(S^2)$ and show that $S^2$ is a geometric Brownian motion. Use this to find $\mathbb{E}[S_1^2]$ and the standard deviation of $S_1$.
>>Take $G = S^2$, so $G_t = 0$, $G_S = 2S$, and $G_{SS} = 2$. The stock price form of the lemma gives
>>$$d(S^2) = \left(\mu S \cdot 2S + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2 \cdot 2\right)dt + \sigma S \cdot 2S\,dW_t = (2\mu + \sigma^2)\,S^2\,dt + 2\sigma\,S^2\,dW_t.$$
>>So $S^2$ is a geometric Brownian motion with drift $2\mu + \sigma^2 = 0.2225$ and volatility $2\sigma = 0.5$. Its mean grows at its drift, so
>>$$\mathbb{E}[S_1^2] = 50^2e^{0.2225} = 3122.99.$$
>>The stock itself has $\mathbb{E}[S_1] = 50e^{0.08} = \$54.16$. So $\operatorname{Var}(S_1) = 3122.99 - 54.16^2 = 3122.99 - 2933.78 = 189.21$, and the standard deviation is $\$13.76$. This matches the variance formula $S_0^2e^{2\mu t}(e^{\sigma^2t} - 1)$ of geometric Brownian motion.
