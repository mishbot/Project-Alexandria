>[!note] Root-Mean-Square Speed
>The **root-mean-square speed** of the molecules in a gas is the square root of the average of the squared speeds,
>$$v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\overline{v^2}}.$$
>It is the speed a molecule would need for its kinetic energy to equal the average kinetic energy $\overline{K} = \tfrac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2}$. Substituting the [[2.3.1 Molecular Interpretation of Temperature|molecular interpretation of temperature]] $\tfrac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2} = \tfrac{3}{2} kT$ gives
>$$v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3kT}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}},$$
>where $m$ is the mass of one molecule, $M$ is the molar mass in kilograms per mole, $T$ is in kelvins, $k$ is Boltzmann's constant, and $R$ is the gas constant. The two forms are equal because $M = N_A m$ and $R = N_A k$.
>
>The rms speed grows with the square root of the temperature and falls with the square root of the mass. At $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$, hydrogen molecules have $v_{\text{rms}} = 1900\ \text{m/s}$, helium atoms $1350\ \text{m/s}$, nitrogen molecules $511\ \text{m/s}$, oxygen molecules $478\ \text{m/s}$, and carbon dioxide molecules $408\ \text{m/s}$. Air molecules move at about $500\ \text{m/s}$, somewhat faster than sound.
>
>The rms speed is not the average speed. Molecules in a gas have a spread of speeds, described by the [[2.3.3 Maxwell Speed Distribution|Maxwell distribution]]. The average speed is $\overline{v} = \sqrt{8kT/\pi m}$, about $8$ percent below $v_{\text{rms}}$, and the most common speed is lower still. The rms value is the one that kinetic energy and pressure depend on.

# Intuition

The obvious average of molecular motion is useless. Molecules move in every direction with equal likelihood, so the average velocity vector of a gas at rest is zero. An average of speeds ignores direction and is not zero, but it is not the quantity that appears in the physics. Kinetic energy depends on $v^2$. So does pressure, because a faster molecule both hits the wall more often and hits it harder. The natural average is therefore the average of $v^2$. Taking its square root turns the result back into a speed. The diagram shows five velocity vectors that sum to zero, together with their speeds and the two averages.

The formula says two things about any gas. Heating it makes the molecules faster, but slowly. Doubling the absolute temperature raises the rms speed by only $41$ percent. Lighter molecules are faster at the same temperature. A helium atom has one seventh the mass of a nitrogen molecule and moves $2.6$ times as fast. Both facts follow from the rule that every molecule, light or heavy, carries the same average kinetic energy at a given temperature.

# Properties

#### *(Four Speeds, One Gas)* A gas has $10^{22}$ molecular velocities, and there is no unique way to replace them with one number.

Four candidates come up. The mean velocity vector is zero. The mean speed $\overline{v} = \sqrt{8kT/\pi m}$ is the average of the magnitudes. The rms speed $\sqrt{3kT/m}$ is the average that energy depends on. The most probable speed $\sqrt{2kT/m}$ is the peak of the distribution. All four describe the same gas, and the question decides which one to use. A question about momentum or net flow uses the mean velocity. A question about how far molecules travel uses the mean speed. A question about energy, pressure, or temperature uses $v_{\text{rms}}$. Thermodynamics is the first subject in the course where the right average depends on what is being asked.

#### *(A Standard Deviation)* The rms speed is the standard deviation of the molecular velocity.

The general definition of an rms value is $\sqrt{\overline{(\vec{v} - \overline{\vec{v}})^2}}$, the root of the mean squared difference from the mean. Expanding the square gives an equivalent form. The cross term averages to $-2\,\overline{\vec v} \cdot \overline{\vec v}$, so
$$\overline{(\vec{v} - \overline{\vec{v}})^2} = \overline{v^2} - \overline{\vec{v}} \cdot \overline{\vec{v}}, \qquad v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\overline{v^2} - \overline{\vec{v}}^{\,2}}.$$
For a gas at rest the mean velocity $\overline{\vec{v}}$ is zero, so both forms reduce to $\sqrt{\overline{v^2}}$. The quantity inside the root is how much a single velocity measurement differs from the average, squared and averaged, which is the variance of the velocity. So $v_{\text{rms}}$ is a [[2.5b Second-Order Moments|standard deviation]] and measures the spread of velocities, not a typical speed. Every velocity is a deviation from zero, and $v_{\text{rms}}$ is their typical size.

#### *(Scaling)* The rms speed changes with $\sqrt{T}$ and with $1/\sqrt{m}$, so ratios need no constants.

For two gases at the same temperature, $v_1 / v_2 = \sqrt{m_2 / m_1}$. For one gas at two temperatures, $v_1 / v_2 = \sqrt{T_1 / T_2}$. Two gases have the same rms speed when $T_1 / m_1 = T_2 / m_2$, so the heavier gas must be hotter in proportion to its mass. Ratios of this kind answer most problems without a value for $k$ or $R$.

#### *(Speed of Sound)* Sound travels through a gas at about two thirds of the rms speed.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not connect the rms speed to the speed of sound. This property follows Giancoli section 18.1.

A sound wave moves by molecules passing their motion on to their neighbors through collisions, so it cannot outrun the molecules themselves. The [[1.1w.1 Sound|speed of sound]] in a gas is $\sqrt{\gamma kT/m}$, where $\gamma$ is a constant near $1.4$ for air. The sound speed is therefore about $\sqrt{\gamma/3} \approx 0.68$ of $v_{\text{rms}}$. In air at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ the two speeds are $343\ \text{m/s}$ and $510\ \text{m/s}$. Both scale as $\sqrt{T/m}$, which is why sound is faster in warm air and in helium.

#### *(Escape from a Planet)* Light gases leak out of an atmosphere because their fastest molecules exceed the escape speed.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover atmospheric escape. This property follows Giancoli section 18.2.

Earth's escape speed is $11.2\ \text{km/s}$. At $300\ \text{K}$ the rms speed of hydrogen is about $1.9\ \text{km/s}$, far below that. The rms speed is only an average, and the Maxwell distribution gives a small fraction of molecules speeds several times larger. Over geological time, enough hydrogen and helium molecules at the top of the atmosphere have exceeded $11.2\ \text{km/s}$ to escape. Earth has lost nearly all of both. Nitrogen and oxygen are $14$ to $16$ times heavier, their fast tail is far shorter, and they stay. The Moon, with an escape speed of $2.4\ \text{km/s}$, has lost every gas.

#### *(Free Flight)* In an evacuated tube a molecule travels at its thermal speed in a straight line.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover free flight or the mean free path. This property follows Giancoli section 18.6.

When the [[2.3.6 Mean Free Path|mean free path]] exceeds the size of the container, molecules cross it without colliding. A gas released into such a tube advances at the speed of its molecules. Two gases released at once therefore cover distances in the ratio of their rms speeds. In air at atmospheric pressure the picture fails. A molecule collides every $10^{-10}\ \text{s}$ and wanders in a random walk. A released gas therefore spreads far more slowly than $500\ \text{m/s}$ would suggest.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Nitrogen and Helium at Room Temperature
>The molar masses of nitrogen gas and helium are $28.0\ \text{g/mol}$ and $4.00\ \text{g/mol}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the rms speed of each at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>>The molar form of the formula uses $M$ in kilograms per mole,
>>$$v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}} = \sqrt{\frac{3\,(8.314\ \text{J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}))(293.15\ \text{K})}{0.0280\ \text{kg/mol}}} = 511\ \text{m/s}$$
>>for nitrogen. Helium has one seventh of the molar mass, so its speed is $\sqrt{7} = 2.65$ times larger, $v_{\text{rms}} = 1350\ \text{m/s}$.

>[!example] (Medium) Two Gases Released into a Tube
>A tube of length $L$ has been pumped out, so it contains almost no gas. A container of oxygen at one end and a container of hydrogen at the other end are opened at the same moment. Both containers are at the same temperature, and the tube is long enough that the molecules fly without colliding.
>
>>[!example]- Where along the tube do the two gases meet?
>>Each gas advances at the rms speed of its molecules. Both gases travel for the same time until they meet, so the distances are in the ratio of the speeds. At equal temperature the speeds go as $1/\sqrt{m}$, so
>>$$\frac{d_{\text{O}_2}}{d_{\text{H}_2}} = \frac{v_{\text{O}_2}}{v_{\text{H}_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{m_{\text{H}_2}}{m_{\text{O}_2}}} = \sqrt{\frac{2.0}{32.0}} = \frac{1}{4}.$$
>>The distances add to $L$, so $d_{\text{O}_2} = L/5$ and $d_{\text{H}_2} = 4L/5$. The gases meet one fifth of the way from the oxygen end. The result is a ratio, so no temperature value is needed.

>[!example] (Medium) Matching Speeds with Temperature
>A sample of helium and a sample of nitrogen are to have the same rms speed. The nitrogen is at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- What temperature must the helium have?
>>Equal rms speeds require equal values of $T/m$, so
>>$$T_{\text{He}} = T_{\text{N}_2}\,\frac{m_{\text{He}}}{m_{\text{N}_2}} = (293.15\ \text{K})\,\frac{4.00}{28.0} = 41.9\ \text{K},$$
>>which is $-231\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Helium must be very cold to move as slowly as room-temperature nitrogen. At this temperature helium is still a gas, since it liquefies only at $4.2\ \text{K}$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover escape speed from a planet. This example follows Giancoli section 18.2.

>[!example] (Hard) Oxygen at Escape Speed
>Earth's escape speed is $11.2\ \text{km/s}$.
>
>>[!example]- At what temperature would the rms speed of oxygen molecules equal the escape speed?
>>Solving the rms formula for $T$ with $m = 32.0 \times 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg} = 5.31 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg}$ gives
>>$$T = \frac{m v_{\text{rms}}^2}{3k} = \frac{(5.31 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg})(1.12 \times 10^{4}\ \text{m/s})^2}{3\,(1.381 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K})} = 1.6 \times 10^{5}\ \text{K}.$$
>>No part of the atmosphere is anywhere near this hot, so oxygen does not escape in bulk. Escape of the fastest few molecules still happens at ordinary temperatures, but for oxygen the rate is negligible. For hydrogen the required temperature is sixteen times lower, and the fast tail of the distribution matters.
