>[!note] Electric Potential
>This section describes electric forces with energy. In a uniform field the electric force does the same work on every path, so it is conservative. It therefore has an [[3.4.1. Electric Potential Energy|electric potential energy]], $U = -q_0Ex + C$ for a test charge $q_0$, with a freely chosen zero. Dividing by the test charge gives the [[3.4.2. Electric Potential|electric potential]] $V = U/q_0$, in volts, with $U = q_0V$. The source charges alone set $V$. The potential follows [[3.4.3. Electric Potential from Electric Field|from the field]] as $V_B - V_A = -\int_A^B \vec E\cdot d\vec s$. That integral needs the field in a whole region, usually from Gauss's law.
>
>A [[3.4.4. Electric Potential of a Point Charge|point charge]] makes $V = q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$, zero at infinity. Potentials of several charges add as signed numbers. Two opposite charges seen from far away make the [[3.4.4a Electric Dipole Potential|dipole potential]] $p\cos\theta/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$. Charge spread over a line or surface gives its potential by [[3.4.4b Electric Potential of a Continuous Charge Distribution|slice and dice]], with no components. The [[3.4.5. Electric Field from Electric Potential|field follows from the potential]] as $\vec E = -\nabla V$. A conductor has one potential throughout, and the field crosses every [[3.4.6. Equipotential Surfaces|equipotential surface]] at a right angle. A group of point charges stores the [[3.4.7. Electrostatic Potential Energy of a System of Charges|electrostatic potential energy]] $\sum q_i q_j/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_{ij})$, summed over pairs.

# Contents

- [[3.4.1. Electric Potential Energy]]: work by a uniform field, path independence, $\Delta U = -W$, the free choice of zero, and the four graphs. **Not taught:** nothing.
- [[3.4.2. Electric Potential]]: $V = U/q_0$, the volt, $U = q_0V$, which way charges move, and the confusing name. **Not taught:** the electron-volt.
- [[3.4.3. Electric Potential from Electric Field]]: $V_B - V_A = -\int_A^B \vec E\cdot d\vec s$, its sign rule, a uniform field, an infinite line, and a conducting shell. **Not taught:** a shell with its own net charge, and the potential of a uniformly charged ball.
- [[3.4.4. Electric Potential of a Point Charge]]: $V = q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$ with the zero at infinity, and superposition of potentials as signed numbers. **Not taught:** nothing.
    - [[3.4.4a Electric Dipole Potential]]: the exact potential, the binomial approximation, and $p\cos\theta/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$. **Not taught:** nothing.
    - [[3.4.4b Electric Potential of a Continuous Charge Distribution]]: slice and dice for $V$, the ring, the finite rod, and the spherical cap. **Not taught:** nothing.
- [[3.4.5. Electric Field from Electric Potential]]: $\vec E = -\nabla V$, the ring's axial field, and the three ways to find $\vec E$. **Not taught:** the full dipole field from its potential.
- [[3.4.6. Equipotential Surfaces]]: a conductor at one potential, equipotentials perpendicular to $\vec E$, and equipotential maps. **Not taught:** equipotential surfaces in general, their right angle with the field, and equipotential maps.
- [[3.4.7. Electrostatic Potential Energy of a System of Charges]]: the pair energy $q_1 q_2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_{12})$ and the sum over pairs. **Not taught:** the whole note.

# Recommended Path

Read [[3.4.1. Electric Potential Energy]] to [[3.4.3. Electric Potential from Electric Field]] in order. They define the potential and its sign rule. The three notes under [[3.4.4. Electric Potential of a Point Charge]] hold the core calculations and need the most practice. [[3.4.5. Electric Field from Electric Potential]] reverses the integral of 3.4.3. [[3.4.6. Equipotential Surfaces]] and [[3.4.7. Electrostatic Potential Energy of a System of Charges]] are short and come last.
