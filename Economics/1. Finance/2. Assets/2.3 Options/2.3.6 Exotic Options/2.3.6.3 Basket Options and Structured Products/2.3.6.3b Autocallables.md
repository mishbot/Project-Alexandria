>[!note] Autocallables
>An **autocallable** is a structured note, a bond with options built into one security. It pays a coupon on each observation date when the index is at or above a coupon trigger. It repays the principal early once the index is above a higher redemption trigger. Let $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = T$ be the observation dates, $S_{t_i}$ the index level at $t_i$, $L$ the notional, and $H$ the coupon. An autocallable with coupon trigger $K_1$ and redemption (call) trigger $K_2 > K_1$ makes these payments at $t_i$,
>$$\text{coupon}(t_i) = H\,I_{K_1}\Big(\frac{S_{t_i}}{S_0}\Big) \ \text{ if not redeemed before } t_i, \qquad \text{redemption}(t_i) = L \ \text{ if } \ \frac{S_{t_i}}{S_0} > K_2.$$
>The indicator $I_K(x)$ is $1$ when $x \ge K$ and $0$ otherwise. A note that is never called repays its principal at $T$ by a rule $R(S_T)$. The note holds three kinds of option.
>- Each coupon is a cash binary on the index that an early call can cancel ([[2.3.6.1b Binary Options]]).
>- The redemption trigger gives the issuer the right to end the note early.
>- Without principal protection, the principal at $T$ holds a short down-and-in put ([[2.3.6.2a Barrier Options]]). A [[2.3.6.3e Reverse Convertible Notes|reverse convertible note]] sells the same put.

# Intuition

A plain bond pays a fixed coupon whatever the market does. Some investors expect an index to move sideways or fall a little. They want more income than a bond pays. An autocallable pays them a high coupon while the index stays above the coupon trigger. The investor pays for it in two ways. The issuer ends the note when the index rises, so the income stops in the best markets. A large fall by maturity also costs principal. The premiums of these two sold options fund the high coupon.

# Properties

#### *(Autocallable Schedule)* An autocallable pays conditional coupons until it is called, and a large fall by maturity costs principal.

>[!warning] Notional and Coupon Symbols
>The lecture writes the notional as $N$ and the coupon as $mN$, with $m$ the coupon rate per period. This note writes the notional as $L$ and the coupon as $H = mL$, so that $N$ stays the normal CDF.

The word "callable" refers to an option that the note holders sell to the issuer. It lets the issuer redeem the note when the conditions are met. In return, the note pays a higher coupon than a plain bond, and the coupon is conditional. Let $\tau$ be the first eligible call date with $S_{t_i}/S_0 > K_2$, or $\tau = \infty$ if there is none. The coupon at $t_i$ is
$$\text{coupon}(t_i) = H\,\mathbf{1}\{\tau \ge t_i\}\,I_{K_1}\Big(\frac{S_{t_i}}{S_0}\Big).$$
The first indicator pays the coupon on the call date and stops all later ones. The second checks the coupon trigger. On the date $\tau$ the note pays $L + H$ and ends. A coupon missed below $K_1$ is not made up later. A note that is never called pays its principal rule $R(S_T)$ plus the last coupon at $T$.

The animation follows a three-year note with notional $L = \$1{,}000$ and six semiannual dates. It pays $H = \$35$ when $S_{t_i}/S_0 \ge K_1 = 80\%$, and it tests $K_2 = 100\%$ on dates 2 to 5 only. At $T$ it repays $L$ if $S_T \ge B = 0.8S_0$, and $LS_T/S_0$ below that. The design follows a published RBC note on the S&P 500 from June 2026. That note had quarterly dates and a $90\%$ coupon trigger and barrier.

#### *(Coupons as Binaries)* A coupon that no early call can cancel is a cash binary call, so the issuer can hedge it with one.

Take a coupon date $t_1$ that comes before every call date. The note pays $H$ at $t_1$ if $S_{t_1} \ge K_1S_0$, and nothing otherwise. A cash call with payout $H$, strike $K_1S_0$ and expiry $t_1$ pays the same amount in every state. So the coupon is worth $He^{-rt_1}N(d_2)$, with $d_2$ taken at strike $K_1S_0$ ([[2.3.6.1b Binary Options]]). The issuer owes the coupon and can buy this cash call to fund it. A European call would not match the coupon. It pays a variable amount above its strike, while the coupon is a fixed amount. With time $\tau$ left to $t_1$, the coupon's delta is
$$H\Delta_b = \frac{He^{-r\tau}n(d_2)}{S\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}.$$
Here $n$ is the standard normal density, and $\Delta_b$ is the delta of a unit cash call. The delta is largest when the index is near the trigger shortly before $t_1$. A coupon after the first call date is paid only if the note survives every earlier call test. Its value then depends on the path. A dealer needs a model of the whole path to price it.

#### *(Selling a Down-and-In Put)* An autocallable without protection repays principal net of a short down-and-in put, the same option a reverse convertible sells.

The principal rule above is the notional minus a put payoff that exists only below the barrier,
$$R(S_T) = L - a(S_0 - S_T)\,\mathbf{1}\{S_T < B\}, \qquad a = \frac{L}{S_0}.$$
The second term is $a$ down-and-in puts with strike $S_0$ and barrier $B$. The investor is short these puts, and their premium helps pay for the coupons. The put strike is $S_0$, which lies above the barrier. So the loss is already $L - aB = (1 - B/S_0)L$ when the index ends slightly below the barrier, \$200 on the note above. This note checks the barrier only at $T$. A general down-and-in put knocks in if the price touches the barrier on any monitoring date ([[2.3.6.2a Barrier Options]]). Autocallables differ in how they treat the principal.
- An **autocallable participating note** protects the principal. If the note is not called early, it can pay a large share of the index's rise.
- An **autocallable without principal protection** embeds a short down-and-in put, as here, to raise the coupon.

A [[2.3.6.3e Reverse Convertible Notes|reverse convertible note]] makes the same trade without the call feature. The investor buys a bond with a high coupon and sells a down-and-in put to the issuer. The premium of that put pays for the extra coupon. If the put knocks in and the stock ends below the strike, the investor gets back less than the principal.

# Derivation

We replicate the payment at $T$ of the autocallable above when it is never called. Its coupon trigger equals the barrier, $K_1S_0 = B$. Write $a = L/S_0$. The final payment, principal plus the last coupon, is
$$F_T = \begin{cases} L + H, & S_T \ge B, \\ aS_T, & S_T < B. \end{cases}$$
The payment jumps by $L + H - aB$ at $B$. A hedge in the index alone would need very large trades near $B$ close to $T$. Binaries reproduce the jump directly.

The first portfolio holds a cash call that pays $L + H$ when $S_T \ge B$, and $a$ asset puts at $B$. Each asset put pays $S_T$ when $S_T < B$. Below $B$ the puts pay $aS_T$, and at or above $B$ the cash call pays $L + H$.

The second portfolio uses ordinary puts. Let $A = aB$ and $J = L + H - aB$. The portfolio holds cash that pays $A$ at $T$. It sells $a$ European puts with strike $B$ and buys $J$ unit cash calls at $B$. The table lists the payments at $T$.

| Position | $S_T < B$ | $S_T \ge B$ |
|---|---|---|
| Cash paying $A = aB$ | $aB$ | $aB$ |
| Short $a$ puts at $B$ | $-a(B - S_T)$ | $0$ |
| Long $J$ unit cash calls at $B$ | $0$ | $L + H - aB$ |
| Total | $aS_T$ | $L + H$ |

Both portfolios pay $F_T$ in every state. By the [[4.2.2a Arbitrage|law of one price]], each costs the same as the payment. The [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]] with strike $B$ prices the put at $p(B)$ and the unit cash call at $b(B) = e^{-rT}N(d_2)$. The value and the delta of the payment are
$$V_0 = Ae^{-rT} - a\,p(B) + J\,b(B), \qquad \Delta_V = -a\,\Delta_p(B) + J\,\Delta_b(B).$$
Here $\Delta_p$ is the put's delta and $\Delta_b = e^{-r\tau}n(d_2)/(S\sigma\sqrt{\tau})$ is the delta of a unit cash call with time $\tau$ left. Close to $T$ and close to $B$, $n(d_2)$ is near its peak and $\sqrt{\tau}$ is small. The binary term then dominates the delta, because the payment jumps by $J$ over a narrow range of prices.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Payments on Three Index Paths
>A one-year autocallable has a notional of \$10,000 and four quarterly observation dates. On each date it pays a coupon of \$180 if the index is at least $75\%$ of its starting level. On dates 1 to 3 the issuer redeems the note if the index is above $100\%$. At maturity the note repays \$10,000 if the index is at least $75\%$, and $\$10{,}000 \times S_T/S_0$ below that. Ignore discounting and issuer default.
>
>>[!example]- Find the payments and their total on three paths, with the index written as a fraction of its start. Path 1 is $0.93$, $0.88$, $0.72$, $0.81$. Path 2 is $0.98$, $1.03$. Path 3 is $0.90$, $0.79$, $0.70$, $0.66$.
>>Path 1 never rises above $100\%$, so it reaches maturity. It pays \$180 on dates 1 and 2. On date 3 the index is below $75\%$, so that coupon is lost. At maturity the index is at $81\%$, above the barrier. The note pays \$180 plus \$10,000. The total is \$10,540.
>>
>>Path 2 pays \$180 on date 1. On date 2 the index is at $103\%$, above the call trigger. The note pays \$10,000 plus that date's coupon of \$180 and ends. The total is \$10,360 after only six months.
>>
>>Path 3 pays \$180 on dates 1 and 2, and nothing on date 3. At maturity the index is at $66\%$, below both the coupon trigger and the barrier. There is no coupon, and the principal is $10{,}000 \times 0.66 = \$6{,}600$. The total is \$6,960. The investor bears the full fall of $34\%$ from the start. A final level slightly below $75\%$ would already cost about $25\%$ of the principal.

>[!example] (Medium) Valuing and Hedging One Coupon
>An autocallable on an index pays a first coupon of \$45 per \$1,000 note in six months. It pays the coupon only if the index is then at least $85\%$ of its starting level. The note cannot be called before this coupon. Normalize the index to $S_0 = 100$. The risk-free rate is $3.5\%$ and the dividend yield is $1.5\%$, both continuously compounded. The volatility is $22\%$. Ignore issuer default and any delay between observation and payment.
>
>>[!example]- Find the coupon's value and delta today. Then reprice it with the index at $85$, with six months still left to the coupon date.
>>The coupon is a cash call with payout $H = 45$, strike $85$ and $\tau = 0.5$. Its value is $He^{-r\tau}N(d_2)$ with
>>$$d_2 = \frac{\ln(S/85) + (r - q - \sigma^2/2)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} = \frac{0.16252 + (0.035 - 0.015 - 0.0242)(0.5)}{0.22\sqrt{0.5}} = \frac{0.16252 - 0.0021}{0.15556} = 1.03121.$$
>>With $N(d_2) = 0.84878$ and $He^{-r\tau} = 45e^{-0.0175} = 44.2194$, the coupon is worth $44.2194 \times 0.84878 = \$37.53$.
>>
>>The delta uses $n(d_2) = 0.23442$:
>>$$H\Delta_b = \frac{44.2194 \times 0.23442}{100 \times 0.15556} = \frac{10.3659}{15.5563} = 0.6663.$$
>>The coupon gains about \$0.67 per index point.
>>
>>At $S = 85$, $\ln(S/85) = 0$, so $d_2 = -0.0021/0.15556 = -0.01350$. Then $N(d_2) = 0.49461$, and the coupon is worth $44.2194 \times 0.49461 = \$21.87$. With $n(d_2) = 0.39891$, the delta is $17.6394/13.2229 = 1.3340$. The delta doubles at the trigger. So the issuer's hedge needs twice the index exposure per note at $85$ as at $100$.

>>
>>For strike $200$ and $\tau = 3$, the pieces are $\ln(250/200) = 0.22314$, $(r - q + \sigma^2/2)\tau = 0.15$ and $\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = 0.34641$. >>$$\Delta_V = -4(-0.1345) + 235(0.003082) = 0.538 + 0.724 = 1.26.$$

>>One week before maturity, $\tau = 1/52$. At $S = 200$, $d_2 = 0.0069$ and $n(d_2) = 0.39893$. The binary part of the delta is
>>$$J\Delta_b = \frac{235 \times e^{-0.045/52} \times 0.39893}{200 \times 0.20 \times \sqrt{1/52}} = \frac{93.67}{5.547} = 16.89.$$
