>[!note] Income Changes
>An **income change** moves income from $M$ to $M'$ while the prices $p_1, p_2$ stay fixed. The [[6.2.1 Budget Constraint|budget line]] $x_2 = M'/p_2 - (p_1/p_2)x_1$ shifts parallel to itself. Its slope $-p_1/p_2$ does not change, and both intercepts $M'/p_1$ and $M'/p_2$ move out when $M' > M$. The **income expansion path** is the curve traced by the optimal bundle $(x_1^*(\mathbf{p}, M), x_2^*(\mathbf{p}, M))$ as $M$ varies. The **Engel curve** of good $i$ is the [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|Marshallian demand]] $x_i^*(M)$ as a function of income alone, with $p_1$ and $p_2$ held fixed. It is usually drawn with $M$ on the vertical axis. Good $i$ is **normal** at $(\mathbf{p}, M)$ if
>$$\frac{\partial x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)}{\partial M} > 0,$$
>and **inferior** if this derivative is negative. The classification is local: the same good can be normal at low income and inferior at high income.
>
>The budget binds at every income, so spending always adds up to income. Differentiating $p_1x_1^* + p_2x_2^* = M$ with respect to $M$ gives
>$$p_1\frac{\partial x_1^*}{\partial M} + p_2\frac{\partial x_2^*}{\partial M} = 1.$$
>The two terms are the parts of an extra dollar spent on good 1 and on good 2. With two goods, both goods cannot be inferior, because the left side would then be negative. For [[6.4a Cobb-Douglas]] utility $x_1^\alpha x_2^{1-\alpha}$, demand $x_1^* = \alpha M/p_1$ and $x_2^* = (1-\alpha)M/p_2$ is proportional to income. Both goods are normal, both Engel curves are straight lines through the origin, and the expansion path is a ray with slope $\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{p_1}{p_2}$. Whether a good is normal or inferior sets the sign of its income effect in the [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]].

# Intuition

Income is the one parameter that leaves relative prices alone. A raise lets the consumer afford bundles that were out of reach, but the market still trades the two goods at the rate $p_1/p_2$. The question is how the consumer spreads the extra money. For most goods the consumer buys more. For some goods the consumer buys less, because a richer consumer switches to a better alternative. A student who rides the bus may switch to rideshares after a first job.

The first diagram shows a consumer with $u = x_1^{1/2}x_2^{1/2}$, prices $p_1 = 1$ and $p_2 = 2$, and incomes $M = 4, 8, 12$. Each budget line touches an indifference curve at the optimum $A$, $B$, or $C$. Joining these points traces the income expansion path. For this utility the path is a ray from the origin, because the consumer always spends half of income on each good.

# Properties

#### *(Normality Test)* The implicit function theorem signs $\partial x_i^*/\partial M$ from the second derivatives of $u$.

Apply the recipe of [[6.3.1 Comparative Statics]] to the conditions $u'_{x_1} = \lambda p_1$, $u'_{x_2} = \lambda p_2$, and $p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 = M$, with unknowns $(\lambda, x_1, x_2)$. Income enters only the budget equation. The result is
$$\frac{\partial x_1^*}{\partial M} = \frac{p_2u''_{x_1x_2} - p_1u''_{x_2x_2}}{|H|}, \qquad \frac{\partial x_2^*}{\partial M} = \frac{p_1u''_{x_1x_2} - p_2u''_{x_1x_1}}{|H|}.$$
Here $|H| = -p_1^2u''_{x_2x_2} + 2p_1p_2u''_{x_1x_2} - p_2^2u''_{x_1x_1}$ is the bordered Hessian determinant from [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]], positive at a maximum. The sufficient second-order conditions $u''_{x_ix_i} < 0$ and $u''_{x_1x_2} > 0$ also make both numerators positive, so both goods are normal. When $u''_{x_2x_2} < 0$, good 1 can be inferior only if $u''_{x_1x_2} < 0$. In words, more of good 2 must lower the marginal utility of good 1.

#### *(Spending Split)* When one good is inferior, spending on the other good rises by more than the income gain.

The budget identity gives $p_2\,\partial x_2^*/\partial M = 1 - p_1\,\partial x_1^*/\partial M$. If good 1 is inferior, the right side is larger than $1$. A one-dollar raise then leads the consumer to cut spending on good 1 and put more than one dollar into good 2. With $n$ goods the identity becomes $\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\,\partial x_i^*/\partial M = 1$. At least one good must be normal, but several goods can be inferior at once.

#### *(Standard Families)* For the four standard utility families, demand is proportional to income.

The [[6.4d CES Utility|CES]] demand derived in [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]] is $M$ times a function of prices alone. The same holds for its limiting cases. [[6.4a Cobb-Douglas]] gives $x_1^* = \alpha M/p_1$. [[6.4c Perfect Complements|Perfect complements]] $\min(x_1, x_2)$ give $x_1^* = x_2^* = M/(p_1 + p_2)$. [[6.4b Perfect Substitutes|Perfect substitutes]] give $x_1^* = M/p_1$ or $x_1^* = 0$. So every Engel curve in these families is a straight line through the origin, and every expansion path is a ray. None of these families can produce an inferior good. A curved or kinked Engel curve, and in particular an inferior good, needs a different utility function, such as the one in the Hard example.

#### *(Normal and Inferior Are Local)* A good can change type as income grows.

The derivative $\partial x_i^*/\partial M$ is evaluated at one income level. Bus rides can be normal for a student whose income rises from a very low level, and inferior for a professional who can afford rideshares. The Engel curve then bends back, as in the right panel above. A derivative of zero is a third case, in which the good is neither normal nor inferior. A statement such as "potatoes are inferior" holds only over some range of incomes and prices.

# Derivation

We want to show that the income derivatives, weighted by prices, sum to one. With strictly monotone preferences the budget binds at the optimum ([[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]]). This is true at every income, so
$$p_1x_1^*(\mathbf{p}, M) + p_2x_2^*(\mathbf{p}, M) = M \quad \text{for all } M.$$
The two sides are the same function of $M$, so their derivatives are equal. Differentiating term by term with prices held constant gives
$$p_1\frac{\partial x_1^*}{\partial M} + p_2\frac{\partial x_2^*}{\partial M} = 1.$$
If both derivatives were zero or negative, the left side would be at most zero. So at least one of the two goods is normal.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Bus Rides and Rideshares
>A student's monthly transport budget rises from $200$ to $260$ dollars. A bus ride costs $2$ and a rideshare costs $15$. At $200$ the student takes $40$ bus rides and $8$ rideshares. At $260$ the student takes $25$ bus rides and $14$ rideshares.
>
>>[!example]- Over this range, which good is normal and which is inferior? Check the budget identity, and explain why the student could not cut both goods.
>>Bus rides fall from $40$ to $25$ as income rises, so they are inferior over this range. Rideshares rise from $8$ to $14$, so they are normal.
>>
>>Spending on bus rides changes by $2 \cdot (-15) = -30$. Spending on rideshares changes by $15 \cdot 6 = +90$. The total change is $+60$, equal to the raise. Rideshare spending rose by $90$, more than the whole raise of $60$, because the student also moved $30$ out of bus rides.
>>
>>The student spends the whole budget before and after, so total spending must rise by exactly $60$. Cutting both goods would lower total spending. So at least one good must be normal.

>[!example] (Medium) Coffee and Books with Cobb-Douglas Utility
>A student has utility $u = x_1^{0.25}x_2^{0.75}$ over cups of coffee $x_1$ at $p_1 = 4$ and books $x_2$ at $p_2 = 20$. Income rises from $M = 400$ to $M = 480$.
>
>>[!example]- Find both Engel curves and the expansion path. How much of the extra $80$ goes to coffee?
>>Cobb-Douglas demand gives $x_1^* = 0.25M/4 = M/16$ and $x_2^* = 0.75M/20 = 3M/80$. Both Engel curves are lines through the origin. With income on the vertical axis, the coffee Engel curve is $M = 16x_1$. The ratio $x_2^*/x_1^* = (3M/80)/(M/16) = 0.6$ does not depend on $M$, so the expansion path is the ray $x_2 = 0.6x_1$. This matches the slope formula $\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{p_1}{p_2} = 3 \cdot \frac{4}{20} = 0.6$.
>>
>>At $M = 400$ the student buys $25$ coffees and $15$ books. At $M = 480$ the student buys $30$ coffees and $18$ books. Coffee spending rises by $4 \cdot 5 = 20$ and book spending by $20 \cdot 3 = 60$. The share of the raise spent on coffee, $20/80 = 0.25$, equals the share of income spent on coffee. With Cobb-Douglas utility, every dollar of income is split in the same proportions.

>[!example] (Hard) An Engel Curve with a Kink
>A consumer has utility $u = 10\ln(1 + x_1) + x_2$, where $x_1$ is cups of coffee at $p_1 = 2$ and $x_2$ is spending on all other goods at $p_2 = 1$. Both quantities must be nonnegative.
>
>>[!example]- Find the Engel curve for coffee at every income $M > 0$. Where is coffee normal? Check the budget identity on each part of the curve.
>>At an interior optimum the tangency condition is $u'_{x_1}/u'_{x_2} = 10/(1 + x_1) = p_1/p_2 = 2$, so $x_1^* = 4$. The budget then gives $x_2^* = M - 8$. This is valid only when $M \ge 8$.
>>
>>When $M < 8$ the interior formula would require $x_2 < 0$. The optimum is the [[6.2.3 Corner Solutions|corner]] $x_2^* = 0$, $x_1^* = M/2$. At this corner $u'_{x_1}/u'_{x_2} = 10/(1 + M/2) > 2$. The consumer would trade other goods for more coffee at the market rate, but has no other goods left to give up.
>>
>>The Engel curve is $x_1^* = M/2$ for $M \le 8$ and $x_1^* = 4$ for $M \ge 8$. Coffee is normal below $M = 8$. Above $M = 8$ its income derivative is $0$, so coffee is neither normal nor inferior there. With income on the vertical axis, the Engel curve rises along the line $M = 2x_1$ and then turns straight up at $x_1 = 4$.
>>
>>The budget identity holds on both parts. Below $M = 8$ it reads $2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} + 1 \cdot 0 = 1$. Above $M = 8$ it reads $2 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 = 1$. The normality test gives the same answer. Here $u''_{x_1x_2} = u''_{x_2x_2} = 0$, so its numerator for good 1 is zero on the interior part.
