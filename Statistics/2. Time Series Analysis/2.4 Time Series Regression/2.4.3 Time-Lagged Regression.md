>[!note] Time-Lagged Regression
>**Time-lagged regression** (also called *distributed-lag* regression, or finite impulse-response regression) explains an output time series $y_t$ as a weighted sum of present, past, and possibly future values of an input series $x_t$:
>$$y_t \;=\; \sum_{r = -m_-}^{m_+} \beta_r\, x_{t - r} \;+\; v_t,$$
>where $\{\beta_r\}$ are lag coefficients, $m_-, m_+ \geq 0$ are the maximum future and past lags considered, and $v_t$ is a noise term. With $m_- = 0$ (no future lags) the model is **causal**; with both $m_\pm > 0$ it is **non-causal** and useful only for offline analysis. The infinite version $y_t = \sum_r \beta_r x_{t-r} + v_t$ is the population analogue, but in practice only finitely many lags are estimated.
>
>**Estimation.** With $L = m_- + m_+ + 1$ lag coefficients, build the design matrix $\mathbf{X}$ whose row at time $t$ is $(x_{t + m_-}, x_{t + m_- - 1}, \dots, x_{t - m_+})$, and run [[1.2a. Multiple Linear Regression|OLS]]:
>$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}.$$
>
>**When the lag count is large** (often the case in neuroscience, economics, audio), $\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}$ becomes ill-conditioned and OLS overfits. Standard remedy: [[1.5. Regularization|ridge regression]] or lasso, with the regularization strength chosen by cross-validation.
>
>**Connection to filtering.** The fitted $\hat\beta_r$ are the weights of a finite linear filter applied to $x_t$ (see [[2.3.4 Linear Filters and Frequency Response]]); the frequency response $\hat A(\omega) = \sum_r \hat\beta_r e^{-2\pi i r \omega}$ describes which frequencies of $x$ drive $y$.
>
>>[!warning] Correlated Predictor Lags
>>Lagged versions of the same series are heavily correlated. The columns of $\mathbf{X}$ in a distributed-lag model are far from orthogonal; adjacent columns can have correlation above $0.9$. OLS standard errors will be large and individual $\hat\beta_r$ may flip sign run-to-run, even when the sum $\sum_r \hat\beta_r$ is stable. Inference on individual lag coefficients is unreliable; inference on aggregate impulse-response shape is what to trust.

# Intuition

Time-lagged regression is the simplest way to model "$x$ drives $y$ with some delay" without committing to a specific dynamical mechanism. Instead of writing a parametric model like an AR or ARMA, the analyst lets the data choose the impulse response: how strongly an impulse in $x$ at time $t = 0$ shows up in $y$ at times $t = 0, 1, 2, \dots$. The weights $\beta_r$ are exactly that impulse response, and their full sequence describes both the *shape* and the *delay* of the input-to-output coupling.

The classic example in time-series textbooks is the SOI–Recruitment fish-population data: a climate index ($x$) drives a fish-population variable ($y$) with a delay of several months, and the distributed-lag fit shows precisely how the response builds and dissipates. The neuroscience example covered in lecture is *spectro-temporal receptive field* (STRF) estimation: $x$ is a sound spectrogram and $y$ is the firing rate of an auditory neuron, with the fitted $\beta$ surface describing which sound features at which past times drive the neuron's response.

# Properties

#### *(Equivalence to Filtering)* The fitted model can be rewritten $\hat y_t = (\hat\beta * x)_t$, a discrete convolution. The frequency response is the DTFT of $\hat\beta$.

This is why time-lagged regression is sometimes called *finite-impulse-response (FIR) filter identification*: from the engineer's point of view, we are estimating the filter that best maps $x$ to $y$. The frequency response $\hat A(\omega)$ is informative diagnostically: a low-pass filter (high gain at low $\omega$, low gain at high $\omega$) means the coupling is dominated by slow components; a band-pass filter localizes the coupling to a specific frequency range.

#### *(OLS Optimality on Whitened Inputs)* If $x_t$ is white noise and $v_t$ is white noise independent of $x$, then $\hat\beta_r = \hat\gamma_{xy}(r) / \hat\gamma_{xx}(0)$, so the OLS estimate equals the rescaled sample [[2.2.4 Cross-Covariance Function|cross-covariance]].

For non-white inputs the OLS estimate is the cross-covariance after *pre-whitening* both series with the same filter. This is the basis of the standard distributed-lag identification procedure: fit an ARMA to $x$ to get its innovations, apply the same filter to $y$, then read off lag coefficients from the cross-correlation of the whitened series.

#### *(Lag Count vs. Sample Size)* For OLS to be well-conditioned, the lag count $L$ should satisfy $L \ll n$, typically $L \leq n/10$ as a rule of thumb.

When $L$ is comparable to $n$, the design matrix has fewer rows than effective columns, the estimator overfits dramatically, and out-of-sample prediction error explodes. Ridge regression with a CV-tuned penalty extends usable $L$ to several hundred even with $n$ in the thousands, and is the standard remedy in modern STRF and finance applications.

#### *(Causal vs. Non-Causal Identification)* Including future lags ($m_- > 0$) helps detect whether $y$ leads $x$ (significant negative-lag coefficients) or whether the assumed direction of causality is wrong.

A clean causal coupling shows non-zero $\hat\beta_r$ at $r \geq 0$ and noise at $r < 0$; a wrong-direction model shows the mirror pattern. This is a powerful sanity check that pure ARMA modeling lacks. (Causality here is in the engineering sense, the temporal direction of the linear coupling, not Granger causality, which is a related but distinct test.)

#### *(Residual Diagnostics)* The residuals $\hat v_t = y_t - \hat y_t$ should be white. If they are not, the lag count is too short, or there is missing endogenous structure that needs separate ARMA modeling.

A two-stage workflow: (i) fit the distributed-lag model, (ii) fit an ARMA to the residuals to capture any leftover serial correlation. Combined this gives a *transfer function* (or ARMAX) model: $y_t = \sum_r \beta_r x_{t-r} + \text{ARMA noise}$.

#### *(Standard-Error Inflation under Serial Correlation)* OLS standard errors are wrong when $v_t$ is autocorrelated; HAC standard errors (Newey-West) or GLS with an estimated noise covariance are needed for valid inference.

The point estimates $\hat\beta_r$ remain unbiased, but their advertised significance can be wildly wrong if computed naively. In econometric practice, HAC corrections are standard for distributed-lag models; in neuroscience, the inference is often visual (compare to the noise distribution from circularly-shifted permutation tests).

# Examples

>[!example] Climate-driven fish recruitment
>Let $x_t$ be the monthly Southern Oscillation Index and $y_t$ be monthly fish recruitment. A distributed-lag fit with $L = 24$ monthly lags shows $\hat\beta_r$ peaking at $r \approx 5$ months and decaying smoothly out to $r \approx 12$.
>>[!example]- Interpret.
>>The peak at lag 5 says climate conditions drive fish populations with about a five-month delay, a plausible biological lead time for ENSO-related sea-surface temperature changes to propagate through the food chain to recruitment. The decay tail to $r = 12$ says some climate effects accumulate over the better part of a year. The shape is a smooth, unimodal impulse response, typical of a low-pass coupling. The fitted frequency response $\hat A(\omega)$ would show a peak near $\omega = 0$, confirming that low-frequency (slow) variations in $x$ drive low-frequency (slow) variations in $y$.

>[!example] Spectro-temporal receptive field (STRF)
>An auditory neuron's firing rate $y_t$ is regressed on a sound spectrogram $x_{t, f}$ across $F = 32$ frequency channels and $L = 50$ time lags ($\sim 100$ ms at $f_s = 500$ Hz).
>>[!example]- How is the model parameterized and fit?
>>The full design has $F \times L = 1600$ regressors per output sample. With a typical recording of $n = 10^5$ samples, OLS is feasible but overfits without regularization. Ridge regression with regularization $\lambda$ chosen by 5-fold CV is standard. The fitted coefficient surface $\hat\beta_{r, f}$ is the *spectro-temporal receptive field*: a 2-D plot of frequency vs. lag that shows the neuron's preferred sound feature. Auditory cortical neurons typically show a band-limited preferred frequency (rows of $\hat\beta$ peak at one or two channels) and a $\sim 30$ ms response delay (columns of $\hat\beta$ peak at $r \approx 15$).

>[!example] Diagnosing direction with non-causal lags
>You suspect $x$ leads $y$ but want to be sure. Fit a distributed-lag model with $m_- = 10$, $m_+ = 10$, a 21-lag fit including 10 future and 10 past lags.
>>[!example]- What pattern confirms the causal direction?
>>Significant $\hat\beta_r$ for $r \geq 0$ (past values of $x$ predict $y$) with $\hat\beta_r \approx 0$ for $r < 0$ (future values of $x$ do not) is consistent with $x \to y$ causality. The opposite pattern (significant negative-lag coefficients) would mean $y \to x$: the variables are coupled in the reverse direction, and the model setup was wrong. A symmetric pattern (both significant) suggests bidirectional coupling or a common driver, neither of which the simple distributed-lag model can disentangle.
