>[!note] Profit Maximization and Supply
>A **price-taking** firm sells any quantity at the market price $p$ and cannot change that price. The second step of the two-step approach takes the cost function $c(w, r, y)$ from [[6.7.3 Cost Minimization]] and picks output:
>$$\max_{y \ge 0}\ \pi(y) = py - c(w, r, y).$$
>- First-order condition, **price equals marginal cost**: $p - c'_y(w, r, y^*) = 0$.
>- Second-order condition: $-c''_{yy}(w, r, y^*) < 0$. The MC curve must be increasing at $y^*$.
>- Break-even check: the firm produces only if $\pi(y^*) \ge 0$, that is, $p \ge AC(y^*)$. Otherwise $y^* = 0$.
>- The **supply function** $y^*(p, w, r)$ is the rising part of the MC curve above min AC, and zero below it:
>$$y^*(p) = \begin{cases} (c'_y)^{-1}(p) & \text{if } p > \min AC, \\ 0 & \text{if } p < \min AC. \end{cases}$$
>- At $p = \min AC$ the firm is indifferent between $y = 0$ and the output that minimizes AC. If AC has its minimum at $y \to 0$, as under DRS with no setup cost, the supply curve starts at the origin.
>- Slope, from the [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] applied to the FOC:
>$$\frac{\partial y^*}{\partial p} = -\frac{1}{-c''_{yy}(w, r, y^*)} = \frac{1}{c''_{yy}(w, r, y^*)} > 0 \quad \text{whenever the SOC holds}.$$
>- Profit at the optimum is $\pi = y^*\,(p - AC(y^*))$. The profit per unit is the gap between MC and AC at $y^*$.
>- Returns to scale decide whether a supply function exists. For $y = AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta}$, with $c = B\,(y/A)^{1/(\alpha+\beta)}$ from [[6.7.4 Cost Curves]]:
>  - DRS, $\alpha + \beta < 1$: interior optimum $y^* = A\left(\frac{(\alpha+\beta)\,pA}{B}\right)^{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{1-\alpha-\beta}}$, increasing in $p$. This is the only well-behaved case.
>  - CRS, $\alpha + \beta = 1$: $y^* = 0$ if $p < B/A$, any $y^* \in [0, \infty)$ if $p = B/A$, and $y^* \to \infty$ if $p > B/A$.
>  - IRS, $\alpha + \beta > 1$: the FOC point is a profit minimum, and $y^* \to \infty$ at any $p > 0$.
>- The one-input technology $y = L^{\alpha}$ follows the same three cases, with $\alpha$ in place of $\alpha + \beta$, $A = 1$, and $B = w$. For $\alpha < 1$, $y^* = (\alpha p/w)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$ at every $p > 0$. For $\alpha = 1$, the threshold price is $w$. For $\alpha > 1$, $y^* \to \infty$.

# Intuition

A price-taking firm sells every unit at $p$. One more unit adds $p$ to revenue and $MC$ to cost. While $p > MC$, the extra unit raises profit, so the firm expands. While $p < MC$, the last unit loses money, so the firm cuts back. It stops where $p = MC$. This rule is about the last unit only. It does not say whether the firm should produce at all. That second question compares price with average cost. Total profit is $y\,(p - AC)$, so the firm covers its costs only if $p \ge AC$. The supply curve combines both answers.

In the diagram, revenue $py$ is a straight line with slope $p = 30$. The cost is $c(y) = 100 + y^2$ for $y > 0$, where $100$ is a setup cost, and $c(0) = 0$. Profit is the vertical gap between the revenue line and the cost curve. The gap is largest where the cost curve has the same slope as the revenue line, which is the condition $p = MC$. Here that happens at $y = 15$, with a gap of $450 - 325 = 125$. Closing, at the dot on the origin, gives zero, so the firm produces.

# Properties

#### *(MC Above Min AC)* Supply follows the rising MC curve from the bottom of AC upward and is zero at lower prices.

To the right of min AC, MC lies above AC, so a price $p = MC$ there exceeds AC and profit is positive. At a price below min AC, every positive output has $p < AC$ and makes a loss, so the firm produces nothing. The supply curve therefore jumps from $0$ to the output that minimizes AC. A firm with a U-shaped AC never supplies a small amount. The animation uses the firm from the diagram above, with min AC $= 20$ at $y = 10$. At $p = 15$ the FOC point $y = 7.5$ loses $43.75$, so $y^* = 0$. At $p = 20$ the firm breaks even. At $p = 30$ and $p = 40$ it earns $125$ and $300$, the rectangles $y^*(p - AC)$.

#### *(Rising MC Is Required)* The FOC finds a maximum only where MC slopes up.

Where MC falls, the units right after the FOC point cost less than $p$, so expanding raises profit. The FOC point is then a profit minimum. Under Cobb-Douglas IRS, MC falls at every output, and profit grows without bound. A non-convex technology has a U-shaped MC. For prices a little above min MC, the FOC has two roots. The root on the falling branch is a minimum. The root on the rising branch is a local maximum, but it can still lose money. A full answer therefore needs three checks: the FOC, the SOC, and $p \ge AC$. The Hard example runs all three.

#### *(Supply Slopes Up)* A steep MC curve makes supply respond little to price, and a flat MC curve makes it respond a lot.

The slope $1/c''_{yy}$ is the inverse of the slope of MC. The supply curve is the MC curve read sideways, so its slope in the $(y, p)$ plane equals $c''_{yy}$. When capacity is fixed, MC is almost vertical and supply barely moves with price. Under CRS, $c''_{yy} = 0$ and supply is horizontal at $p = MC = AC$. These two limits are the inflexible and flexible supply of [[6.8.4 Tax Incidence]]. Adding the supply curves of many firms gives market supply in [[6.8.1 Aggregation]].

#### *(Profit Two Ways)* Profit at the optimum equals both a rectangle and the area between price and MC, minus the fixed cost.

The rectangle is $y^*\,(p - AC(y^*))$. For the area, write cost as fixed cost plus the sum of marginal costs, $c(y^*) = F + \int_0^{y^*} c'_y(y)\,dy$. Then $\pi = \int_0^{y^*}(p - c'_y(y))\,dy - F$. When the fixed cost is paid even at zero output, it equals $c(0)$, and the formula reads $\pi = \int_0^{y^*}(p - c'_y(y))\,dy - c(0)$. Each unit up to $y^*$ adds $p - MC$ to profit. [[6.8.5 Consumer and Producer Surplus]] builds producer surplus on this formula.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture's supply rule uses min AC, which assumes the firm avoids all costs by closing. The rule for sunk costs below was not covered.

#### *(Sunk Costs and Shutdown)* If a fixed cost must be paid even at zero output, the firm compares price with min AVC instead of min AC.

Closing gives profit $-F$. Producing gives $py - VC(y) - F$. The firm produces if $py - VC(y) \ge 0$, that is, if $p \ge AVC(y)$. The sunk cost appears on both sides and does not affect the choice. The print shop of [[6.7.4 Cost Curves]] has min AVC $= 6$ and min AC $= 15$. If its lease is sunk, it keeps printing at any price above $6$. Between $6$ and $15$ it loses money, but less than the lease.

# Derivation

We want the supply function for $y = AK^{\alpha}L^{\beta}$, where cost minimization gave $c(y) = B\,(y/A)^{1/(\alpha+\beta)}$. The firm solves $\max_y\ py - B\,(y/A)^{1/(\alpha+\beta)}$. The FOC is
$$p - \frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}\,\frac{B}{A}\left(\frac{y}{A}\right)^{\frac{1-(\alpha+\beta)}{\alpha+\beta}} = 0.$$
The second derivative of profit is
$$-\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}\,\frac{1-(\alpha+\beta)}{\alpha+\beta}\,\frac{B}{A^2}\left(\frac{y}{A}\right)^{\frac{1-2(\alpha+\beta)}{\alpha+\beta}}.$$
All factors except $1 - (\alpha+\beta)$ are positive, so the SOC holds exactly when $\alpha + \beta < 1$. Under DRS, solving the FOC gives $(y/A)^{(1-(\alpha+\beta))/(\alpha+\beta)} = (\alpha+\beta)\,pA/B$, which gives $y^*$ in the callout. AC is $(B/A)(y/A)^{(1-(\alpha+\beta))/(\alpha+\beta)}$, which tends to $0$ as $y \to 0$, so the firm produces at every $p > 0$. With $A = 1$, $\alpha = \beta = 1/4$, and $w = r = 1$, we get $B = 2$, $c = 2y^2$, and $y^* = p/4$. Under CRS, $c = (B/A)\,y$ and $\pi = (p - B/A)\,y$, which gives the three cases in the callout. Under IRS, the SOC expression is positive, so the FOC point is a minimum. Cost grows like $y^{1/(\alpha+\beta)}$, slower than revenue $py$, so profit tends to infinity.

For the slope in general, write the FOC as $G(y, p) = p - c'_y(w, r, y) = 0$. The implicit function theorem gives $\partial y^*/\partial p = -G_p/G_y = -1/(-c''_{yy})$. The SOC makes the denominator negative, so supply slopes up.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Cobb-Douglas Supply and a Wage Rise
>A firm has $y = K^{1/4}L^{1/4}$, so $A = 1$ and $\alpha = \beta = 1/4$. Input prices are $w = r = 1$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the cost function and the supply function, and the output and profit at $p = 8$. Then the wage doubles to $w = 2$. Redo the problem.
>>Here $\alpha + \beta = 1/2$, so the firm has DRS. With $w = r = 1$, $\frac{w}{r}\frac{\alpha}{\beta} = 1$ and $B = w + r = 2$, so $c(y) = 2y^2$, $MC = 4y$, and $AC = 2y$. The formula gives $y^* = \left(\tfrac12 \cdot p/2\right)^{1} = p/4$ at every $p > 0$, since min AC is $0$. At $p = 8$, $y^* = 2$ and $\pi = 16 - 8 = 8$.
>>
>>With $w = 2$, $\frac{w}{r}\frac{\alpha}{\beta} = 2$ and $B = 2 \cdot 2^{-1/2} + 2^{1/2} = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83$. Now $c(y) = 2\sqrt{2}\,y^2$ and $MC = 4\sqrt{2}\,y$, so $y^* = p/(4\sqrt{2}) \approx 0.177\,p$. At $p = 8$, $y^* = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$ and $\pi = 8\sqrt{2} - 2\sqrt{2} \cdot 2 = 4\sqrt{2} \approx 5.66$. A higher input price raises MC at every output, so the supply curve rotates toward the price axis.

>[!example] (Medium) A Food Truck
>A food truck pays a setup cost of $\$100$ per day (generator, fuel, and parking), but only on days it opens. Its variable cost is $y^2$, so $c(y) = 100 + y^2$ for $y > 0$ and $c(0) = 0$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the supply function. Compute profit at $p = 30$ in two ways, and decide what the truck does at $p = 15$.
>>$MC = 2y$ and $AC = 100/y + y$. AC is smallest where $1 - 100/y^2 = 0$, at $y = 10$ with $AC = 20 = MC(10)$. The supply function is $y^* = p/2$ for $p > 20$ and $y^* = 0$ for $p < 20$. At $p = 20$ both $0$ and $10$ are optimal. At $p = 30$, $y^* = 15$ and $AC(15) = 21.67$, so the rectangle is $15 \times 8.33 = 125$. The area is $\int_0^{15}(30 - 2y)\,dy - 100 = 450 - 225 - 100 = 125$. At $p = 15$, the FOC gives $y = 7.5$ with $AC = 20.83$, a loss of $7.5 \times 5.83 = 43.75$. The truck stays closed and earns $0$.

>[!example] (Hard) A Non-Convex Technology
>A firm has $c(y) = y^3 - 6y^2 + 15y$, with no fixed cost.
>
>>[!example]- (a) At $p = 4.5$, solve the FOC, classify each root, and find $y^*$. (b) Find the supply function. (c) At $p = 9$, compute $\partial y^*/\partial p$ with $1/c''$ and check it against (b).
>>(a) $MC = 3y^2 - 12y + 15 = 4.5$ gives $y^2 - 4y + 3.5 = 0$, so $y = 2 \pm \sqrt{0.5}$, that is $1.29$ or $2.71$. Here $c''_{yy} = 6y - 12$. It equals $-4.24$ at $y = 1.29$, a profit minimum, and $+4.24$ at $y = 2.71$, a local maximum. But $AC(2.71) = 6.09 > 4.5$, so profit there is $-4.29$. The firm produces $y^* = 0$.
>>
>>(b) $AC = y^2 - 6y + 15$ is smallest at $y = 3$, where $AC = 6 = MC(3)$. On the rising branch of MC, $y = 2 + \sqrt{(p - 3)/3}$. So $y^* = 2 + \sqrt{(p-3)/3}$ for $p \ge 6$ and $y^* = 0$ for $p < 6$. Supply jumps from $0$ to $3$ at $p = 6$, even with no fixed cost.
>>
>>(c) At $p = 9$, $y^* = 2 + \sqrt{2} = 3.414$ and $c''_{yy} = 6(3.414) - 12 = 8.49$, so $\partial y^*/\partial p = 1/8.49 = 0.118$. Differentiating (b) gives $\frac{1}{6\sqrt{(p-3)/3}} = \frac{1}{6\sqrt{2}} = 0.118$. Profit is $3.414 \times (9 - 6.17) = 9.66$.
