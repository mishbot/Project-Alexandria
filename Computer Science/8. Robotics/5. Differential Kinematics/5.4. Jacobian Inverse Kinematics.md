>[!note] Jacobian Inverse Kinematics
>**Jacobian-based inverse kinematics** uses the [[5.2. Robot Jacobians|Jacobian]] to iteratively solve the [[2.4. Inverse Kinematics|inverse kinematics]] problem numerically. Rather than finding a closed-form solution, the algorithm linearizes the forward kinematic map at each step and takes a small step in joint space toward the desired pose.
>
>Given a desired pose $g_D \in SE(3)$ and a current configuration $\Theta_t$, the spatial velocity needed to correct the pose error is extracted using the **matrix logarithm**:
>$$\hat{V}_D = \log\bigl(g_D\,g_{st}(\Theta_t)^{-1}\bigr) \in \mathfrak{se}(3),$$
>which gives the twist that would carry the current end-effector pose to the desired pose in one exponential step. The joint update is then
>$$\Theta_{t+1} = \Theta_t + \alpha\, J^{s}(\Theta_t)^{\dagger}\, V_D,$$
>where $J^{s\dagger}$ is the pseudoinverse of the spatial Jacobian and $\alpha \in (0, 1]$ is a step size. The algorithm iterates until $\|V_D\| < \epsilon$.
>
>Near [[5.3. Singularities and Manipulability|singularities]], the pseudoinverse amplifies noise and produces enormous joint velocities. **Damped least squares** (Levenberg–Marquardt) regularizes this by solving
>$$\Theta_{t+1} = \Theta_t + \alpha\, J^T(JJ^T + \lambda^2 I)^{-1}\, V_D,$$
>which is the solution to the regularized problem $\min_{\dot{\Theta}} \|J\dot{\Theta} - V_D\|^2 + \lambda^2\|\dot{\Theta}\|^2$. The damping parameter $\lambda > 0$ trades off tracking accuracy for bounded joint velocities.
>
>For **redundant manipulators** ($n > 6$), the pseudoinverse provides a minimum-norm joint velocity, and the remaining null-space freedom can be used for secondary objectives:
>$$\dot{\Theta} = J^{\dagger}V_D + (I - J^{\dagger}J)\,w,$$
>where $w \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is an arbitrary vector and $(I - J^{\dagger}J)$ projects it into $\mathcal{N}(J)$.
>
>>[!warning] Local Method
>>Jacobian IK is a local, iterative method. It can converge to a local minimum (especially when the target is unreachable), may oscillate near singularities, and does not enumerate all solutions. For manipulators with closed-form IK (e.g., via [[2.5. Paden-Kahan Subproblems|Paden–Kahan subproblems]]), analytic methods are preferred when available.

# Intuition
The Jacobian IK algorithm is essentially Newton's method applied to the equation $g_{st}(\Theta) = g_D$ on the Lie group $SE(3)$. At each iteration, the "error" between the current and desired pose is measured not by subtracting matrices (which is meaningless for elements of $SE(3)$) but by computing the relative transform $g_D g_{st}^{-1}$ and taking its logarithm. The logarithm maps the error from $SE(3)$ back to the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{se}(3)$, where it becomes a twist vector that can be linearly related to joint velocities via the Jacobian. This is the natural generalization of Newton's method to manifold-valued functions.

Damped least squares can be understood geometrically: instead of inverting the Jacobian exactly (which blows up when singular values are small), it "shrinks" the inverse of each singular value from $1/\sigma_i$ to $\sigma_i/(\sigma_i^2 + \lambda^2)$. Large singular values are barely affected, but small ones are smoothly attenuated, preventing the explosion. The cost is reduced accuracy in the singular directions—the end-effector may not reach the exact target along those directions, but the joint velocities remain bounded.

The null-space projection for redundant arms allows the manipulator to "use its extra joints" for a secondary task (like maximizing [[5.3. Singularities and Manipulability#Manipulability Measures|manipulability]], avoiding joint limits, or dodging obstacles) without affecting the primary end-effector tracking. The projection $(I - J^\dagger J)$ kills any component of $w$ that would change the end-effector velocity, keeping only the self-motion component.

# Properties

## The Matrix Logarithm on $SE(3)$

#### (*Definition*) For $g \in SE(3)$ with $g = \begin{bmatrix} R & p \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, the logarithm $\hat{\xi}\theta = \log(g) \in \mathfrak{se}(3)$ recovers the twist $\xi$ and angle $\theta$ such that $e^{\hat{\xi}\theta} = g$. The rotational part uses the $SO(3)$ [[1.5. Exponential Coordinates|log map]] to find $(\omega, \theta)$ from $R$. The translational part solves $p = A(\theta)\,v$ where
$$A(\theta) = \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}I + \left(1 - \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\right)\omega\omega^T + \frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta}\hat{\omega},$$
giving $v = A(\theta)^{-1}p$.

#### (*Error Twist*) The twist $V_D = (\log(g_D g_{st}^{-1}))^\vee \in \mathbb{R}^6$ is the error expressed in the spatial frame. When $g_{st} = g_D$, $V_D = 0$. The body-frame variant uses $(\log(g_{st}^{-1}g_D))^\vee$ and the body Jacobian.

## Pseudoinverse Methods

#### (*Moore–Penrose Pseudoinverse*) For $J \in \mathbb{R}^{6\times n}$ with $n \geq 6$ (redundant or square), the right pseudoinverse $J^\dagger = J^T(JJ^T)^{-1}$ gives the minimum-norm solution $\dot{\Theta} = J^\dagger V$ to the underdetermined system $J\dot{\Theta} = V$. For $n < 6$, the left pseudoinverse $J^\dagger = (J^TJ)^{-1}J^T$ gives the least-squares solution.

#### (*Via SVD*) If $J = U\Sigma V^T$, then $J^\dagger = V\Sigma^\dagger U^T$ where $\Sigma^\dagger$ replaces each nonzero $\sigma_i$ with $1/\sigma_i$. This makes the singular-value amplification explicit: near a singularity, $\sigma_i \to 0$ and $1/\sigma_i \to \infty$.

## Damped Least Squares

#### (*Formulation*) Solve $\min_{\dot{\Theta}} \|J\dot{\Theta} - V_D\|^2 + \lambda^2\|\dot{\Theta}\|^2$. The solution is
$$\dot{\Theta} = J^T(JJ^T + \lambda^2 I)^{-1}V_D.$$
Equivalently, via SVD: each singular value contribution $\sigma_i/({\sigma_i^2 + \lambda^2})$ replaces the pseudoinverse's $1/\sigma_i$.

#### (*Damping Parameter*) $\lambda = 0$ recovers the pseudoinverse. Larger $\lambda$ gives smoother, bounded joint velocities at the cost of tracking accuracy. In practice, $\lambda$ can be adapted: small when far from singularities ($\sigma_{\min}$ large), increased as $\sigma_{\min} \to 0$.

#### (*Convergence*) Damped least squares converges linearly. The algorithm does not guarantee reaching the exact target at a singularity—the damping term prevents the last bit of correction in the singular direction. This is acceptable because the alternative (infinite joint velocity) is physically impossible.

## Redundant Manipulators and Null-Space Optimization

#### (*Null-Space Projection*) For $n > 6$ with $\operatorname{rank}(J) = 6$, the general solution to $J\dot{\Theta} = V$ is
$$\dot{\Theta} = J^\dagger V + (I - J^\dagger J)\,w,$$
where $w \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is arbitrary. The matrix $N = I - J^\dagger J$ is the orthogonal projector onto $\mathcal{N}(J)$: it satisfies $N^2 = N$, $N^T = N$, and $JN = 0$.

#### (*Secondary Objectives*) Common choices for $w$ include:
- **Manipulability maximization:** $w = k_0\,\nabla_\Theta \mu_3(\Theta)$, pushing the configuration toward higher manipulability.
- **Joint-limit avoidance:** $w = -k_0\,\nabla_\Theta H(\Theta)$ where $H$ penalizes proximity to joint limits.
- **Obstacle avoidance:** $w$ chosen to increase distance from obstacles.
Since $Nw \in \mathcal{N}(J)$, the secondary objective does not affect the end-effector velocity.

#### (*7-DoF Example*) A 7-DoF arm (e.g., KUKA iiwa) has a 1D null space at non-singular configurations. The self-motion is an "elbow swivel"—the elbow traces a circle while the wrist position and orientation remain fixed. This extra DoF allows the arm to reach around obstacles or avoid joint limits.

## Step Size Selection

#### (*Constant Step Size*) $\alpha \in (0, 1]$. Smaller $\alpha$ increases stability but slows convergence. $\alpha = 1$ corresponds to a full Newton step.

#### (*Adaptive Step Size*) When $\|V_D\|$ is large, a small $\alpha$ prevents overshooting; when $\|V_D\|$ is small, $\alpha$ can be increased for faster convergence. A common heuristic is $\alpha = \min(1, \alpha_{\max}/\|V_D\|)$.

# Examples
>[!example] Jacobian IK Algorithm (Pseudocode)
>>[!example]- Full algorithm
>>```
>>Input: g_D (desired pose), Theta_0 (initial config),
>>       alpha (step size), epsilon (tolerance), max_iter
>>
>>Theta = Theta_0
>>for t = 1 to max_iter:
>>    g_current = FK(Theta)
>>    error_g = g_D @ inv(g_current)
>>    V_D = vee(logm(error_g))        # 6-vector twist error
>>
>>    if ||V_D|| < epsilon:
>>        return Theta                  # converged
>>
>>    J = spatial_jacobian(Theta)
>>
>>    # Option A: Pseudoinverse (away from singularities)
>>    dTheta = alpha * pinv(J) @ V_D
>>
>>    # Option B: Damped least squares (near singularities)
>>    # dTheta = alpha * J.T @ inv(J @ J.T + lam^2 * I) @ V_D
>>
>>    # Option C: Redundant with null-space (n > 6)
>>    # dTheta = alpha * pinv(J) @ V_D + (I - pinv(J) @ J) @ w
>>
>>    Theta = Theta + dTheta
>>
>>return Theta  # may not have converged
>>```

>A 2R arm at $\theta_2 = 0.01$ (near full extension) with desired radial velocity $V_D = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$:
>>[!example]- Comparing pseudoinverse vs. damped
>>Singular values of $J$: $\sigma_1 \approx 2$, $\sigma_2 \approx 0.01$.
>>
>>**Pseudoinverse:** $\dot{\theta} \approx 1/\sigma_2 = 100$ in the singular direction—physically unrealizable.
>>
>>**Damped ($\lambda = 0.1$):** contribution $\sigma_2/(\sigma_2^2 + \lambda^2) \approx 0.01/0.0101 \approx 0.99$—bounded and smooth. The end-effector won't perfectly track the radial command, but joint velocities stay reasonable.
