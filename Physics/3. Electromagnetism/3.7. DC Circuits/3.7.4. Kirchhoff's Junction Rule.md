>[!note] Kirchhoff's Junction Rule
>A **junction** is a point in a circuit where three or more wires meet. A junction of wires cannot store charge, and it cannot create or destroy charge. Every charge that enters a junction must leave it at once. **Kirchhoff's junction rule** states that the currents at any junction add to zero:
>$$\sum_i I_i = 0.$$
>Each current $I_i$ counts with a plus sign if it flows into the junction and a minus sign if it flows out. A junction with $I_1$ flowing in and $I_2$ and $I_3$ flowing out gives $I_1 - I_2 - I_3 = 0$, or $I_1 = I_2 + I_3$. The rule is conservation of charge written for currents. The EE note [[2.2b KCL]] calls it Kirchhoff's current law and applies it at every node.

# Intuition

Gustav Kirchhoff stated his two circuit rules in 1845. Engineers often call them laws, and physicists call them rules. The junction rule is the first of the two. Charge moves through a wire without piling up anywhere along it. At a junction it can only divide among the outgoing wires or merge from the incoming ones. The charge that arrives each second equals the charge that leaves each second.

Charges flow along the four wires of the Easy example. Wire 4 starts empty, so charge would pile up at the junction. The junction rule gives $I_4 = -2.3\ \text{A}$, and then the flows balance.

# Derivation

Draw a small closed surface around the junction, and let $q_{\text{in}}$ be the net charge inside it. Each current into the junction carries charge in at the rate $I$, and each current out carries charge away at its rate. By [[3.1.2. Conservation of Charge]], the charge inside changes only by flowing across the surface:
$$\frac{dq_{\text{in}}}{dt} = \sum_{\text{in}} I - \sum_{\text{out}} I.$$
The left side is the rate at which the enclosed charge grows. The first sum covers the currents that enter, and the second covers those that leave. A junction of wires has no place to hold extra charge, so $q_{\text{in}}$ stays constant and its rate is zero. Moving every term to one side, with plus signs for currents in and minus signs for currents out, gives $\sum_i I_i = 0$.

# Properties

#### *(Arbitrary Directions)* The arrow drawn for each current is a free choice, and a negative answer means the current flows against its arrow.

The signs in the rule follow the arrows. The equations stay correct for any choice of arrows. Solving them gives each current with a sign. A positive value means that the current flows along its arrow. A negative value means it flows the other way, with the same size. The true directions often depend on every emf and resistance in the circuit. There is no need to guess them in advance.

#### *(One Current per Segment)* Every element along a stretch of wire between two junctions carries the same current.

A segment has no junction in its middle, so charge cannot enter or leave it on the way. The junction rule at each joint along the segment, with one wire in and one wire out, gives equal currents. Each segment therefore needs only one current label. Resistors in series share a current for the same reason.

#### *(One Junction Is Redundant)* In a circuit with $J$ junctions, only $J - 1$ junction equations are independent.

Each segment current leaves one junction and enters another. In the sum of all $J$ junction equations, it appears once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign. The sum is therefore $0 = 0$. Any one equation equals minus the sum of the others, so it adds no information. A classroom example had four junctions and used three of them. Any three would have done.

#### *(Capacitor Plates)* A capacitor plate collects charge, so the current in its wire equals the rate of change of the plate's charge.

For the plate whose charge is $Q$, the current $I$ in its wire, counted toward the plate, satisfies $I = dQ/dt$. The opposite plate loses charge at the same rate, so the same current leaves it through its own wire. The capacitor as a whole stores no net charge. The current into one side therefore equals the current out of the other side. Wire junctions next to a capacitor still obey the junction rule at every instant, even while the currents change.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Four-Wire Junction
>Four wires meet at a junction. Wire 1 carries $3.0\ \text{A}$ into it, and wire 2 carries $0.50\ \text{A}$ into it. Wire 3 carries $1.2\ \text{A}$ out. The arrow for wire 4 is drawn into the junction.
>
>>[!example]- Find the current in wire 4 and its true direction.
>>Counting currents in as positive and currents out as negative, the junction rule gives
>>$$3.0 + 0.50 - 1.2 + I_4 = 0.$$
>>Solving gives $I_4 = -2.3\ \text{A}$. The minus sign means that the current flows against its arrow. Wire 4 carries $2.3\ \text{A}$ out of the junction. With the arrow drawn outward, the same steps give $+2.3\ \text{A}$.

>[!example] (Medium) Three Junctions, Two Equations
>A circuit has junctions $a$, $b$, and $c$ and five segments, each drawn with a current arrow. Segment 1 holds a source and carries $I_1$ from $c$ to $a$. Segments 2 and 3 are separate resistors, each with its arrow from $a$ to $b$. Segments 4 and 5 are two more separate resistors. Segment 4 carries $I_4$ from $b$ to $c$, and segment 5 carries $I_5$ from $c$ to $b$. A solution of the circuit gives $I_1 = 4.0\ \text{A}$, $I_2 = 1.5\ \text{A}$, and $I_4 = 3.0\ \text{A}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find $I_3$ and $I_5$ with their true directions. Show that the third junction equation adds nothing.
>>At $a$, $I_1$ enters, and $I_2$ and $I_3$ leave:
>>$$I_1 - I_2 - I_3 = 0, \qquad I_3 = 4.0 - 1.5 = 2.5\ \text{A}.$$
>>This current is positive, so it flows from $a$ to $b$ along its arrow. At $b$, $I_2$, $I_3$, and $I_5$ enter, and $I_4$ leaves:
>>$$I_2 + I_3 + I_5 - I_4 = 0, \qquad I_5 = 3.0 - 1.5 - 2.5 = -1.0\ \text{A}.$$
>>The minus sign means that $1.0\ \text{A}$ flows from $b$ to $c$ in segment 5, against its arrow. Segments 4 and 5 both carry current from $b$ to $c$.
>>
>>At $c$, $I_4$ enters, and $I_1$ and $I_5$ leave, so $I_4 - I_1 - I_5 = 0$. The values give $3.0 - 4.0 - (-1.0) = 0$, which holds automatically. This equation is minus the sum of the other two. Three junctions give only two independent equations.

>[!example] (Hard) Charging Rate at a Junction
>A wire brings current into junction $P$. From $P$, one wire runs through a $4.0\ \text{k}\Omega$ resistor to point $B$. A second wire runs from $P$ to one plate of a $10\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor, whose other plate connects to $B$. A wire from $B$ returns the current to the rest of the circuit. At one instant the incoming current is $2.0\ \text{mA}$. The capacitor then has $6.0\ \text{V}$ across it, with the plate at $P$ positive.
>
>>[!example]- Find the current to the capacitor and the rate at which its potential difference grows at that instant. If the incoming current stayed at $2.0\ \text{mA}$, at what potential difference would the capacitor stop charging?
>>The resistor and the capacitor both run from $P$ to $B$, so they are in parallel. The resistor has $6.0\ \text{V}$ across it and carries $6.0/4000 = 1.5\ \text{mA}$. The junction rule at $P$ gives the current toward the capacitor:
>>$$I_C = 2.0 - 1.5 = 0.50\ \text{mA}.$$
>>This current flows onto the positive plate, so $dQ/dt = I_C$. With $Q = CV$ and a fixed $C$, the potential difference grows at
>>$$\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{I_C}{C} = \frac{0.50 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{A}}{10 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{F}} = 50\ \text{V/s}.$$
>>This rate holds only at that instant, since $I_C$ shrinks as $V$ grows. The capacitor stops charging when $I_C = 0$. All $2.0\ \text{mA}$ then passes through the resistor, which needs $V = (2.0 \times 10^{-3})(4000) = 8.0\ \text{V}$.
