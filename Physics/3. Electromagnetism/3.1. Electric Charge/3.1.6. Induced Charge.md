>[!note] Induced Charge
>An **induced charge** is a separation of charge inside a neutral object, caused by a charge nearby. When a charged rod comes near a neutral conductor, the [[3.1.3. Conductors and Insulators|free electrons]] in the conductor move:
>- A negative rod pushes electrons to the far side. The near side is left with exposed positive ions and turns positive.
>- A positive rod pulls electrons to the near side. The near side turns negative and the far side turns positive.
>
>The conductor's net charge stays zero, by [[3.1.2. Conservation of Charge|conservation of charge]]. It now has a positive end and a negative end of equal size. A pair of equal and opposite charges a short distance apart is a **dipole**. One produced this way is an **induced dipole**.
>
>The induced charge on the near side always has the sign opposite to the rod. By [[3.1.4. Coulomb's Law|Coulomb's law]] the force falls off with distance. The attraction to the near side is larger than the repulsion from the far side. A charged object therefore attracts a neutral object, whatever the sign of its charge. This leads to a test for charge:
>- **Repulsion** between a charged rod and an object means the object carries charge of the same sign as the rod.
>- **Attraction** means the object carries the opposite charge or is neutral. Attraction alone cannot tell which.
>
>When the rod is taken away, the electrons spread back out and the induced charges disappear.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not cover charging by induction. In that method a conductor is grounded while the rod is near, and it keeps a net charge. Giancoli section 21.4 describes it.

# Intuition

Hang two identical metal spheres from threads. A negatively charged rod held near the first sphere pushes it away. Held near the second sphere, the rod pulls it closer. The first sphere must be negative, since only like charges repel. The second sphere could be positive. It could also be neutral, and the rod would still pull it.

A neutral metal sphere is a lattice of positive ions inside a sea of free electrons. The negative rod repels those electrons, and they crowd to the far side of the sphere. That leaves the positive ions on the near side uncovered. The near side is now positive and closer to the rod. The far side is negative and farther away. Closer charges push and pull harder, so the attraction is larger and the sphere swings toward the rod. A positive rod gives the same result with every sign reversed.

The animation brings a negative rod up to a neutral metal sphere. The free electrons move to the far side. The bare ions on the near side are pulled harder than the far electrons are pushed. The last frame uses a positive rod. The electrons now gather on the near side, and the sphere is still attracted.

# Properties

#### *(Why the Near Side Dominates)* The induced charges are equal in size, so the net force comes entirely from their different distances.

Let a rod with charge $Q$ sit a distance $r_1$ from the near induced charge. Its distance to the far one is $r_2 > r_1$. Let the induced charges be $\mp q$, with the near one opposite in sign to $Q$. [[3.1.5. Superposition Principle|Superposition]] gives a net attraction of
$$F = k\,|Q|\,q\left(\frac{1}{r_1^2} - \frac{1}{r_2^2}\right).$$
The bracket is positive whenever $r_1 < r_2$. As the rod moves away, the ratio $r_2/r_1$ approaches $1$, and the two terms nearly cancel. The induced charge $q$ also shrinks, because a farther rod pushes the electrons less. The attraction to a neutral object therefore fades much faster with distance than the force between two charges.

#### *(Charge Shuttle)* A neutral conducting ball between charged plates swings to one plate, picks up charge, and is pushed to the other.

A classroom demonstration hangs a ping-pong ball coated with metal paint between two plates with opposite charges. The plates induce a dipole in the ball. The ball can never hang exactly in the middle, so one plate is closer and its pull is larger. The ball swings to that plate and touches it. The contact gives the ball some charge of that plate's sign. Now the plate repels the ball and the other plate attracts it, so the ball swings across. There it gives up its charge, picks up the opposite sign, and swings back. Each round trip carries charge from one plate to the other. With the plates disconnected from their supply, the plates would slowly discharge. The total charge of the plates and the ball would stay fixed.

#### *(Insulators Polarize Too)* In an insulator each atom becomes a tiny induced dipole, so neutral insulators are also attracted to charged objects.

The electrons of an insulator cannot travel through it. A nearby charge can still pull each atom's electron cloud slightly to one side of its nucleus. The cloud stretches from a sphere into an oval, and each atom becomes a small induced dipole. The effect is far weaker than in a conductor. It is still why a charged comb picks up scraps of paper. Some molecules, such as water, are dipoles even without a nearby charge. These are **permanent dipoles**. The note [[3.2.6. Electric Dipole]] gives the field and torque of a dipole, and [[3.5.4. Dielectrics]] uses polarized insulators inside capacitors.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) The Rolling Can
>An empty aluminum can lies on its side on a table. A glass rod rubbed with silk carries a positive charge and is held near the can, without touching.
>
>>[!example]- Which way does the can roll, and what is its net charge?
>>The positive rod pulls free electrons to the near side of the can, which turns negative. The far side is left positive. The near side is closer, so its attraction is larger than the far side's repulsion. The can rolls toward the rod. Its net charge stays zero throughout.

>[!example] (Medium) Three Hanging Spheres
>A negatively charged rod repels sphere A and attracts spheres B and C. When B and C are brought near each other, they attract.
>
>>[!example]- What can be concluded about each sphere?
>>Repulsion means A is negative. Attraction to a negative rod means B is positive or neutral, and the same holds for C. If both were positive, they would repel each other. If both were neutral, neither could induce charge in the other, and they would feel no force. Their attraction therefore means exactly one of them is positive and the other is neutral. The data cannot say which is which.

>[!example] (Hard) How Fast the Pull Fades
>A small rod with charge $-50\ \text{nC}$ is held near a neutral metal block. The induced charges are $\pm 2.0\ \text{nC}$, at $5.0\ \text{cm}$ and $8.0\ \text{cm}$ from the rod. Treat all three charges as points on one line.
>
>>[!example]- Find the net force on the block. Then move the rod $5.0\ \text{cm}$ farther away, keep the induced charges at $\pm 2.0\ \text{nC}$, and compare.
>>The near charge is $+2.0\ \text{nC}$ and is attracted with
>>$$F_1 = (8.99 \times 10^{9})\,\frac{(50 \times 10^{-9})(2.0 \times 10^{-9})}{(0.050)^2} = 3.60 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{N}.$$
>>The far charge is repelled with
>>$$F_2 = (8.99 \times 10^{9})\,\frac{(50 \times 10^{-9})(2.0 \times 10^{-9})}{(0.080)^2} = 1.40 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{N}.$$
>>The net force is $2.19 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{N}$ toward the rod. With the rod $5.0\ \text{cm}$ farther away, the distances are $10\ \text{cm}$ and $13\ \text{cm}$. The two forces become $8.99 \times 10^{-5}$ and $5.32 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{N}$, and the net is $3.67 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{N}$. Doubling the distance to the near charge cut the net force by a factor of $6.0$. Two point charges would drop by a factor of $4$. In a real block the induced charges would also shrink, so the true drop is larger still.
