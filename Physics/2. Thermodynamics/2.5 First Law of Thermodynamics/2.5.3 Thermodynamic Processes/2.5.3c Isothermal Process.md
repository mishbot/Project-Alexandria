>[!note] Isothermal Process
>An **isothermal process** changes the state of a gas while its temperature stays fixed. On a $P$-$V$ diagram it follows an **isotherm**, the hyperbola $PV = NkT$ with $T$ constant, so $P \propto 1/V$. The [[2.5.1 Thermodynamic Work|work]] done by the gas is the area under the hyperbola,
>$$W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} \frac{NkT}{V}\,dV = NkT \ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} = P_1 V_1 \ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} = P_2 V_2 \ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}.$$
>The constant $NkT$ comes out of the integral. The integral of $1/V$ is the logarithm, and a difference of logarithms is the logarithm of the quotient,
>$$\int_{V_1}^{V_2} \frac{dV}{V} = \ln V_2 - \ln V_1 = \ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}.$$
>The last two forms of $W$ use $P_1 V_1 = P_2 V_2 = NkT$ and are handy when the temperature is not given. Expansion gives $V_2 > V_1$, a positive logarithm, and $W > 0$. Compression gives $W < 0$. Since $V_2 / V_1 = P_1 / P_2$ on an isotherm, the ratio can also be written with pressures.
>
>The [[2.4.1 Internal Energy|internal energy]] of an ideal gas depends on temperature alone, so
>$$\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0, \qquad Q = W.$$
>Every joule of heat that enters leaves as work, and every joule of work done on the gas leaves as heat. There is heat flow with no change in temperature, so no formula of the form $Q = C\,\Delta T$ can describe this process. An isothermal process is realized by keeping the gas in contact with a large reservoir at fixed temperature, such as an ice bath at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The piston must move slowly enough that heat keeps up.

# Intuition

Let a gas expand while it sits in a bath of ice water. As the piston moves out the gas does work and would cool, but the bath feeds heat in as fast as the gas can spend it, and the temperature never moves. The molecules end the process with the same average kinetic energy they started with. Everything the bath supplied went out through the piston as work. This is the process a physicist likes best, because the conversion of heat into work is complete. The price is speed: the process must be slow enough for the bath to keep the gas at its temperature at every instant. The sketch shows the setup.

# Properties

#### *(Heat Becomes Work Completely)* In an isothermal expansion the heat absorbed equals the work done, with nothing left over.

The internal energy cannot change, so the first law leaves $Q = W$ with no other term. This is the largest fraction of heat that any single expansion can turn into work, and it is the reason the [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot cycle]] uses isothermal legs to take in and give out heat. The conversion runs both ways. An isothermal compression turns work into heat completely, and the heat leaves into the bath.

#### *(The Logarithm Is the Volume Ratio)* Doubling the volume always costs the same work, $NkT \ln 2$, whatever the starting volume.

The work depends on the ratio $V_2 / V_1$ and not on the volumes separately. Expanding from $1$ to $2$ liters does the same work as expanding from $10$ to $20$ liters at the same temperature and the same $N$. This is the signature of a $1/V$ pressure curve. Each doubling of volume halves the pressure and adds the same area.

#### *(Slow by Definition)* An isothermal process must be slow enough for heat to flow in or out as fast as work is done.

A fast expansion outruns the reservoir, the gas cools, and the process is closer to [[2.5.3d Adiabatic Process|adiabatic]]. A real process lies between the two. The isothermal and adiabatic curves through one point are the two limits, and the adiabatic curve is the steeper one. Which limit applies depends on the ratio of the time the process takes to the time heat needs to cross the container.

#### *(No Specific Heat)* The ratio $Q / \Delta T$ is infinite for an isothermal process, so no heat capacity describes it.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not discuss a heat capacity for an isothermal process. This property is a consequence of $Q = W$ and is standard textbook material.

Heat flows and the temperature does not change. A formula like $Q = n C\,\Delta T$ would need $C = \infty$. This is the clearest case of the rule that the heat absorbed by a gas depends on the process and must be computed from the [[2.5.2 First Law of Thermodynamics|first law]], not looked up. The [[2.5.3a Isovolumetric Process|isovolumetric]] and [[2.5.3b Isobaric Process|isobaric]] processes happen to give $Q \propto \Delta T$, and that is a feature of those two paths rather than a property of the gas.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Isothermal Expansion
>Half a mole of an ideal gas at $300\ \text{K}$ expands slowly from $2.00\ \text{L}$ to $6.00\ \text{L}$ while in contact with a bath at $300\ \text{K}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the work done and the heat absorbed.
>>The work is the logarithm formula,
>>$$W = nRT \ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} = (0.500)(8.314)(300) \ln 3 = 1.37 \times 10^{3}\ \text{J}.$$
>>The temperature is fixed, so $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$ and $Q = W = 1.37\ \text{kJ}$. The bath supplied every joule that the gas delivered to the piston.

>[!example] (Medium) Compressing Air into a Quarter of Its Volume
>One liter of air at $1.00\ \text{atm}$ and $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is compressed slowly to $0.250\ \text{L}$ while held at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the final pressure, the work, and the heat.
>>On an isotherm $P_2 = P_1 V_1 / V_2 = 4.00\ \text{atm}$. The work uses $P_1 V_1$ in place of $NkT$,
>>$$W = P_1 V_1 \ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} = (1.013 \times 10^{5})(1.00 \times 10^{-3}) \ln\frac{1}{4} = (101.3\ \text{J})(-1.386) = -140\ \text{J}.$$
>>The surroundings did $140\ \text{J}$ of work on the gas. Since $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$, $Q = W = -140\ \text{J}$: the gas gave $140\ \text{J}$ of heat to the bath. Without the bath the same compression would have heated the air, and the [[2.5.3d Adiabatic Process|adiabatic]] note works that case out.

>[!example] (Hard) Two Isotherms Compared
>Two samples of the same gas, one at $300\ \text{K}$ and one at $600\ \text{K}$, each contain $N$ molecules and each expand isothermally from $V$ to $3V$.
>
>>[!example]- Compare the work done, the heat absorbed, and the final pressures.
>>The work is $NkT \ln 3$ in both cases, so the hot sample does twice the work and absorbs twice the heat of the cold one. The volume ratio is the same, so the pressure falls by the factor $3$ in both, but the hot sample starts at twice the pressure and ends at twice the pressure. On the diagram the hot isotherm lies above the cold one at every volume and encloses twice the area between $V$ and $3V$. Work along an isotherm scales with the temperature of the isotherm, which is the fact the [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot efficiency]] $1 - T_L / T_H$ rests on.
