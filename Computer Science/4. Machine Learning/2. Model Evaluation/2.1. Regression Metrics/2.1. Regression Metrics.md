>[!note] Regression Metrics
>**Regression metrics** score numeric predictions $\hat y_i$ against true values $y_i \in \mathbb{R}$. Most of them average a pointwise loss $\ell(y, \hat y)$ that depends only on the residual $e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$:
>$$\hat R = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell(y_i, \hat y_i).$$
>Here $n$ is the number of predictions, and $\hat R$ is the empirical risk on those points. The same average serves as a training loss on the training set and as an evaluation metric on held-out data. The family has four members:
>
>- [[2.1.1. Mean Squared Error|Mean squared error]] (MSE) averages $e_i^2$. Its square root, the RMSE, is in the units of $y$.
>- [[2.1.2. Mean Absolute Error|Mean absolute error]] (MAE) averages $|e_i|$.
>- [[2.1.3. Huber Loss|Huber loss]] is quadratic for $|e_i| \le \delta$ and linear beyond the threshold $\delta$.
>- [[2.1.4. R-Squared|R-squared]] ($R^2$) is $1 - \mathrm{SSE}/\mathrm{SST}$, the share of the variance of $y$ that the model explains. It is a ratio of two sums of squares, so it has no pointwise form.
>
>The decision rule is as follows:
>
>- **MSE or RMSE** is the default metric to report and the default loss to train with.
>- **MAE** fits when a few large errors dominate or when the median is the quantity of interest.
>- **Huber loss** is the training loss when both matter, smooth gradients near zero and robustness to a few large errors.
>- **$R^2$** compares the quality of fit across targets measured on different scales.
>
>Some data contain a large fraction of gross mismatches, such as wrong feature matches in vision. A robust loss is not enough there, and [[4.4.1. RANSAC]] fits the model to a consensus subset and ignores the other points.

# Intuition

All four metrics start from the residuals. They differ in how fast the penalty grows with the size of a residual. Squared error grows quadratically, so a residual of $10$ costs $100$ times as much as a residual of $1$. Absolute error grows linearly, so the same residual costs $10$ times as much. A squared-error fit therefore moves toward its largest errors. An absolute-error fit gives each point a pull of fixed size. Huber loss is quadratic for small residuals and linear for large ones.

Each loss has its own best constant prediction, the mean under squared error and the median under absolute error. A faster-growing loss also lets one outlier move the fit further. The losses differ in efficiency and robustness. An efficient estimator has low variance for a given amount of data. The mean is the most efficient choice under Gaussian noise. A robust estimator stays near the truth when a few points are corrupted, and the median is the standard example. $R^2$ differs from the other three in another way. It divides the squared error by the variance of $y$, so it has no units. It can therefore compare a model of prices with a model of delivery times.

# Comparison Table

| Metric | Formula | Units | Minimizer | Outlier sensitivity | Differentiable at 0 | Probabilistic view | Default use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [[2.1.1. Mean Squared Error\|MSE]] (and RMSE) | $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i e_i^2$ (RMSE $= \sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}}$) | $y^2$ (RMSE: $y$) | mean | high, a residual's pull grows with its size | yes | Gaussian noise | default metric and training loss |
| [[2.1.2. Mean Absolute Error\|MAE]] | $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \lvert e_i\rvert$ | $y$ | median | low, every pull has size $1$ | no, subgradient $[-1, 1]$ | Laplace noise | typical error with outliers, median forecasts |
| [[2.1.3. Huber Loss\|Huber]] | $\frac{1}{2}e^2$ for $\lvert e\rvert \le \delta$, else $\delta(\lvert e\rvert - \frac{1}{2}\delta)$ | $y^2$ | median as $\delta \to 0$, mean for large $\delta$ | low, pulls capped at $\delta$ | yes, the second derivative jumps at $\pm\delta$ | Gaussian center, Laplace tails | training with occasional outliers, box regression |
| [[2.1.4. R-Squared\|$R^2$]] | $1 - \mathrm{SSE}/\mathrm{SST}$ | none | same fit as MSE | high, as MSE | yes, as SSE | none, a descriptive ratio | comparing fit across targets, OLS summaries |

# When to Use Which

Start with MSE as the training loss and RMSE as the reported number. RMSE is in the units of $y$, so a reader can compare it with typical values of the target. [[3.1. Linear Regression|Least squares]] and most regression software minimize squared error, and its gradient is smooth everywhere. Report MAE instead when a few large errors would dominate RMSE, or when readers care about the typical error. Train with MAE when the median is the right point forecast. A forecaster wants the median when each unit of error costs the same in both directions. Train with Huber loss when the noise is mostly Gaussian with occasional outliers. It keeps the efficiency of squared error near zero and caps the pull of large residuals. A common threshold is $\delta = 1.345\,\hat\sigma$, with $\hat\sigma$ a robust estimate of the noise scale. Report $R^2$ next to RMSE when readers need a unitless number or must compare targets on different scales. Compute it on held-out data for model comparison, where it can be negative. MAE and Huber loss only cap the pull of large residuals. A point with an extreme input can still move the fit far. Both losses fail when outliers make up a large share of the data. Use [[4.4.1. RANSAC]] in those cases.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) One Late Delivery, Four Scores
>A delivery app predicts six delivery times in minutes. The true times are $y = [18, 22, 25, 30, 35, 70]$, and the predictions are $\hat y = [20, 21, 27, 29, 33, 40]$. The last delivery hit a road closure.
>
>>[!example]- Compute MSE, RMSE, MAE and $R^2$ with and without the last delivery. Which metric moves most?
>>The residuals are $e = [-2, 1, -2, 1, 2, 30]$. With all six points, $\mathrm{MSE} = (4 + 1 + 4 + 1 + 4 + 900)/6 = 152.3$, so $\mathrm{RMSE} = 12.34$ minutes. The MAE is $(2 + 1 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 30)/6 = 6.33$ minutes. The mean of $y$ is $33.3$, $\mathrm{SST} = 1791.3$ and $\mathrm{SSE} = 914$, so $R^2 = 0.49$. Without the last delivery, $\mathrm{RMSE} = 1.67$, $\mathrm{MAE} = 1.60$ and $R^2 = 0.92$. One point multiplies RMSE by $7.4$ and MAE by $4.0$. The ratio RMSE/MAE rises from $1.05$ to $1.95$, which signals that a few errors are much larger than the rest. With $\delta = 5$, Huber loss charges the late delivery $5 \cdot (30 - 2.5) = 137.5$, against $\frac{1}{2} \cdot 30^2 = 450$ under half the squared error.
