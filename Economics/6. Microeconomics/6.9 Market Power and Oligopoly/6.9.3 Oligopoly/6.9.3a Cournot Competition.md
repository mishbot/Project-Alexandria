>[!note] Cournot Competition
>In **Cournot competition**, firms choose quantities at the same time, and the price adjusts until consumers buy the total output. With two firms and costs $c_i(y_i) = c\,y_i$, firm $i \in \{1, 2\}$ takes the rival's quantity $y_{-i}$ as given and solves
>$$\max_{y_i}\ p(y_i + y_{-i})\,y_i - c\,y_i .$$
>A **Cournot equilibrium** is a [[2.2c Nash Equilibrium|Nash equilibrium]] in quantities: $y_1^*$ is optimal given $y_2^*$, and $y_2^*$ is optimal given $y_1^*$. An interior equilibrium solves both first-order conditions at once,
>$$p'_Y(y_1^* + y_2^*)\,y_1^* + p - c = 0, \qquad p'_Y(y_2^* + y_1^*)\,y_2^* + p - c = 0 ,$$
>where $p'_Y$ is the slope of inverse demand at total output $Y = y_1 + y_2$.
>- With linear inverse demand $p(Y) = a - bY$, where $a > c > 0$ and $b > 0$, firm $i$'s [[2.2a Best Response|best response]], also called its **reaction function**, is
>$$y_i^*(y_{-i}) = \max\left\{0,\ \frac{a-c}{2b} - \frac{y_{-i}}{2}\right\}.$$
>- The two best-response lines cross once, at the unique equilibrium
>$$y_1^* = y_2^* = \frac{a-c}{3b}, \qquad Y_D^* = 2\,\frac{a-c}{3b}, \qquad p_D^* = \frac13 a + \frac23 c, \qquad \pi_i^* = \frac{(a-c)^2}{9b}.$$
>- Cournot firms price above marginal cost. Each FOC gives $p - c = -p'_Y(Y)\,y_i > 0$. Total output lies strictly between the [[6.9.1 Monopoly|monopoly]] output $\frac{a-c}{2b}$ and the competitive output $\frac{a-c}{b}$.
>- The second-order condition is $2p'_Y(Y) + p''_{YY}(Y)\,y_i < 0$. For linear demand it reads $-2b < 0$ and always holds.
>- Cournot is the duopoly model with quantities chosen at the same time. [[6.9.3b Bertrand Competition|Bertrand]] firms choose prices instead and drive the price down to $c$. In [[6.9.3c Stackelberg Competition|Stackelberg]] one firm chooses its quantity first, which raises total output and lowers the price.

# Intuition

Two cement plants serve one town. Each plant sets its output before the season. All the cement then goes to market, and the price falls until buyers take it. Every ton from either plant lowers the price, so each plant's profit depends on the other's output. A plant that expects a large rival output faces a small leftover market and produces less. A plant that expects a small rival output produces more. An equilibrium is a pair of outputs where each plant's guess about the other is correct and neither wants to change.

This is a [[2.1 Strategic Form Game|game in strategic form]] with players $1, \dots, I$, strategies $s_i \in S_i$, and payoffs $U_i(s_i, s_{-i})$. A profile $s^* = (s_i^*, s_{-i}^*)$ is a Nash equilibrium if $U_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \ge U_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*)$ for all $s_i \in S_i$ and every player $i$. Each strategy is then a best response to the others, so the equilibrium is a fixed point of the best responses, where the best-response curves cross. In Cournot the players are the firms, the strategies are quantities $y_i \ge 0$, and the payoffs are profits. The figure shows the two reaction functions crossing at the Cournot point $C$. The animated path is a way to read the fixed point. Firm 1 starts at the monopoly output, firm 2 best-responds, firm 1 best-responds to that, and so on. The outputs approach $C$, the one point where each output is a best response to the other. The model itself has no such rounds, since both firms choose once.

# Properties

#### *(Residual Demand)* Each firm acts as a monopolist on the demand that its rival leaves over.

With $y_{-i}$ fixed, firm $i$ faces the **residual demand** $p = (a - b\,y_{-i}) - b\,y_i$. This line has the slope of market demand and an intercept lowered by $b\,y_{-i}$. Its marginal revenue is $a - b\,y_{-i} - 2b\,y_i$, and setting it equal to $c$ gives the best response. When the rival produces one more unit, the residual demand shifts down and the firm's optimal output falls by $1/2$. Quantities are therefore **strategic substitutes**. If $y_{-i} \ge (a-c)/b$, the residual demand lies below $c$ at every positive output, and the best response is $y_i = 0$. The figure uses $a = 100$, $b = 1$, $c = 10$, and $y_2 = 30$.

#### *(Counting the Price Drop)* A Cournot firm counts the price drop on its own units only, so Cournot firms together produce more than a monopolist and less than price takers.

One more unit of output lowers the price by $|p'_Y(Y)|$ on every unit sold. The three first-order conditions differ only in how many of those units the firm counts,
$$\underbrace{p = c'_y(y)}_{\text{price taker}}, \qquad \underbrace{p + p'_Y(Y)\,y_i = c'_y(y_i)}_{\text{Cournot firm}}, \qquad \underbrace{p + p'_Y(Y)\,Y = c'_y(Y)}_{\text{monopolist}}.$$
A price taker counts none of them, and a monopolist counts all $Y$ units. A Cournot firm counts its own $y_i$ units. The price drop on the rival's $y_{-i}$ units is the rival's loss, and firm $i$ ignores it. So the two firms together produce more than the output that maximizes their joint profit. Each firm still counts the drop on its own units, so it stops before the price reaches $c$.

#### *(Markup and Market Share)* A firm's percentage markup equals its market share divided by the elasticity of market demand, in absolute value.

Divide firm $i$'s FOC $p'_Y(Y)\,y_i + p - c_i = 0$ by $p$. With market share $s_i = y_i/Y$ and the elasticity $\varepsilon_{Y,p} = \frac{dY}{dp}\frac{p}{Y} < 0$,
$$\frac{p - c_i}{p} = -p'_Y(Y)\,\frac{Y}{p}\cdot\frac{y_i}{Y} = -\frac{s_i}{\varepsilon_{Y,p}}.$$
With $s_i = 1$ this is the monopoly markup $-1/\varepsilon_{Y,p}$. A small firm has a small markup. With linear demand the FOC reads $p - c_i = b\,y_i$, so $\pi_i = b\,y_i^2$. A firm with lower cost therefore has a larger share, a larger markup, and a larger profit.

#### *(Many Firms)* With $n$ identical firms, the Cournot price falls toward marginal cost as $n$ grows.

The symmetric solution of the $n$ linear FOCs is $y_i^* = \frac{a-c}{(n+1)b}$, so $Y^* = \frac{n}{n+1}\cdot\frac{a-c}{b}$ and $p^* = \frac{a + nc}{n+1}$. With $n = 1$ this is the monopoly outcome, and with $n = 2$ it is the duopoly above. As $n \to \infty$, the price falls to $c$ and output rises to the competitive level $(a-c)/b$.

# Derivation

We want the equilibrium for $p(Y) = a - bY$ and cost $c\,y_i$. Firm 1's profit is $\pi_1 = (a - by_1 - by_2)\,y_1 - c\,y_1$. By [[7.1 Unconstrained Optimality Conditions|the first-order condition]],
$$\frac{\partial \pi_1}{\partial y_1} = a - 2by_1 - by_2 - c = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y_1^* = \frac{a-c}{2b} - \frac{y_2}{2}.$$
The second derivative is $-2b < 0$, so this is a maximum. When $y_2 > (a-c)/b$ the formula turns negative, and the constraint $y_1 \ge 0$ binds instead, which gives the $\max\{0, \cdot\}$. Firm 2's problem is the same with the indices swapped. Substituting firm 2's best response into firm 1's gives
$$y_1 = \frac{a-c}{2b} - \frac12\left(\frac{a-c}{2b} - \frac{y_1}{2}\right) = \frac{a-c}{4b} + \frac{y_1}{4} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y_1^* = \frac{a-c}{3b},$$
and then $y_2^* = \frac{a-c}{2b} - \frac{a-c}{6b} = \frac{a-c}{3b}$. Total output is $Y_D^* = 2(a-c)/(3b)$ and the price is $p_D^* = a - \frac23(a-c) = \frac13 a + \frac23 c$. Each firm earns $(p_D^* - c)\,y_i^* = \frac{a-c}{3}\cdot\frac{a-c}{3b} = \frac{(a-c)^2}{9b}$. In the $(y_1, y_2)$ plane the interior best responses are lines with slopes $-2$ and $-1/2$, so they cross once. A corner equilibrium would need one firm at zero. If firm 1 produced zero, firm 2 would produce $(a-c)/(2b)$. That is below $(a-c)/b$, so firm 1's best response would be positive. The same holds with the firms swapped. No corner equilibrium exists, and the equilibrium is unique.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Two Cement Plants
>Two cement plants face inverse demand $p = 100 - Y$, in dollars per ton, and each has marginal cost $c = 10$. They choose outputs at the same time.
>
>>[!example]- Find the Cournot equilibrium, the price, and each plant's profit. Compare with one owner who runs both plants.
>>Here $a = 100$, $b = 1$, and $a - c = 90$. Each plant's best response is $y_i = 45 - y_{-i}/2$. The equilibrium is $y_1^* = y_2^* = 90/3 = 30$, so $Y_D^* = 60$ and $p_D^* = 40$. Each plant earns $(40 - 10)\cdot 30 = 900$. As a check, the best response to $y_2 = 30$ is $45 - 15 = 30$. One owner acts as a monopolist: output $90/2 = 45$, price $55$, profit $45 \cdot 45 = 2{,}025$. The duopoly produces $15$ more tons, charges $\$15$ less, and earns $1{,}800$ in total. Its price is still $\$30$ above marginal cost.

>[!example] (Medium) Why the Cement Cartel Breaks Down
>The plants of the Easy example agree to split the monopoly output, $22.5$ tons each.
>
>>[!example]- (a) Find each plant's profit under the agreement. (b) If plant 2 keeps the agreement, what output maximizes plant 1's profit, and what does each plant earn? (c) Treat the choice between $22.5$ and $30$ tons as a two-by-two game. Find its equilibrium.
>>(a) At $Y = 45$ the price is $55$, and each plant earns $45 \cdot 22.5 = 1{,}012.5$, more than the Cournot profit of $900$.
>>
>>(b) Plant 1's best response to $y_2 = 22.5$ is $45 - 11.25 = 33.75$. The price falls to $43.75$. Plant 1 earns $33.75 \cdot 33.75 = 1{,}139.06$ and plant 2 earns $33.75 \cdot 22.5 = 759.38$. The agreed output is not a best response, so the agreement is not a Nash equilibrium.
>>
>>(c) When one plant produces $30$ and the other $22.5$, the price is $47.5$, and the profits are $1{,}125$ and $843.75$. With plant 1's profit first, the payoffs are $(1{,}012.5,\ 1{,}012.5)$ if both produce $22.5$, $(1{,}125,\ 843.75)$ if only plant 1 produces $30$, $(843.75,\ 1{,}125)$ if only plant 2 does, and $(900,\ 900)$ if both do. Producing $30$ is a [[2.2b Strategic Dominance|dominant strategy]] for each plant, since $1{,}125 > 1{,}012.5$ and $900 > 843.75$. The unique equilibrium is $(30, 30)$, and both plants would prefer $(22.5, 22.5)$. The cartel is a prisoner's dilemma. If the game repeats forever with a small enough chance of ending each period, the plants can keep the cartel by producing $22.5$ while the rival does and $30$ forever after any deviation ([[4.1 Infinitely Repeated Game]]).

>The lecture assumed equal costs. The Hard example drops that assumption and uses the markup formula from the Properties.

>[!example] (Hard) A Plant With an Old Kiln
>Demand is still $p = 100 - Y$. Plant 1 has $c_1 = 10$, and plant 2 runs an old kiln with $c_2 = 20$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the equilibrium outputs, the price, and the profits. Check the markup formula $(p - c_i)/p = -s_i/\varepsilon_{Y,p}$.
>>The FOCs are $100 - 2y_1 - y_2 - 10 = 0$ and $100 - y_1 - 2y_2 - 20 = 0$, that is, $2y_1 + y_2 = 90$ and $y_1 + 2y_2 = 80$. Solving gives $y_1^* = 100/3 \approx 33.33$ and $y_2^* = 70/3 \approx 23.33$. Total output is $56.67$ and the price is $43.33$. Profits are $\pi_1 = 33.33 \cdot 33.33 = 1{,}111.1$ and $\pi_2 = 23.33 \cdot 23.33 = 544.4$, since $p - c_i = b\,y_i$ with $b = 1$. The shares are $s_1 = 0.588$ and $s_2 = 0.412$. With $Y = 100 - p$, the elasticity is $\varepsilon_{Y,p} = -p/Y = -0.765$. The formula gives $0.588/0.765 = 0.769$ for plant 1, and the markup is $33.33/43.33 = 0.769$. For plant 2 both sides equal $0.538$. Market demand is inelastic here, which a monopolist would never accept. A Cournot firm can, because each firm counts only its own share of the price drop.
