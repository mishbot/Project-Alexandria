>[!note] Microeconomics
>Microeconomics studies how individual consumers and firms make choices under constraints, and how those choices respond to prices and income. The first six sections cover consumer theory. A consumer ranks bundles of goods with a [[6.1 Preferences and Utility|preference relation]], summarized by a utility function $u(x_1, x_2)$ whose indifference curves have slope $\text{MRS} = -u'_{x_1}/u'_{x_2}$. In [[6.2 Consumer Choice|consumer choice]] the consumer picks the best bundle on the budget line $p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 = M$. The result is the Marshallian demand $x_i^*(\mathbf{p}, M)$, and the dual problem of minimizing spending gives the Hicksian demand $h_i(\mathbf{p}, \bar u)$.
>
>[[6.3 Comparative Statics]] asks how demand moves when income or a price changes. The [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation|Slutsky equation]] splits every price effect into a substitution effect and an income effect, and the signs of the cross effects define complements and substitutes. [[6.4 Utility Function Families]] compares the four standard utility functions, Cobb-Douglas, perfect substitutes, perfect complements, and CES, with their demands. [[6.5 Applications]] applies the same tools to labor supply, saving and borrowing over two periods, discrimination in the labor market, altruism and charitable giving, and present-biased preferences that make plans time inconsistent. [[6.6 Uncertainty and Risk]] extends the consumer to choices made before income is known. A lottery is ranked by its expected utility $\sum_i p_i\,U(M_i)$, and a concave $U$ makes a person risk averse. The same model explains why people buy insurance and why a risk-averse investor still holds some stock when its expected excess return is positive.
>
>The next three sections cover firms and markets. [[6.7 Production and Costs]] describes technology by a production function $y = f(L, K)$, whose isoquants have slope $\text{MRTS} = -f'_L/f'_K$. The firm solves its problem in two steps. It first finds the cheapest inputs for each output, which gives the cost function $c(w, r, y)$. It then picks the output where price equals marginal cost, $p = c'_y$. Supply is the marginal cost curve above average cost. [[6.8 Competitive Markets]] adds individual demands and firm supplies into market curves. The equilibrium price solves $Y^S(p^*) = X^D(p^*)$, and the implicit function theorem gives its response to shocks, including the incidence of a tax. Consumer and producer surplus measure gains from trade in dollars. They give the deadweight loss of a tax, the gains from opening to trade, and the costs of rent control. With free entry, the long-run price equals minimum average cost. [[6.9 Market Power and Oligopoly]] drops price taking. A monopolist sets marginal revenue equal to marginal cost and charges the markup $(p - c'_y)/p = -1/\varepsilon_{y,p}$. With a few firms, each firm's best output depends on its rivals' choices, so the Cournot, Bertrand, and Stackelberg models use game theory. The section ends with second-price auctions, where bidding one's value is weakly dominant.
>
>The math behind these notes lives in the Mathematics folder. The [[2.5. Implicit Function Theorem]] gives the slope of indifference curves and every comparative-statics derivative. [[7.1 Unconstrained Optimality Conditions]], [[7.2 Lagrange Multipliers]], and the [[7.3 Envelope Theorem]] give the first- and second-order conditions and the derivatives of optimal values. [[2.5a Expectation]] supplies expected values and Jensen's inequality for the uncertainty notes. The oligopoly and auction notes link to the game theory notes, such as [[2.2c Nash Equilibrium]] and [[3.2b Subgame Perfect Nash Equilibrium]].
>
>These notes follow ECON 101A at Berkeley, Lectures 1 to 11 of Fall 2026. The consumer-theory sections also follow Nicholson's *Microeconomic Theory*, chapters 2 to 6, 16, and 17. Income is $M$. The MRS is the signed slope $dx_2/dx_1$ of an indifference curve, so the tangency condition reads $\text{MRS} = -p_1/p_2$. The MRTS is likewise the signed slope $dK/dL$ of an isoquant, so cost minimization gives $\text{MRTS} = -w/r$. Lagrangians subtract the constraint, $\mathcal{L} = f - \lambda(h - c)$, so multipliers on binding budget constraints are positive. # Contents

- [[6.1 Preferences and Utility]]: preference relations, monotone and convex preferences, utility functions and ordinality, indifference curves and the MRS.
- [[6.2 Consumer Choice]]: the budget constraint, utility maximization, corner solutions, indirect utility, and expenditure minimization.
- [[6.3 Comparative Statics]]: the method, homogeneity of demand, income and price changes, the Slutsky equation, and complements and substitutes.
- [[6.4 Utility Function Families]]: Cobb-Douglas, perfect substitutes, perfect complements, and CES, compared side by side.
- [[6.5 Applications]]: opportunity cost, labor supply, intertemporal choice, taste-based discrimination, altruism and charitable donations, and time consistency.
- [[6.6 Uncertainty and Risk]]: expected utility, risk aversion and the Arrow-Pratt measures, insurance, and investment in a risky asset.
- [[6.7 Production and Costs]]: production functions, returns to scale, cost minimization, cost curves, profit maximization and supply, and one-step profit maximization.
- [[6.8 Competitive Markets]]: aggregation, short-run equilibrium, elasticities, tax incidence, consumer and producer surplus, welfare applications, and long-run equilibrium with free entry.
- [[6.9 Market Power and Oligopoly]]: monopoly, price discrimination, Cournot, Bertrand, and Stackelberg oligopoly, and second-price auctions.

# Recommended Path

Read the sections in order, since every note uses only earlier notes and the math notes. For a fast review before an exam, read the callouts of [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization]], [[6.2.5 Expenditure Minimization]], [[6.3.5 Slutsky Equation]], and [[6.3.6 Complements and Substitutes]], then the comparison table in [[6.4 Utility Function Families]]. Then work the examples in [[6.5.2 Labor Supply]] and [[6.5.3 Intertemporal Choice]], which combine the Lagrangian, the implicit function theorem, and the substitution and income split in one problem.

For Midterm 2 review of Lectures 6 to 11, start with the callouts of [[6.5.5 Altruism and Charitable Donations]] and [[6.5.6 Time Consistency]]. Then read [[6.6.1 Expected Utility]] and [[6.6.2 Risk Aversion]], and work the Easy examples of [[6.6.3 Insurance]] and [[6.6.4 Investment in a Risky Asset]]. For firms, read the callouts of [[6.7.3 Cost Minimization]] and [[6.7.5 Profit Maximization and Supply]], the two steps of the firm's problem. Then work the Easy example of [[6.7.2 Returns to Scale]], which links returns to scale to the shape of supply. For markets, read [[6.8.2 Short-Run Market Equilibrium]] and [[6.8.4 Tax Incidence]] and work their Easy examples, which share one linear market. Then read [[6.9.1 Monopoly]], the comparison table in [[6.9.3 Oligopoly]], and the three-case proof in [[6.9.4 Second-Price Auctions]]. Each section index lists what lecture did not teach. The callouts and the Easy examples cover the taught core.
