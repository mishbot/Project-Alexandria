>[!note] Convolutional Neural Networks
>A **convolutional neural network** is a two-dimensional [[6.1. Scanning Networks|scanning network]] designed for data with spatial structure, such as images. A CNN applies the **same learned local function** (a convolutional kernel) to many overlapping spatial patches of the input, producing feature maps that detect patterns regardless of their position.
>
>
>Given an input $x \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, a convolutional layer applies a bank of $K$ filters, each of size $F \times F \times C$. The $k$-th filter produces an $(H-F+1) \times (W-F+1)$ feature map,
>$$a_{i,j,k} = \sum_{u,v,c} W^{(k)}_{u,v,c}\, x_{i+u,\, j+v,\, c} + b_k, \qquad z_{i,j,k} = h(a_{i,j,k}),$$
>where $h(\cdot)$ is a nonlinearity such as ReLU. Note that padding is frequently employed to restore the feature map to the shape of the original input, such that in general, padding by $P$ rows and columns leads to a shape of $(H+2P-F+1) \times (W+2P-F+1)$. If convolution is done with a stride of $S > 1$, then the dimension of the output is $(D+2P-F) / / S + 1$
>
>A CNN therefore inherits the key properties of scanning networks:
>- **Sparse connectivity:** each neuron sees only an $F\times F$ patch.
>- **Shared connectivity pattern:** the same kernel weights $\{W,b\}$ are reused at every spatial location.
>- **Shift equivariance:** shifting the input produces a corresponding shift in the activation map.
>
>Stacking convolution layers grows receptive fields hierarchically, enabling early layers to detect edges and later layers to capture textures, shapes, and object-level structure. Optional **pooling** layers (max-pooling or average-pooling) downsample spatial dimensions by aggregating over local neighborhoods, providing robustness to small shifts and scale variations:
>$$z^{(\ell)}_{i,j,k} = \max_{(u,v)\in P} z^{(\ell-1)}_{i+u,\, j+v,\, k}.$$ 
>
>After several such layers extract increasingly abstract features, the final spatial representation is flattened and passed to one or more **fully connected layers** that compute the output

# Examples
>[!example] Convolution Operation in CNNs
>In this question, we will look at the mechanism of weight sharing in convolutions. Suppose that we have an input vector with $D = 9$ dimensions:
>$$x=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}, x_{2}, \ldots, x_{9} \end{bmatrix}^T.$$
>We compute a 1D convolution with the kernel filter that has $K = 3$ weights (parameters):
>$$k=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}, x_{2}, x_{3} \end{bmatrix}^T.$$
>>[!example]- What’s the output dimension if we apply filter $k$ with no padding and stride of $1$? What are the first and last elements of the output?
>>Given a stride of $1$ and no padding, $D_{\text{out}}=9-3+1=7$. The first element is $k_{1}x_{1} + k_{2}x_{2}+k_{3}x_{3}$, whereas the last element is $k_{1}x_{7}+k_{2}x_{8}+k_{2}x_{9}$.
>
>>[!example]- What’s the output dimension if we apply filter $k$ with padding of size 1 and stride of 2? What are the first and last elements of the output?
>>Given a stride of $2$ and padding of $1$, we have that $D_{\text{out}}=(9-3+2 \cdot 1) / / 2 + 1=5$. The first element will be $0+k_{2}x_{1}+k_{3}x_{2}$, whereas the last element will be $k_{1}x_{8}+k_{2}x_{9}+0$.
>
>Recall that CNN filters have the property of weight sharing, meaning that different portions of an image can share the same weight to extract the same set of features. A convolution is actually a linear operation, which we can express as $x′ = Kx$ for some weight matrix $K$.
>>[!example]- Find $K$ for the convolution applied in the first part. What is its dimension?
>>$K$ has a dimension of $7\times 9$: it needs to match the shape of the input $x$ which has a dimension of $3$, as well as map the input to an output vector with dimension $7$ (as calculated in the first part). Each row has 3 nonzero elements:
>>$$K=\begin{bmatrix}k_{1} & k_{2} & k_{3} & 0 & \ldots & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & k_{1} & k_{2} & k_{3} & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\ & & & \vdots & & & \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \ldots & k_{1} & k_{2} & k_{3}\end{bmatrix}.$$
