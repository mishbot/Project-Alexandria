>[!note] Capacitance
>A **capacitor** is two conductors separated by an insulator or by empty space. The conductors are called its **plates**, whatever their shape. The EE note [[2.1g Capacitor]] treats the capacitor as a circuit element. Circuits in these notes use a few ideal elements. Each element has two ends, $A$ and $B$. The notation $V_{AB} = V_A - V_B$ means the potential at $A$ minus the potential at $B$.
>- A **wire** keeps everything connected to it at one potential, so $V_{AB} = 0$. Its length and shape do not matter.
>- A **switch** is a piece of metal that can join two terminals. When it is closed, $V_{AB} = 0$. When it is open, the rest of the circuit sets $V_{AB}$.
>- A **power supply**, such as a battery, holds a fixed potential difference $V$ between its terminals. Its symbol is one long and one short parallel line. The long line is at the higher potential, so $V_{AB} = +V$ when $A$ is on the long side.
>- A **capacitor** is drawn as two parallel lines of equal length.
>
>No point is chosen as zero potential, so no ground appears. Every statement uses potential differences only.
>
>Connect two neutral plates to a supply through a switch, and close it. The supply pulls electrons off one plate and pushes them onto the other. The first plate becomes positive and the second negative, with charges $+Q$ and $-Q$. The electrons stop moving when the potential difference between the plates equals the supply's $V$. The charge $Q$ on the positive plate is called the **charge stored** on the capacitor. The total charge on the two plates stays zero.
>
>For every capacitor, the stored charge is proportional to the potential difference:
>$$Q = CV.$$
>Here $Q$ is the size of the charge on either plate, and $V$ is the potential difference between the plates. The constant $C$ is the **capacitance**. The capacitance depends only on the shapes, sizes, and spacing of the conductors and on the material between them. It does not depend on $Q$ or $V$. Its unit is the **farad**, $1\ \text{F} = 1\ \text{C/V}$. One farad is a very large capacitance. Circuit capacitors are usually microfarads, $1\ \mu\text{F} = 10^{-6}\ \text{F}$, or picofarads, $1\ \text{pF} = 10^{-12}\ \text{F}$.
>
>Capacitors have many uses. In a capacitive touchscreen, a finger changes the capacitance at one spot, and the circuit detects where. Memory chips store each bit as a charged or an empty capacitor. Capacitors also store energy and release it quickly, as in a camera flash.
>
>To compute $C$ for any pair of conductors:
>- Put $+Q$ on one conductor and $-Q$ on the other.
>- Find the field $\vec E$ between them with [[3.3.2. Gauss's Law|Gauss's law]].
>- Integrate along any path from one conductor to the other, $V_B - V_A = -\int_A^B \vec E\cdot d\vec s$.
>- Divide, $C = Q/V$, with $V$ the size of the potential difference. The charge $Q$ cancels.

# Intuition

A capacitor holds positive and negative charge apart on two conductors. A supply moves electrons from one plate to the other through the wires. The first electron leaves a neutral plate and moves to a neutral plate, so it takes almost no work. After that, the positive plate pulls each electron back, and the negative plate repels it. Each electron therefore takes more work to move than the one before. The transfer stops when the plates' potential difference reaches the supply's value. A larger supply voltage moves more charge before that happens.

The capacitance measures how much charge a pair of conductors holds per volt. Two large plates close together hold a lot. Two small conductors far apart hold little. The geometry decides how strong a field a given charge creates. The field then decides the potential difference.

The wire rule keeps circuits simple. Every point on one piece of metal has the same potential, whatever its length or shape. A supply terminal and the plate wired to it therefore share one potential. The potential difference across the plates then equals the supply's $V$.

A supply charges two neutral plates through a switch. Electrons leave one plate and collect on the other until $V$ reaches the supply's $V_0$. The graph traces $Q = CV$ as the charge builds.

# Properties

#### *(Why $Q$ and $V$ Are Proportional)* Doubling the charge on both conductors doubles the field everywhere, so it doubles the potential difference.

Charges on a conductor in equilibrium arrange themselves so that the field inside the metal is zero. Doubling every charge keeps that arrangement, and the field inside stays zero. By [[3.1.5. Superposition Principle|superposition]], the field everywhere outside the metal doubles. The integral of the field from one conductor to the other doubles too. The ratio $Q/V$ therefore stays the same, and it depends only on the geometry and the material. The kind of metal does not enter, since every metal has zero field inside.

#### *(Stored Charge Means One Plate)* The charge $Q$ in $Q = CV$ is the charge on the positive plate, while the capacitor's net charge is zero.

The supply moves charge from one plate to the other and creates none, by [[3.1.2. Conservation of Charge|conservation of charge]]. Plates that start neutral end with $+Q$ and $-Q$. The phrase "stored charge" refers to the charge moved across. The net charge of the capacitor is still zero. Writing $2Q$ for the stored charge, or $0$, is a common mistake. A problem that gives "the charge on a capacitor" means $Q$.

#### *(No Ground Needed)* Circuit analysis needs only potential differences, so no point has to be chosen as zero potential.

A ground is a point whose potential is called zero. A circuit can have only one such point. A circuit that sits inside a larger circuit cannot choose its own. Writing every relation with $V_{AB}$ avoids the choice. The results then hold wherever the circuit sits. EE circuit courses usually choose a ground to make node analysis shorter.

#### *(Ideal Parts Charge Instantly)* With ideal wires and an ideal supply, the capacitor would charge in zero time, which no real circuit does.

Ideal wires have no resistance, and an ideal supply can deliver any current. Nothing then sets a time scale, so the charges would move instantly. Real electrons have mass, and real wires resist their motion. Real supplies also limit the current they deliver. The charging therefore takes a finite time, which [[3.7.6. RC Circuit]] computes. The final charges are the same as in the ideal circuit.

>[!example] (Easy) Charge on a Small Capacitor
>A $220\ \text{pF}$ capacitor in a phone circuit sits across $3.3\ \text{V}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the stored charge and the number of electrons the supply moved.
>>The stored charge is
>>$$Q = CV = (220 \times 10^{-12})(3.3) = 7.26 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{C}.$$
>>Each electron carries $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$, so the number moved is
>>$$N = \frac{Q}{e} = \frac{7.26 \times 10^{-10}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}} = 4.53 \times 10^{9}.$$
>>The positive plate lost these electrons, and the negative plate gained them.

>[!example] (Medium) Capacitance From a Change
>A supply holds a fixed potential difference across a capacitor. Raising the supply from $6.0\ \text{V}$ to $15.0\ \text{V}$ moves another $2.64 \times 10^{14}$ electrons from one plate to the other.
>
>>[!example]- Find the capacitance and the stored charge at $15.0\ \text{V}$.
>>The extra charge is
>>$$\Delta Q = Ne = (2.64 \times 10^{14})(1.602 \times 10^{-19}) = 4.23 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{C}.$$
>>The capacitance does not depend on $V$, so $Q = CV$ gives $\Delta Q = C\,\Delta V$. With $\Delta V = 9.0\ \text{V}$,
>>$$C = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta V} = \frac{4.23 \times 10^{-5}}{9.0} = 4.70 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{F} = 4.70\ \mu\text{F}.$$
>>At $15.0\ \text{V}$ the stored charge is $Q = CV = (4.70 \times 10^{-6})(15.0) = 7.05 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{C}$, or $70.5\ \mu\text{C}$.

>[!example] (Hard) Capacitance From a Field Measurement
>Two flat metal plates, each of area $0.050\ \text{m}^2$, face each other and connect to a $250\ \text{V}$ supply. A probe at the inner face of the positive plate reads a field of $4.0 \times 10^{5}\ \text{V/m}$. A second reading halfway across the gap gives the same value.
>
>>[!example]- Find the charge on each plate, the capacitance, and the gap. What does the probe read if the supply is raised to $400\ \text{V}$?
>>Immediately outside a conductor, the field is $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$, from [[3.3.3. Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium]]. The charge density on the inner face is therefore
>>$$\sigma = \varepsilon_0 E = (8.85 \times 10^{-12})(4.0 \times 10^{5}) = 3.54 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C/m}^2.$$
>>The charge sits on the inner face, so the plate carries $Q = \sigma A = (3.54 \times 10^{-6})(0.050) = 1.77 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{C}$. The other plate carries $-1.77 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{C}$. Dividing by the potential difference gives
>>$$C = \frac{Q}{V} = \frac{1.77 \times 10^{-7}}{250} = 7.08 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{F} = 708\ \text{pF}.$$
>>
>>The two readings agree, so the field is uniform across the gap. Then $V = Ed$, and the gap is $d = V/E = 250/(4.0 \times 10^{5}) = 6.25 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m}$, or $0.625\ \text{mm}$.
>>
>>At $400\ \text{V}$ the capacitance is unchanged, so the charge rises in proportion, to $2.83 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{C}$. The charge density and the field rise by the same factor $400/250 = 1.6$. The probe reads $E = V/d = 6.4 \times 10^{5}\ \text{V/m}$.
