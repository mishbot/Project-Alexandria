>[!note] Electric Flux
>A **flux** measures how much of a vector field passes through a surface. The idea applies to many fields, such as flows of energy, mass, or charge. Light from a lamp carries energy, and its **energy flux** $\vec S$ points along the light. Its size is the energy per second crossing one square meter, in $\text{W/m}^2$. The vector $\vec S$ is also called the Poynting vector.
>
>The orientation of a surface is described by its **area vector** $\vec A$. This vector is perpendicular to a flat surface, and its length equals the surface's area. For a parallelogram with edge vectors $\vec a$ and $\vec b$, the area vector is the [[3.3. Vector Multiplication|cross product]]
>$$\vec A = \vec a \times \vec b.$$
>Let $\phi$ be the angle between $\vec a$ and $\vec b$. The length $ab\sin\phi$ is the parallelogram's area, and the right-hand rule picks one of the two perpendicular directions.
>
>The **electric flux** measures how much electric field crosses a surface. For a flat surface in a uniform field $\vec E$, it is the [[3.3. Vector Multiplication|dot product]]
>$$\Phi_E = \vec E\cdot\vec A = EA\cos\theta.$$
>Here $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec E$ and $\vec A$. The unit of flux is the $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}$. The angle sets the sign and the size:
>- At $\theta = 0$ the field is perpendicular to the surface, and the flux is largest, $\Phi_E = EA$.
>- At $\theta = 90^\circ$ the field runs along the surface, and $\Phi_E = 0$.
>- For $\theta > 90^\circ$ the field crosses against $\vec A$, and $\Phi_E < 0$.
>
>A curved surface, or a field that changes from point to point, needs a sum. Cut the surface into small pieces. Each piece is nearly flat and has its own small area vector $d\vec A$. Adding the pieces gives a surface integral over the whole surface:
>$$\Phi_E = \int_{\text{surface}} \vec E\cdot d\vec A.$$
>The integral must name its surface, and surfaces come in two types:
>- An **open surface** has an edge, as a sheet of paper does. The direction of $d\vec A$ is a choice and must be stated.
>- A **closed surface** has no edge and separates an inside from an outside, as a balloon does. Its $d\vec A$ always points outward. Its flux is written with a circle, $\Phi_E = \oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A$. A positive value means a net outward flux.
>
>In the [[3.2.2. Electric Field Lines|field-line]] picture, the flux is proportional to the net number of lines crossing the surface. Lines crossing along $d\vec A$ count as positive, and lines crossing against it count as negative.

# Intuition

A lit incandescent bulb warms a hand held in front of it. The light carries energy away from the filament in a definite direction. A hand held to the side of the beam stays cool. A dark screen facing the bulb for a time $\Delta t$ measures the energy flux. The screen's temperature rises by $\Delta T$. With the screen's mass $m$ and specific heat $c$, it absorbed the energy $mc\,\Delta T$. Dividing by the time and by the screen's area $A$ gives
$$S = \frac{mc\,\Delta T}{A\,\Delta t}.$$
This holds when the screen's area vector points along the light and the screen loses no heat.

Suppose an assistant holds the screen edge-on to the light. No light crosses it, and it absorbs nothing. A screen tilted part of the way absorbs an amount in between. The orientation of the screen matters as well as its size. The dot product $\vec S\cdot\vec A$ records both. Only the component of $\vec S$ along $\vec A$ carries energy through the screen.

Nothing flows through a surface in an electric field. The flux of $\vec E$ still uses the same mathematics, with $\vec E$ in place of $\vec S$. It counts the field lines that cross the surface. [[3.3.2. Gauss's Law]] connects that count to the charge inside a closed surface.

The animation shows a flat patch in a uniform field, seen from the side. As the patch tilts, fewer lines cross it, and the flux follows $EA\cos\theta$. Past $90^\circ$ the lines cross against $\vec A$, and the flux turns negative.

# Properties

#### *(Shadow Area)* A tilted flat surface in a uniform field has the same flux as its shadow cast along the field.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not use projected areas. This view of $EA\cos\theta$ follows Giancoli chapter 22.

The product $EA\cos\theta$ can be grouped in two ways. Grouped as $(E\cos\theta)A$, it is the field component along $\vec A$ times the full area. Grouped as $E(A\cos\theta)$, it is the full field times a smaller area. That smaller area is the shadow the surface would cast in light shining along $\vec E$. Tilting a surface changes its shadow and its flux together. The shadow view also handles a curved surface in a uniform field. Each small piece has the flux of its own shadow. If every piece faces the field from the same side, the whole surface has the flux of its whole shadow.

#### *(Sign Needs a Direction)* A flux has a sign only after the direction of $d\vec A$ is fixed.

An open surface allows either of the two perpendicular directions for $d\vec A$. Reversing the choice reverses the sign of the flux and leaves its size unchanged. A problem about an open surface must therefore state the direction it uses. In some later problems, the physics fixes the choice with a right-hand rule. For a closed surface there is no choice. The surface separates space into an inside and an outside, so $d\vec A$ points outward at every piece. A positive flux then means that more field leaves the surface than enters it.

The animation shows a closed surface in a uniform field, seen from the side. Lines that enter cross against $d\vec A$ and count negative. Lines that leave count positive, and the two counts cancel.

#### *(Why Lines Measure Flux)* Counting field lines gives the flux because the line density is proportional to the field's size.

The [[3.2.2. Electric Field Lines|density of field lines]] is the number crossing a unit area perpendicular to them. The number through a small piece is then proportional to $E$ times its area perpendicular to the lines. That product is $\vec E\cdot d\vec A$. Adding the pieces turns the line count into the integral $\int \vec E\cdot d\vec A$. A tilted piece has fewer lines crossing it, in the same ratio $\cos\theta$ as its flux. Lines that enter a closed surface and leave again are treated in [[3.3.2. Gauss's Law]].

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Tilted Sheet
>A square sheet $20\ \text{cm}$ on a side sits in a uniform field of $2.0 \times 10^{3}\ \text{N/C}$. Its area vector makes an angle $\theta$ with the field.
>
>>[!example]- Find the flux for $\theta = 0$, $60^\circ$, $90^\circ$, and $120^\circ$.
>>The area is $A = (0.20)^2 = 0.040\ \text{m}^2$, so $EA = 80\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}$. The flux $\Phi_E = EA\cos\theta$ takes four values:
>>- At $\theta = 0$, $\Phi_E = 80\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}$.
>>- At $\theta = 60^\circ$, $\Phi_E = 80 \times 0.50 = 40\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}$.
>>- At $\theta = 90^\circ$, $\Phi_E = 0$, since the field runs along the sheet.
>>- At $\theta = 120^\circ$, $\Phi_E = 80 \times (-0.50) = -40\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}$.
>>
>>The last case is the $60^\circ$ case with the area vector reversed.

>[!example] (Medium) Measuring an Energy Flux
>A blackened copper plate, $10\ \text{cm}$ by $10\ \text{cm}$ with a mass of $30\ \text{g}$, faces a lamp. Copper has a specific heat of $385\ \text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K})$. In $60\ \text{s}$ the plate warms by $2.0\ \text{K}$. Ignore the heat the plate loses.
>
>>[!example]- Find the energy flux of the light. How long does the same warming take with the plate's area vector at $50^\circ$ to the light?
>>The plate absorbs $mc\,\Delta T = (0.030)(385)(2.0) = 23.1\ \text{J}$. Its area is $0.010\ \text{m}^2$, so the energy flux is
>>$$S = \frac{mc\,\Delta T}{A\,\Delta t} = \frac{23.1\ \text{J}}{(0.010\ \text{m}^2)(60\ \text{s})} = 38.5\ \text{W/m}^2.$$
>>Tilted at $50^\circ$, the plate absorbs the power $SA\cos 50^\circ$. That is $0.643$ of the power it absorbed when facing the lamp. The same $23.1\ \text{J}$ then takes
>>$$\Delta t' = \frac{60\ \text{s}}{\cos 50^\circ} = 93\ \text{s}.$$

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The next example uses the shadow view of flux, which lecture did not use.

>[!example] (Hard) Flux Through a Dome
>A hemispherical dome of radius $R = 15\ \text{cm}$ sits on a flat circular base. A uniform field of $1.5 \times 10^{3}\ \text{N/C}$ points straight up, along the dome's axis. On the dome, $d\vec A$ points away from the center of the base.
>
>>[!example]- Find the flux through the curved dome.
>>Every piece of the dome tilts upward or stands vertical, so no piece has a negative flux. By the shadow property, each piece has the flux of its shadow on the horizontal base. The shadows of all the pieces fill the base disk exactly once. The flux through the dome therefore equals the flux through a flat disk of radius $R$:
>>$$\Phi_E = E\,\pi R^2 = (1.5 \times 10^{3})\,\pi\,(0.15)^2 = 106\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}.$$
>>A vertical piece near the rim has a shadow of zero area, and its flux is zero too.
