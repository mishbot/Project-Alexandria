>[!note] Options
>An **option** is a financial instrument (traded both OTC and on exchanges) that gives the holder **the right, but not the obligation**, to buy or sell an underlying asset at a fixed predetermined price called the **strike price** (or **exercise price**) on or before a future maturity date, after which the option is invalidated:
>- A **call option** gives the holder the right to buy the underlier, whereas a **put option** gives the holder the right to sell the underlier.
>	- The short side must exercise the opposite action once the option is exercised.
>- **American options** can be executed before or on the maturity date, whereas **European options** (common form for non-US options) can only be executed on the maturity date.
>
>
>The price (or premium) of an option at time $t \geq 0$ can be decomposed into two components–its **intrinsic value** (payoff), and its **time value**–such that
>$$P_{t} = \text{IV}_{t} + \text{TV}_{t}.$$
>Here the intrinsic value (payoff) is the value the option would have if the TTM was zero:
>$$\text{IV}_{t}=\begin{cases}
\max(S_{t}-F_{0}, 0) & \text{call option}, \\
\max(F_{0}-S_{t}, 0) & \text{put option},
\end{cases}$$
>where $S_{t}$ is the spot at time $t$, and $F_{0}$ is the strike.
>
>Thus, if $P_{t} > \text{IV}_{t}$ (or equivalently, $\text{TV}_{t}>0$), then one should either sell the option or hold it rather than exercise it. Note that as the option approaches maturity (i.e., $t \to T$), $\text{TV}_{t} \to 0$ and $\text{IV}_{t} \to P_{t}$.
>
>A basic no-arbitrage restriction linking calls, puts, the stock, and the risk-free asset is **put–call parity**. For European options with the same strike $K$ and maturity $T$, the call–put spread must equal the stock price minus the present value of the strike:
>
>$$C_t-P_t = S_t - K e^{-r(T-t)}.$$
>
> This identity matters because it states that two portfolios with identical payoffs must have the same price. In particular, $C_t-P_t+K e^{-r(T-t)}$ replicates holding one share of stock, so any violation implies a law-of-one-price inconsistency (ignoring frictions).
> 
> The more volatile that the underlying asset is, the higher the probability that it will be in-the-money at expiration. Given the uncertainty of options pricing, models can be used to estimate option values–one of which is the [[2.2d.2i Black-Scholes Options Pricing|Black-Scholes equation]].
