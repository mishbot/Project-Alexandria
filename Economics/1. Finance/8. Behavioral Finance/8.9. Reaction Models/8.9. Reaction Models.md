>[!note] Reaction Models
>**Reaction models** are the belief-based behavioral asset-pricing models designed to explain — *jointly* — two empirical patterns that any complete theory of momentum must produce simultaneously: short-run continuation ($1$–$12$ month [[8.8. Momentum|momentum]] and post-earnings drift) and long-run reversal ($3$–$5$ year [[De Bondt & Thaler 1985|De Bondt-Thaler]] mean-reversion and the value premium). Two generations share this goal. The **first generation** (late 1990s) assembles the patterns from discrete cognitive biases — conservatism, representativeness, slow information diffusion. The **second generation** (2010s) derives them from a single extrapolation parameter in a full equilibrium model of the aggregate market.
>
>Three leading models define the family:
>- **[[8.9.1. Barberis-Shleifer-Vishny Model|Barberis, Shleifer & Vishny (1998)]]** — a single representative investor whose beliefs oscillate between a *mean-reverting* regime (capturing [[8.6.1. Conservatism|conservatism bias]]) and a *trending* regime (capturing [[8.6.5. Representativeness Heuristic|representativeness]]). When earnings shocks first arrive, the conservative regime dominates and the investor under-reacts; after a long string of same-direction shocks, the trending regime dominates and the investor over-reacts. Continuation followed by reversal emerges from a single agent confused about which earnings process is generating the data.
>- **[[8.9.2. Hong-Stein Model|Hong & Stein (1999)]]** — two boundedly-rational agent types interacting in the same market. *Newswatchers* trade on private fundamental information that diffuses slowly across the population, generating gradual under-reaction. *Momentum traders* condition only on past prices and chase trends with simple univariate strategies. Adding momentum traders mitigates under-reaction but creates eventual over-reaction, because their trades are partially misread as fundamental news by future generations. Continuation and reversal emerge from the *interaction* between heterogeneous agents.
>- **[[8.9.3. X-CAPM|Barberis, Greenwood, Jin & Shleifer (2015)]]** — a continuous-time equilibrium model of the aggregate stock market with a fraction $1-\mu$ of extrapolators (who forecast next-period returns as a recency-weighted average of past returns) and a fraction $\mu$ of rational traders with finite risk tolerance. A single extrapolation parameter simultaneously generates excess volatility, price-dividend-ratio predictability, aggregate bubbles, and time-series momentum-then-reversal — unifying anomalies the first-generation models needed separate stories for.
>
>**Decision rule.** Use BSV when the question is about a single representative investor's psychology and the target involves cross-sectional cognitive biases — conservatism and representativeness map directly onto its two regimes. Use Hong-Stein when the question is about heterogeneous-agent interaction, gradual information diffusion, or when the empirical evidence involves cross-sectional variation in information environments (analyst coverage, firm size, trading volume). Use X-CAPM when the target is the *aggregate* stock market and the empirical hook is survey-expectation data or the joint fit to excess volatility, $P/D$ predictability, and bubbles — X-CAPM is silent on the cross-section. The three are complementary rather than rival.
>
>Both models share three substantive commitments: (i) arbitrage by fully rational types is limited; (ii) the goal is to explain *deviations* from efficiency, not to prove markets are inefficient from scratch; (iii) success requires not just fitting momentum and reversal but generating *additional* testable predictions that can be falsified out of sample. Critics of behavioral finance argue that any single anomaly can be retrofitted with a story; both BSV and Hong-Stein answer this by predicting cross-sectional variation in momentum strength that subsequent empirical work has confirmed.

# Intuition

The puzzle is sharp: the same cross-section produces both short-run continuation and long-run reversal. A pure under-reaction story explains the drift but predicts monotonic convergence with no overshoot. A pure over-reaction story explains the reversal but predicts immediate overshoot with no drift window. A credible behavioral model has to produce *both* halves with one mechanism and one parameter set.

BSV and Hong-Stein are the first-generation answers, and they take opposite modeling routes. BSV puts a single investor in front of a random walk and gives her two competing mental models — a mean-reverting regime and a trending regime — that she switches between as evidence accumulates. Continuation and reversal both fall out of the trajectory of her posterior. Hong-Stein keeps each agent rational but limits *what they pay attention to*: newswatchers see fundamentals and ignore prices, momentum traders see prices and ignore fundamentals. Continuation comes from gradual diffusion of news across newswatchers; reversal comes from momentum traders piling in late and being unable to tell whether the recent drift is news or earlier momentum-trader flow.

X-CAPM, the second-generation answer, collapses both routes into one mechanism. Instead of assembling continuation and reversal from separate biases or separate agent types, it posits a single extrapolation parameter for one agent type and lets limited rational arbitrage keep the system from exploding. The same parameter that amplifies dividend shocks on impact also produces the slow mean-reversion that follows, so excess volatility, $P/D$ predictability, bubbles, and time-series momentum-then-reversal all come from the same place. The tradeoff is scope: X-CAPM targets the aggregate market only and says nothing about the cross-section that BSV and Hong-Stein were built to explain.

| Generation | First | First | Second |
| Modeling philosophy | Single representative investor with biased beliefs | Two heterogeneous agent types interacting | Two investor types in continuous-time equilibrium |
| Source of continuation | Conservatism: under-weights single news shocks | Slow diffusion of private info across newswatchers | Extrapolators chase past price changes |
| Source of reversal | Representativeness: over-extrapolates streaks | Momentum traders chase prices and overshoot | Rational-trader pull + sentiment decay |

| Empirical target | Cross-section of stocks | Cross-section of stocks | Aggregate stock market only |

| Unification | Separate biases for each anomaly | Separate mechanisms for each anomaly | One parameter generates all aggregate-market anomalies |

# When to Use Which

Reach for **BSV** when the empirical hook is *time-series* — how an investor's beliefs evolve as evidence streams in — or experimental evidence on how subjects react to streaks vs. single shocks (Bloomfield & Hales, $2001$). Reach for **Hong-Stein** when the hook is *cross-sectional* — momentum stronger in low-analyst-coverage stocks (Hong, Lim & Stein, $2000$) or correlated with trading volume (Lee & Swaminathan, $2000$). Reach for **X-CAPM** when the target is the *aggregate* market — excess volatility, $P/D$ predictability, aggregate bubbles, or survey-expectation data (Greenwood & Shleifer, $2014$). The three are complementary: most modern syntheses combine [[8.6.7. Extrapolative Beliefs|extrapolative beliefs]] (X-CAPM-flavored) with heterogeneous-agent dynamics (Hong-Stein-flavored).
