>[!note] Greeks
>The **Greeks** measure how the value of an option changes when one pricing input moves and the others stay fixed. Let $f(S, t)$ be the value at time $t$ of a derivative on a stock with price $S$. The stock has volatility $\sigma$ and continuous dividend yield $q$. The risk-free rate is $r$, and $\tau = T - t$ is the time to expiry. Each Greek is a partial derivative of $f$, and there are five main ones.
>- **Delta** $\Delta = \partial f/\partial S$ is the change in value per \$1 rise in the stock. It is also the number of shares that hedges one option ([[2.3.4a Delta]]).
>- **Gamma** $\Gamma = \partial^2 f/\partial S^2$ is the change in delta per \$1 rise in the stock. It measures the curvature of $f$ in $S$ ([[2.3.4b Gamma and Theta]]).
>- **Theta** $\Theta = \partial f/\partial t$ is the change in value per year of calendar time with $S$ held fixed. It measures time decay ([[2.3.4b Gamma and Theta]]).
>- **Vega** $\mathcal{V} = \partial f/\partial \sigma$ is the change in value per unit rise in volatility ([[2.3.4c Vega and Rho]]).
>- **Rho** $\rho = \partial f/\partial r$ is the change in value per unit rise in the interest rate ([[2.3.4c Vega and Rho]]).
>
>For European calls and puts, the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]] gives every Greek in closed form, listed in the table below. Every derivative price satisfies the [[2.3.3e Black-Scholes-Merton Equation|BSM equation]], so its Greeks obey one relation:
>$$\Theta + (r - q)S\Delta + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\Gamma = rf.$$
>Each term is in dollars per year. Now take a portfolio $\Pi = \sum_i w_i f_i$ of derivatives on the same stock, where $w_i$ is the number of units held (negative when short). If the portfolio is delta neutral, $\Delta_\Pi = 0$, the relation becomes
>$$\Theta_\Pi + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\Gamma_\Pi = r\Pi.$$
>As a rule of thumb, a large negative theta comes with a large positive gamma, and the reverse. Other sensitivities extend the list: the dividend sensitivity $\partial f/\partial q$, vanna, volga, and charm. At the end of each day, a trading desk reports dollar delta, dollar vega, dollar theta, and dollar rho. The Greeks extend the binomial hedge ratio of [[2.3.3d Delta Hedging]] to every input of the price.

>[!warning] Notation
>This note writes vega as $\mathcal{V}$, where the lecture writes the word "vega". The lecture writes $\Phi = \partial f/\partial q$ for the dividend sensitivity. That $\Phi$ is not the normal CDF, which these notes write $N$. The standard normal density is $n(x) = N'(x) = e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$.

# Intuition

A dealer who sells an option takes on risk from every input of its price. The stock moves, time passes, volatility changes, and interest rates shift. A Taylor expansion splits a small change in the option's value into one piece per input,
$$df \approx \Delta\,dS + \frac{1}{2}\Gamma\,(dS)^2 + \Theta\,dt + \mathcal{V}\,d\sigma + \rho\,dr.$$
Each Greek is the coefficient of one piece. Gamma enters through the square of the stock move, because $(dS)^2 = \sigma^2S^2\,dt$ for a stock that follows [[7.6 Geometric Brownian Motion|geometric Brownian motion]]. That makes the gamma term the same order of size as the theta term. The dealer can hedge each piece with its own trade. Shares offset the delta piece. Only other options can offset the gamma and vega pieces. [[2.3.5c Volatility Trading]] uses the same expansion to split the profit and loss of a hedged option.

# Formulas

The table lists the BSM Greeks of a European call and put with strike $K$ and dividend yield $q$. The numbers $d_1$ and $d_2$ are
$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r - q + \sigma^2/2)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau}.$$
Here $N$ is the standard normal CDF and $n$ is its density.

| Greek | European call | European put |
|---|---|---|
| $\Delta$ | $e^{-q\tau}N(d_1)$ | $-e^{-q\tau}N(-d_1)$ |
| $\Gamma$ | $\dfrac{e^{-q\tau}n(d_1)}{\sigma S\sqrt{\tau}}$ | same as the call |
| $\Theta$ | $-\dfrac{\sigma Se^{-q\tau}n(d_1)}{2\sqrt{\tau}} + qSe^{-q\tau}N(d_1) - rKe^{-r\tau}N(d_2)$ | $-\dfrac{\sigma Se^{-q\tau}n(d_1)}{2\sqrt{\tau}} - qSe^{-q\tau}N(-d_1) + rKe^{-r\tau}N(-d_2)$ |
| $\mathcal{V}$ | $S\sqrt{\tau}\,e^{-q\tau}n(d_1)$ | same as the call |
| $\rho$ | $K\tau e^{-r\tau}N(d_2)$ | $-K\tau e^{-r\tau}N(-d_2)$ |

Theta is measured per year, so one day of time decay is about $\Theta/365$. Vega and rho are per unit change in $\sigma$ or $r$. A rise of one percentage point changes the value by about $0.01\,\mathcal{V}$ or $0.01\,\rho$. A call and a put with the same strike and expiry share their gamma and vega. By [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity|put-call parity]], the call minus the put is a forward worth $Se^{-q\tau} - Ke^{-r\tau}$. That value is linear in $S$ and does not involve $\sigma$, so it adds nothing to gamma or vega.

>[!warning] Sign of the Rate Term in the Put's Theta
>The lecture notes print the last term of the put's theta as $-rKe^{-r\tau}N(-d_2)$. The sign should be plus. Differentiating parity, $p = c - Se^{-q\tau} + Ke^{-r\tau}$, in $t$ gives $\Theta_p = \Theta_c - qSe^{-q\tau} + rKe^{-r\tau}$, which yields the table entry. The lecture's own plots show a positive theta for deep in-the-money puts, which only the plus sign allows.

# Properties

#### *(Greeks Add Up)* The Greek of a portfolio is the sum of its positions' Greeks, each weighted by the position size.

Differentiation is linear. For $\Pi = \sum_i w_i f_i$, each Greek of the portfolio equals $\sum_i w_i$ times that Greek of instrument $i$, for example $\Delta_\Pi = \sum_i w_i \Delta_i$. A desk can therefore net the risks of thousands of trades into one number per Greek and per stock. A share has $\Delta = 1$, and all its other Greeks are zero. Trading shares sets the book's delta to any value but leaves its gamma, theta, vega, and rho unchanged. To change gamma or vega, the desk must trade options. Deltas on different stocks are counts of different shares, so they cannot be added. Desks convert them to dollars first.

#### *(Theta From the Others)* Given the price, delta, and gamma, the relation fixes theta with no extra computation.

Move the term $(r - q)S\Delta$ to the right side, and split $rf$ into $r(f - \Delta S) + r\Delta S$. The relation becomes
$$\Theta + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\Gamma = r(f - \Delta S) + q\Delta S.$$
The right side is the carry of the replicating portfolio of [[2.3.4a Delta]]. That portfolio holds $\Delta$ shares, which pay dividends $q\Delta S$ per year. It also holds cash $f - \Delta S$, which earns interest at $r$. For a call that cash is the negative amount $-Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2)$, a loan. The left side is the option's price drift from time passing plus its expected gain from curvature over an average move. So the price must grow at exactly the carry of its own hedge. Pricing code uses the relation as a test. The relation has no vega or rho term, because the model holds $\sigma$ and $r$ constant.

#### *(Theta Pays for Gamma)* In a delta-neutral book, one day of theta about equals the gamma gain from a typical one-day move.

When $r\Pi$ is small next to the other terms, the delta-neutral relation gives $\Theta_\Pi \approx -\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\Gamma_\Pi$. Multiply both sides by one day, $\Delta t = 1/365$:
$$\Theta_\Pi\,\Delta t \approx -\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_\Pi\big(\sigma S\sqrt{\Delta t}\big)^2.$$
The term $\sigma S\sqrt{\Delta t}$ is the standard deviation of the stock's one-day move under the model. A move of size $\delta S$ earns a hedged book about $\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_\Pi(\delta S)^2$. So a long-gamma book loses theta every day. It comes out ahead only when the stock moves more than about one standard deviation. A short-gamma book collects theta and loses on large moves. [[2.3.4b Gamma and Theta]] develops this trade-off. [[2.3.5c Volatility Trading]] restates it with realized volatility in place of $\sigma$.

#### *(Shares on a Dividend Stock)* Shares in a delta-neutral book add a dividend term to the relation.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture states the delta-neutral relation for a portfolio of derivatives. The correction for shares of a stock with a dividend yield is an extension.

The relation holds for every instrument that satisfies the BSM equation. A share of a stock with yield $q > 0$ does not. Its Greeks are $\Delta = 1$ and $\Theta = \Gamma = 0$, so the left side of the relation gives $(r - q)S$ in place of $rS$. The missing $qS$ is the dividend, which the share pays in cash instead of price growth. Consider a book of derivatives plus $m$ shares that is delta neutral. It satisfies
$$\Theta_\Pi + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\Gamma_\Pi = r\Pi - qmS.$$
Here $\Theta_\Pi$ counts price changes only, and $qmS$ is the dividend income per year on the shares. A desk that hedges short calls with long shares collects this income, so its prices need to grow less to earn $r\Pi$. With $q = 0$ the extra term vanishes.

#### *(Other Sensitivities)* Four more derivatives measure dividend risk and how delta and vega themselves move.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture defines these four sensitivities. The closed forms and their readings below are an extension.

Each closed form below follows from the BSM formula with dividend yield $q$.
- The **dividend sensitivity** $\Phi = \partial f/\partial q$ equals $-\tau Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1)$ for a call and $\tau Se^{-q\tau}N(-d_1)$ for a put. A higher yield lowers the expected stock price at expiry, so calls lose value and puts gain.
- **Vanna** is $\partial^2 f/\partial S\,\partial\sigma = \partial\Delta/\partial\sigma = \partial\mathcal{V}/\partial S$, and it equals $-e^{-q\tau}n(d_1)\,d_2/\sigma$ for calls and puts alike. A rise in volatility raises a call's delta when $d_2 < 0$ and lowers it when $d_2 > 0$.
- **Volga** is $\partial^2 f/\partial\sigma^2 = \partial\mathcal{V}/\partial\sigma$, and it equals $\mathcal{V}d_1d_2/\sigma$. It is near zero at the money and positive far from it, where $d_1$ and $d_2$ share a sign.
- **Charm** is $\partial^2 f/\partial S\,\partial t = \partial\Delta/\partial t = \partial\Theta/\partial S$, the drift of delta as time passes with $S$ fixed. For a call it equals $qe^{-q\tau}N(d_1) - e^{-q\tau}n(d_1)\big[2(r - q)\tau - d_2\sigma\sqrt{\tau}\big]/(2\tau\sigma\sqrt{\tau})$.

Vanna and charm tell a desk how its hedge drifts when volatility or the calendar moves and the stock does not. Volga tells it whether a large volatility move helps or hurts beyond what vega predicts.

#### *(Dollar Risks)* A desk reports Greeks in dollars so that risks on different stocks add up.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The lecture names dollar delta, vega, theta, and rho as the most watched end-of-day risks. The scaling conventions below are common desk practice and vary between firms.

Each dollar Greek is the profit or loss from a standard move in one input, summed over the book.
- **Dollar delta** is $\Delta S$ per option, the value of a stock position with the same exposure. A $1\%$ rise in the stock changes the book by about $1\%$ of the dollar delta.
- **Dollar vega** is $0.01\,\mathcal{V}$, the profit or loss from a one-point rise in volatility, for example from $25\%$ to $26\%$.
- **Dollar theta** is $\Theta/365$, the profit or loss from one calendar day passing. Some desks divide by $252$ trading days instead.
- **Dollar rho** is $0.0001\,\rho$, the profit or loss from a rise of one basis point in rates. Some desks use one percentage point.

A delta of $500$ shares means very different risks on a \$20 stock and on a \$400 stock. Their dollar deltas, \$10,000 and \$200,000, show the difference and can be added across stocks. Desks that trade complex products also track higher-order sensitivities, such as vanna, volga, and charm.

# Derivation

We want the relation among the Greeks and its delta-neutral form. Start from the BSM equation for a derivative on a stock with dividend yield $q$ ([[2.3.3e Black-Scholes-Merton Equation]]):
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + (r - q)S\frac{\partial f}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial S^2} - rf = 0.$$
The three partial derivatives are $\Theta$, $\Delta$, and $\Gamma$ by definition. Replacing them gives the relation in the callout.

Now take a portfolio $\Pi = \sum_i w_i f_i$ of derivatives on the same stock. Each $f_i$ satisfies the relation. Multiply the relation for $f_i$ by $w_i$ and add over $i$. Each Greek adds up with the same weights, so
$$\Theta_\Pi + (r - q)S\Delta_\Pi + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\Gamma_\Pi = r\Pi.$$
This is the relation for the whole portfolio. A delta-neutral portfolio has $\Delta_\Pi = 0$, which removes the middle term and leaves $\Theta_\Pi + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\Gamma_\Pi = r\Pi$.

As a check, substitute the call's formulas from the table. The gamma term is $\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2 \cdot e^{-q\tau}n(d_1)/(\sigma S\sqrt{\tau}) = \sigma Se^{-q\tau}n(d_1)/(2\sqrt{\tau})$. It cancels the first term of theta exactly. The remaining terms are
$$qSe^{-q\tau}N(d_1) - rKe^{-r\tau}N(d_2) + (r - q)Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1) = r\big[Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1) - Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2)\big] = rc.$$
The bracket is the call price, so the table's formulas satisfy the relation.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A One-Day Forecast From the Greeks
>A stock trades at \$58 and has a dividend yield of $1.5\%$ per year. A European call on it has strike \$60 and expires in $0.4$ years. The volatility is $26\%$ and the risk-free rate is $4.5\%$, both with continuous compounding. The call is worth \$3.22, with $\Delta = 0.4768$, $\Gamma = 0.0415$, $\Theta = -5.41$ per year, $\mathcal{V} = 14.53$, and $\rho = 9.78$. Over the next day the stock rises to \$59.20 and the volatility rises to $27\%$. The rate does not change.
>
>>[!example]- Estimate the call's new value from the Greeks, and compare the estimate with an exact repricing.
>>Use the Taylor expansion with $dS = 1.20$, $dt = 1/365$, and $d\sigma = 0.01$. The four pieces are
>>$$\Delta\,dS = 0.5722, \qquad \frac{1}{2}\Gamma(dS)^2 = 0.0299, \qquad \Theta\,dt = -0.0148, \qquad \mathcal{V}\,d\sigma = 0.1453.$$
>>They sum to about \$0.733, so the estimate is $3.22 + 0.73 = \$3.95$. Repricing with the formula at $S = 59.20$, $\tau = 0.4 - 1/365$, and $\sigma = 27\%$ gives \$3.9493, a change of \$0.7341. The estimate misses by about \$0.002. Without the gamma piece it would miss by \$0.03. Delta accounts for most of the change, and vega adds about a fifth. One day of theta costs only $1.5$ cents.

>[!example] (Medium) Put Greeks With Dividends
>A stock trades at \$82 and has a dividend yield of $2.5\%$ per year. A European put on it has strike \$85 and expires in nine months. The volatility is $22\%$ and the risk-free rate is $3.5\%$, both with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the put's price and its five Greeks, then check the relation among Greeks.
>>Here $\tau = 0.75$ and $\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = 0.19053$. The numerator of $d_1$ is $\ln(82/85) + (0.035 - 0.025 + 0.0242)(0.75) = -0.03593 + 0.02565 = -0.01028$. So $d_1 = -0.0540$ and $d_2 = -0.2445$. The normal CDF and density give $N(-d_1) = 0.521519$, $N(-d_2) = 0.596575$, and $n(d_1) = 0.398362$. The discounted prices are $Se^{-q\tau} = 80.4768$ and $Ke^{-r\tau} = 82.7978$. The put price is
>>$$p = -80.4768 \times 0.521519 + 82.7978 \times 0.596575 = -41.9702 + 49.3951 = \$7.4249.$$
>>The Greeks follow from the table:
>>- $\Delta = -0.981425 \times 0.521519 = -0.5118$.
>>- $\Gamma = 0.981425 \times 0.398362/(0.22 \times 82 \times 0.866025) = 0.02502$.
>>- $\Theta = -4.07203 - 1.04926 + 1.72883 = -3.39246$ per year.
>>- $\mathcal{V} = 82 \times 0.866025 \times 0.981425 \times 0.398362 = 27.76$.
>>- $\rho = -85 \times 0.75 \times 0.974092 \times 0.596575 = -37.05$.
>>
>>The three terms of theta are the volatility term, the dividend term, and the interest term. For the relation, $(r - q)S\Delta = 0.01 \times 82 \times (-0.5118) = -0.41970$ and $\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2\Gamma = 4.07203$. The left side is $-3.39246 - 0.41970 + 4.07203 = 0.25987$. The right side is $rp = 0.035 \times 7.4249 = 0.25987$. The two sides agree. The gamma term equals the volatility term of theta with the opposite sign. What remains is carry, $-1.04926 + 1.72883 - 0.41970 = 0.25987$. A rise of one point in volatility adds about \$0.28 to the put. A rise of one point in $r$ takes away about \$0.37.
