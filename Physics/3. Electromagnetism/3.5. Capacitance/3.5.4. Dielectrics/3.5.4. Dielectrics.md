>[!note] Dielectrics
>A **dielectric** is an insulator placed between the plates of a capacitor. The field between the plates pushes each atom's electron cloud one way and its nucleus the other. Each atom becomes a small **induced dipole**, as in [[3.1.6. Induced Charge]]. Inside the material, each dipole's positive end sits next to its neighbor's negative end. Their charges cancel. At the two faces nothing cancels. A thin layer of negative charge forms on the face next to the positive plate. A layer of positive charge forms on the face next to the negative plate. These **induced surface charges** make a field opposite to the plates' field. For the same charge on the plates, the field in the dielectric is smaller by a factor $K$:
>$$E = \frac{E_0}{K},$$
>where $E_0$ is the field with vacuum between the plates. The number $K$ is the **dielectric constant** of the material. It has no units, and $K \geq 1$ for ordinary materials. A parallel-plate capacitor filled with a dielectric has
>$$C = \frac{K\varepsilon_0 A}{d} = KC_0,$$
>with $C_0 = \varepsilon_0 A/d$ the capacitance with vacuum. Some texts write $\varepsilon = K\varepsilon_0$ and call it the **permittivity** of the material. Typical values of $K$:
>
>| Material | $K$ |
>|---|---|
>| Vacuum | $1$ |
>| Air (1 atm) | $1.0006$ |
>| Typical polymers | $2$ to $3$ |
>| Paper | about $3.7$ |
>| Mica | about $7$ |
>| Water | about $80$ |
>
>The effect of inserting a dielectric depends on what stays fixed:
>- **Fixed charge**, with the supply disconnected. $Q$ stays the same and $C$ rises to $KC_0$. The potential difference and the field fall to $V_0/K$ and $E_0/K$. The stored energy $U = Q^2/(2C)$ falls to $U_0/K$.
>- **Fixed potential difference**, with the supply connected. The field $E = V/d$ stays the same. The supply adds charge until $Q = KQ_0$. The stored energy $U = \tfrac12 CV^2$ rises to $KU_0$.
>
>The energy changes follow the opposite trends described in [[3.5.3. Energy Stored in an Electric Field]].
>
>A different property, the **dielectric strength**, is the largest field a material can hold before it sparks. [[3.5.4a Dielectric Breakdown]] covers it.

# Intuition

Each atom in the slab becomes an induced dipole, as described in [[3.1.6. Induced Charge]]. A helium atom is the simplest case, since its electron cloud starts as a sphere around the nucleus. Between the two ends of each dipole, the dipole's own field points opposite to the applied field.

Fill the gap between two plates with such atoms. Inside the slab, each dipole's positive end touches its neighbor's negative end, so the interior stays neutral. Only the two faces carry net charge. Those face layers form a second, weaker pair of charged sheets with the opposite signs. Their field cancels part of the plates' field.

A weaker field means a smaller potential difference for the same charge. The capacitance $Q/V$ therefore rises. A dielectric raises $C$ without making the gap smaller. Shrinking the gap has a limit, set by sparking in [[3.5.4a Dielectric Breakdown]].

An insulator fills the gap between plates with fixed charge. Its atoms become induced dipoles, and charge layers form on its faces. Fewer field lines cross the slab, so $E = E_0/K$.

# Derivation

Start with a parallel-plate capacitor holding $\pm Q$. With vacuum between the plates, the field is $E_0 = Q/(\varepsilon_0 A)$, from [[3.5.1a Parallel-Plate Capacitor]]. Slide in a slab that fills the gap. Its induced dipoles add an induced field $\vec E_{\text{ind}}$ inside the slab, pointing opposite to $\vec E_0$. Without $E_0$ the material would not polarize, and a stronger field stretches the atoms more. The induced field is therefore proportional to $E_0$. Write its size as $fE_0$, with $f$ a positive number that depends on the material. By superposition, the field in the slab has size
$$E = E_0 - fE_0 = (1 - f)E_0.$$
The factor $1 - f$ lies between $0$ and $1$, since the induced field never exceeds the applied one. Writing it as $1/K$, with $K > 1$, gives $E = E_0/K$.

The potential difference across the gap is then
$$V = Ed = \frac{E_0 d}{K} = \frac{Qd}{K\varepsilon_0 A}.$$
Dividing $Q$ by $V$ gives $C = K\varepsilon_0 A/d$.

# Properties

#### *(The Electroscope Reads Voltage)* In a classroom demonstration, cardboard in the gap lowered the electroscope leaves, because the potential difference fell at fixed charge.

An air-gap capacitor was charged and disconnected, with an electroscope wired to one plate. Sliding a sheet of cardboard between the plates made the leaves fall. Pulling it out made them rise again. The plates were isolated, so their charge could not change. The capacitance rose, and the potential difference fell to $V_0/K$. The leaves respond to the potential of the conductor they touch, so they fell with it.

#### *(Induced Charge Is Smaller)* The induced surface charge is always smaller than the plate charge, so the field keeps its direction.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not compute the induced charge. Giancoli section 24.6 derives it.

Let the plates carry surface charge $\pm\sigma$, with $\sigma = Q/A$, and let the slab's faces carry $\mp\sigma_i$. The induced layers form a pair of oppositely charged sheets. Their field $\sigma_i/\varepsilon_0$ points opposite to the plates' field $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$. Setting the total $(\sigma - \sigma_i)/\varepsilon_0$ equal to $\sigma/(K\varepsilon_0)$ gives
$$\sigma_i = \sigma\left(1 - \frac{1}{K}\right).$$
For paper, with $K = 3.7$, the induced charge is $73\%$ of the plate charge. Air, with $K = 1.0006$, induces only $0.06\%$. As $K$ grows without limit, $\sigma_i$ approaches $\sigma$ and the field approaches zero. That limit describes a metal, whose free electrons cancel the field inside completely.

#### *(Why $K$ Varies)* The dielectric constant depends on how many molecules fill each cubic meter and on how easily each one polarizes.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not explain water's large $K$. Giancoli section 24.6 describes permanent dipoles in a dielectric.

A gas at atmospheric pressure has about a thousand times fewer molecules per cubic meter than a solid. Its induced dipoles are few, so air has $K = 1.0006$. Solids and liquids pack their molecules closely and have $K$ of a few. Water reaches about $80$ for a different reason. Its molecules are permanent dipoles, as described in [[3.2.6. Electric Dipole]]. The field turns them toward alignment, which gives far more surface charge than stretching atoms does.

#### *(The Field Pulls a Dielectric In)* A charged capacitor draws a dielectric into the space between its plates.

In a classroom demonstration, two closely spaced electrodes stood in a dish of glycerin, an insulating liquid. With the supply on, the glycerin rose between the electrodes, against gravity. With the supply off, it fell back. The demonstration kept its supply connected. For an isolated capacitor, the same move lowers $U$ from $Q^2/(2C_0)$ toward $Q^2/(2KC_0)$. With the supply connected, $U$ rises and the supply provides the energy. [[3.5.4b Force on a Dielectric]] treats both cases.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Mica Capacitor
>A capacitor has plates of area $2.0\ \text{cm}^2$ separated by a mica sheet $0.050\ \text{mm}$ thick, with $K = 7$.
>
>>[!example]- Find its capacitance, and compare it with the same plates in air.
>>The area is $A = 2.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. The capacitance is
>>$$C = \frac{K\varepsilon_0 A}{d} = \frac{(7)(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(2.0 \times 10^{-4})}{0.050 \times 10^{-3}} = 2.48 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{F} = 248\ \text{pF}.$$
>>In air the same plates give $C_0 = C/K = 35.4\ \text{pF}$.

>[!example] (Medium) Fixed Charge Versus Fixed Voltage
>A $50\ \text{pF}$ air capacitor is charged to $150\ \text{V}$. A paper sheet with $K = 3.7$ is then slid in to fill the gap.
>
>>[!example]- Find the capacitance, charge, and potential difference afterward, first with the supply disconnected and then with it connected.
>>The starting charge is $Q_0 = C_0V_0 = (50 \times 10^{-12})(150) = 7.5 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{C}$. In both cases the capacitance becomes $C = KC_0 = 185\ \text{pF}$.
>>
>>With the supply disconnected, the charge stays $7.5\ \text{nC}$. The potential difference falls to
>>$$V = \frac{Q_0}{C} = \frac{7.5 \times 10^{-9}}{185 \times 10^{-12}} = 40.5\ \text{V}.$$
>>The field falls by the same factor of $3.7$.
>>
>>With the supply connected, the potential difference stays $150\ \text{V}$. The charge rises to $Q = CV = (185 \times 10^{-12})(150) = 2.78 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{C}$. The supply moved another $20.3\ \text{nC}$ onto the plates, and the field is unchanged.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not treat a slab that fills only part of the gap's thickness. Giancoli chapter 24 treats a similar case.

>[!example] (Hard) A Slab That Fills Part of the Gap
>A parallel-plate capacitor has plates of area $0.010\ \text{m}^2$ separated by $2.0\ \text{mm}$, connected to a $200\ \text{V}$ supply. A polymer slab with $K = 2.5$ and thickness $1.5\ \text{mm}$ lies flat on the lower plate. The remaining $0.50\ \text{mm}$ is air.
>
>>[!example]- Find the capacitance and the field in the air and in the slab.
>>Use the recipe with $\pm Q$ on the plates. The air layer has the field $E_{\text{air}} = Q/(\varepsilon_0 A)$. The slab has $E_{\text{slab}} = E_{\text{air}}/K$. With $t$ the slab thickness, integrating across both layers gives
>>$$V = E_{\text{air}}(d - t) + \frac{E_{\text{air}}}{K}\,t = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A}\left(d - t + \frac{t}{K}\right).$$
>>So the capacitance is
>>$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d - t + t/K}.$$
>>Here $d - t + t/K = 0.50\ \text{mm} + 1.5\ \text{mm}/2.5 = 1.10\ \text{mm}$. Then
>>$$C = \frac{(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(0.010)}{1.10 \times 10^{-3}} = 8.05 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{F} = 80.5\ \text{pF}.$$
>>The empty capacitor has $44.3\ \text{pF}$, and a full slab would give $111\ \text{pF}$. The fields are $E_{\text{air}} = V/(1.10\ \text{mm}) = 1.82 \times 10^{5}\ \text{V/m}$ and $E_{\text{slab}} = 7.27 \times 10^{4}\ \text{V/m}$. As a check, $(1.82 \times 10^{5})(0.50 \times 10^{-3}) + (7.27 \times 10^{4})(1.5 \times 10^{-3}) = 91 + 109 = 200\ \text{V}$. A slab of thickness $t$ acts like an air layer of thickness $t/K$.
