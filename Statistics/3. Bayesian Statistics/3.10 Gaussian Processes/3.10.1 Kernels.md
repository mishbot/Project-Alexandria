>[!note] Kernels for Gaussian Processes
>A **kernel** is a symmetric positive semi-definite function $K : \Omega \times \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ that specifies the covariance structure of a Gaussian process: $K(x, x') = \text{Cov}(f(x), f(x'))$. *Positive semi-definite* means that for any finite collection of points $u_1, \ldots, u_N \in \Omega$, the Gram matrix $\mathbf{K} = (K(u_i, u_j))_{i,j}$ is positive semi-definite. This is the only restriction: every valid kernel defines a valid GP.
>
>The kernel encodes every prior belief about the function — smoothness, stationarity, length-scale, periodicity, boundary conditions. Canonical kernels on $\Omega = [0, \infty)$:
>
>- **Brownian motion**: $K(s,t) = \min(s,t)$. Samples are continuous but nowhere differentiable; $f(0) = 0$.
>- **Scaled BM plus constant**: $K(s,t) = C + \tau^2 \min(s,t)$. Adds an $\mathcal{N}(0,C)$ intercept so $f(0) \neq 0$; with $C \to \infty$ the intercept is flat.
>- **Integrated Brownian Motion (IBM)**: $K(s,t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\max(s,t)\min(s,t)^2 - \tfrac{1}{6}\min(s,t)^3$. Samples are once-differentiable; $f(0) = f'(0) = 0$.
>- **Scaled IBM plus linear term**: $K(s,t) = C(1 + st) + \tau^2\!\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\max(s,t)\min(s,t)^2 - \tfrac{1}{6}\min(s,t)^3\right]$. Adds $\mathcal{N}(0, C)$ priors on intercept and slope; used in [[3.10.2 GP Regression]] because its posterior mean is a natural cubic spline.
>
>Canonical kernels on $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$:
>
>- **Linear**: $K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{x}'$. GP is the Bayesian linear-regression function.
>- **Squared-exponential (RBF)**: $K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \sigma_f^2 \exp\!\left(-\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\|^2 / (2\ell^2)\right)$. Samples are infinitely differentiable; $\ell$ is the length-scale.
>- **Matérn**: smoothness-tunable generalization of RBF. At half-integer smoothness parameters, has closed-form simple expressions.
>
>Valid kernels form a cone under three operations: sums $K_1 + K_2$, products $K_1 \cdot K_2$, and scalar multiples $\alpha K$ (for $\alpha \geq 0$). This *kernel algebra* allows building complex priors from simple parts.

# Intuition

A kernel $K(x, x')$ answers one question: *if $f(x)$ takes a value above its mean, how confident am I that $f(x')$ does too?* Large $K(x, x')$ means the function values are tightly correlated; small $K(x, x')$ means they are nearly independent. The kernel shape translates prior knowledge about the function into correlations between function values at pairs of inputs.

Three axes govern kernel design. *Smoothness* determines how many times sample paths can be differentiated: Brownian samples are continuous but not differentiable, IBM samples are once differentiable, RBF samples are $C^\infty$. *Stationarity* determines whether $K(x, x')$ depends only on the separation $x - x'$ or on the absolute positions; stationary kernels (RBF, Matérn) treat the domain homogeneously, non-stationary kernels (Brownian, IBM) let the variance grow away from the origin. *Boundary conditions* fall out of the kernel: Brownian motion pins $f(0) = 0$, IBM pins both $f(0) = 0$ and $f'(0) = 0$, and adding a linear term with flat priors on the coefficients releases these constraints.

The positive semi-definiteness requirement is not a technicality — it is the statement that *every* finite-dimensional marginal of the induced GP is a proper Gaussian. Violate it and the implied "distribution" assigns negative variance to some linear combination of function values, which is nonsense.

# Properties

#### *(Kernel Algebra)* Sums, products, and nonneg. scalar multiples of valid kernels are valid kernels.

If $K_1$ and $K_2$ are positive semi-definite, so are $K_1 + K_2$, $K_1 \cdot K_2$, and $\alpha K_1$ for $\alpha \geq 0$. This is the operational fact that lets one build elaborate kernels from simple parts. Summing kernels yields *additive* GPs — $f = f_1 + f_2$ with $f_j \sim \text{GP}(0, K_j)$ independent. Multiplying kernels combines effects, for example trend times periodic component. The scaled-IBM-plus-linear-term kernel $K(s,t) = C(1 + st) + \tau^2 K_I(s,t)$ is literally the sum of the "constant $+$ linear" kernel $C(1 + st)$ and the IBM kernel, reflecting the decomposition $f(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \tau I(x)$ with independent components.

#### *(Brownian Motion Kernel)* Continuous non-differentiable samples; variance grows linearly.

The kernel $K(s, t) = \min(s, t)$ is the covariance of standard Brownian motion $W_t$ on $[0, \infty)$. It enforces $f(0) = 0$ almost surely (since $K(0,0) = 0$) and gives $\text{Var}(f(t)) = t$, so the variance grows linearly with $t$. Positive semi-definiteness follows directly from the independent-increments representation: any linear combination $\sum a_i f(t_i)$ rewrites as a linear combination of independent Gaussian increments. Sample paths are continuous but nowhere differentiable — a consequence of the square-root scaling of increments, $W_{t+h} - W_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, h)$, which has magnitude $\sqrt{h}$ rather than $h$. This makes Brownian motion a very rough prior, appropriate when the function is expected to fluctuate on every scale. See [[3.10.3 Integrated Brownian Motion]] for a smoother alternative.

#### *(Integrated Brownian Motion Kernel)* Once-differentiable samples; cubic-spline posterior mean.

The IBM kernel is
$$
K_I(s, t) = \tfrac{1}{2} \max(s, t) \min(s, t)^2 - \tfrac{1}{6} \min(s, t)^3 = \tfrac{1}{6} \min(s,t)^2 \bigl(3\max(s,t) - \min(s,t)\bigr).
$$
It is obtained by integrating Brownian motion once: $I(t) = \int_0^t W_s\,ds$. Since the integral of a continuous path is differentiable, IBM samples are $C^1$. The kernel pins $f(0) = 0$ and $f'(0) = 0$, so in practice one uses the scaled-IBM-plus-linear-term kernel to release these constraints. The headline property: with the scaled-IBM-plus-linear-term kernel, the posterior mean for the [[3.10.2 GP Regression|GP regression]] problem is the *natural cubic spline* interpolant — the same smoother produced by penalized-splines methods in frequentist nonparametric regression. Full details in [[3.10.3 Integrated Brownian Motion]].

#### *(Squared-Exponential / RBF Kernel)* Infinitely smooth stationary default.

The squared-exponential kernel
$$
K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \sigma_f^2 \exp\!\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\|^2}{2 \ell^2}\right)
$$
depends only on the Euclidean distance $\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\|$, so it is stationary and isotropic. Samples are infinitely differentiable — in fact analytic — which makes RBF the default kernel for smooth regression problems. The length-scale $\ell$ controls how rapidly correlations decay: small $\ell$ gives wiggly samples, large $\ell$ gives nearly-constant samples. The signal scale $\sigma_f^2$ controls the overall variance. In high dimensions the RBF is often equipped with *automatic relevance determination* (ARD) weights, giving a separate length-scale per dimension: $K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \sigma_f^2 \exp(-\sum_d (x_d - x_d')^2 / (2\ell_d^2))$, which shrinks irrelevant dimensions to zero influence.

#### *(Linear Kernel)* GP with a linear kernel is Bayesian linear regression.

For $K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{x}'$, the induced GP is $f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^\top \boldsymbol\beta$ with $\boldsymbol\beta \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$. The covariance reproduces: $\text{Cov}(\mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta, {\mathbf{x}'}^\top\boldsymbol\beta) = \mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{x}'$. This collapses GP regression to [[3.8.1 Bayesian Linear Regression]] with a standard-normal prior. The linear kernel is degenerate — only $d$-dimensional, not infinite-dimensional — so its Gram matrices have rank at most $d$. This observation generalizes: a kernel $K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x})^\top \boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}')$ for any feature map $\boldsymbol\phi$ is a linear kernel in $\boldsymbol\phi$-space, and the GP is $f(\mathbf{x}) = \boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x})^\top \boldsymbol\beta$.

#### *(Non-Stationarity of BM/IBM Kernels)* Variance depends on position, not just separation.

Brownian and Integrated Brownian kernels have $K(s, s) = s$ and $K(s,s) = s^3/3$ respectively — the variance grows with position, so the prior is *non-stationary*. This is usually undesirable when the domain is a bounded interval with no special "origin," but it is exactly what is wanted when $x = 0$ is a meaningful initial time and the function accumulates uncertainty as $x$ grows. For Bayesian nonparametric regression on a generic interval, shift-invariance is usually preferred and the RBF or Matérn kernel is the default choice. Non-stationary kernels shine in time-series settings where the origin is a real anchor.

#### *(Mercer Decomposition)* Every kernel has a spectral eigenfunction decomposition.

By Mercer's theorem, a continuous positive semi-definite kernel on a compact domain admits
$$
K(x, x') = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \lambda_k \phi_k(x) \phi_k(x'),
$$
where $\{\phi_k\}$ is an orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions and $\lambda_k \geq 0$. A GP with this kernel can be written as $f(x) = \sum_k \sqrt{\lambda_k}\, Z_k \phi_k(x)$ with $Z_k \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. This connects GPs to Bayesian linear regression in an infinite-dimensional feature space and underlies the theoretical analysis of GP regression (effective degrees of freedom, rates of convergence). For the Brownian motion kernel on $[0, 1]$, the eigenfunctions are sines and the eigenvalues decay as $1/k^2$. For the IBM kernel, eigenvalues decay as $1/k^4$ — faster decay reflects smoother samples.

#### *(Hyperparameter Estimation)* Kernel parameters are learned via marginal likelihood.

Kernels typically depend on free hyperparameters: length-scale $\ell$, signal variance $\sigma_f^2$, ratio $\gamma = \tau/\sigma$. These are estimated by maximizing (or placing priors on) the *marginal likelihood* $p(\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol\theta) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}; \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{K}_{\boldsymbol\theta} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})$. On a log scale, the marginal likelihood is
$$
\log p(\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol\theta) = -\tfrac{1}{2} \mathbf{y}^\top (\mathbf{K}_{\boldsymbol\theta} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{y} - \tfrac{1}{2} \log |\mathbf{K}_{\boldsymbol\theta} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}| - \tfrac{n}{2} \log 2\pi,
$$
which has a built-in Occam penalty: the log-determinant term shrinks with overly flexible kernels. See [[3.7 Marginal Likelihood]] for why this counts as automatic model selection.

# Examples

>[!example]- (Easy) Verify positive semi-definiteness of the Brownian motion kernel.
>Show that $K(s, t) = \min(s, t)$ is positive semi-definite on $[0, \infty)$.
>
>**Solution.** Take any $t_1 < \cdots < t_N$ and coefficients $a_1, \ldots, a_N$. Set $\alpha_i = W_{t_i} - W_{t_{i-1}}$ (with $t_0 = 0$), so $W_{t_i} = \alpha_1 + \cdots + \alpha_i$ and the $\alpha_j$ are independent with $\alpha_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t_j - t_{j-1})$. Then
>$$
>\sum_{i,j} a_i a_j K(t_i, t_j) = \text{Var}\!\left(\sum_i a_i W_{t_i}\right) = \text{Var}\!\left(\sum_i a_i \sum_{j \leq i} \alpha_j\right) = \sum_j (t_j - t_{j-1}) \Bigl(\sum_{i \geq j} a_i\Bigr)^2 \geq 0.
>$$
>The equality on the right uses independence of the $\alpha_j$. This confirms $K$ is positive semi-definite.

>[!example]- (Medium) Build a kernel with a periodic and trend component.
>Suppose $f : [0, 10] \to \mathbb{R}$ is believed to consist of a smooth long-term trend plus a periodic oscillation of period roughly 1. Construct a kernel.
>
>**Solution.** Use the sum $K = K_{\text{trend}} + K_{\text{periodic}}$ with
>$$
>K_{\text{trend}}(s, t) = \sigma_1^2 \exp(-(s - t)^2 / (2 \ell_1^2)) \quad \text{(RBF with large length-scale $\ell_1 \approx 5$)}
>$$
>$$
>K_{\text{periodic}}(s, t) = \sigma_2^2 \exp\!\left(-\frac{2 \sin^2(\pi(s - t))}{\ell_2^2}\right) \quad \text{(periodic kernel with period 1)}.
>$$
>The trend component captures slow variation; the periodic component captures the one-unit cycle. Because valid kernels are closed under sums, $K$ is positive semi-definite. Fitting to data requires learning $\sigma_1^2, \ell_1, \sigma_2^2, \ell_2$ via marginal likelihood — see [[3.10.2 GP Regression]].

>[!example]- (Hard) GP with RBF kernel on one point recovers the Normal-Normal model.
>Show that if $n = 1$, observing $y_1 = f(x_1) + \epsilon$ under a zero-mean GP with RBF kernel recovers the univariate [[3.5.2 Normal-Normal Model]] for $f(x_1)$.
>
>**Solution.** With $n = 1$, the Gram matrix is the scalar $\mathbf{K} = K(x_1, x_1) = \sigma_f^2$. The prior on $f(x_1)$ is $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_f^2)$. Observing $y_1 \mid f(x_1) \sim \mathcal{N}(f(x_1), \sigma^2)$, the posterior is
>$$
>f(x_1) \mid y_1 \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\frac{\sigma_f^2}{\sigma_f^2 + \sigma^2} y_1,\; \frac{\sigma_f^2 \sigma^2}{\sigma_f^2 + \sigma^2}\right).
>$$
>This is exactly the Normal-Normal posterior for unknown mean with $\mu_0 = 0$, $\tau_0^2 = \sigma_f^2$, noise $\sigma^2$. The posterior mean shrinks $y_1$ toward the prior mean (zero) with weight $\sigma^2 / (\sigma_f^2 + \sigma^2)$. At a new test point $x \neq x_1$, the prediction is $\mathbf{k}^\top (\mathbf{K} + \sigma^2)^{-1} y_1 = (K(x, x_1) / (\sigma_f^2 + \sigma^2)) y_1$, which interpolates between zero (far from $x_1$) and the shrunken observation (near $x_1$).
