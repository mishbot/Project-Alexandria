>[!note] Latent Heat
>A substance that melts or boils absorbs heat without any change in temperature. The heat needed to change the phase of a mass $m$ is
>$$Q = m L,$$
>where $L$ is the **latent heat** of the substance for that transition, in joules per kilogram. The **heat of fusion** $L_F$ applies to melting and freezing. The **heat of vaporization** $L_V$ applies to boiling and condensing. Heat enters during melting and boiling, so $Q = +mL$. The same heat leaves during freezing and condensing, so $Q = -mL$. The mass $m$ is the amount that changes phase, which may be only part of the sample.
>
>For water at atmospheric pressure,
>$$L_F = 333\ \text{kJ/kg} \ (\text{at } 0\ ^\circ\text{C}), \qquad L_V = 2260\ \text{kJ/kg} \ (\text{at } 100\ ^\circ\text{C}).$$
>Boiling water takes nearly seven times the heat that melting the same mass of ice takes. Values for other substances follow.
>
>| Substance | Melting point $(^\circ\text{C})$ | $L_F$ $(\text{kJ/kg})$ | Boiling point $(^\circ\text{C})$ | $L_V$ $(\text{kJ/kg})$ |
>|---|---|---|---|---|
>| Oxygen | $-219$ | $14$ | $-183$ | $210$ |
>| Nitrogen | $-210$ | $26$ | $-196$ | $200$ |
>| Ethyl alcohol | $-114$ | $104$ | $78$ | $850$ |
>| Water | $0$ | $333$ | $100$ | $2260$ |
>| Lead | $327$ | $25$ | $1750$ | $870$ |
>| Silver | $961$ | $88$ | $2193$ | $2300$ |
>| Iron | $1808$ | $289$ | $3023$ | $6340$ |
>
>While two phases coexist in equilibrium, the temperature is fixed at the transition temperature. A bath of ice and water is at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ as long as both are present, and boiling water with steam above it is at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$. A problem that crosses a phase change is solved in stages: $Q = mc\,\Delta T$ within each phase, with the [[2.4.3 Specific Heat|specific heat]] of that phase, and $Q = mL$ at each transition. Evaporation also happens below the boiling point, from the surface, and carries away about $2450\ \text{kJ/kg}$ at room temperature.

# Intuition

In a solid, each atom sits in a well made by the forces from its neighbors, and heating makes it vibrate harder. Push the temperature high enough and the vibrations become large enough to tear atoms loose from their positions. From then on, added heat does not raise the temperature. It goes into breaking the bonds that hold the structure together, one region at a time. The temperature measures average kinetic energy per particle, and that stays fixed while the potential energy of the particles rises. Only when the last of the solid has melted does the temperature start climbing again. Boiling is the same story at a larger scale. The molecules of the liquid are still touching, and turning them into a gas means pulling every one of them away from all of its neighbors. That is why $L_V$ is so much larger than $L_F$. The sketch draws the bonds as springs.

The diagram is the **heating curve** of water. Heat goes in at a steady rate along the horizontal axis. Where the curve slopes, the substance is in one phase and the slope is $1/mc$. Where the curve is flat, two phases coexist and heat goes into the transition. The flat segments are not to scale. The boiling plateau is nearly seven times the melting plateau, and both dwarf the sloped parts. Read from the curve, warming water from $0$ to $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ takes $419\ \text{kJ}$, but turning it to steam at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ takes $2260\ \text{kJ}$ more.

# Properties

#### *(Coexistence Fixes the Temperature)* Two phases in equilibrium can only be at the transition temperature.

If ice and liquid water share a container and nothing is changing, the temperature is $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ at atmospheric pressure. Any heat that enters melts some ice instead of warming the mixture, and any heat that leaves freezes some water instead of cooling it. This is what makes an ice bath a fixed-temperature reference, and it is why the [[2.1.2 Temperature Scales|Celsius scale]] was pinned to the freezing and boiling points of water. The same rule tells a calorimetry problem its answer: if ice remains at the end, the final temperature is $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and the unknown is the mass that melted.

#### *(Internal Energy, Not Temperature)* A phase change stores energy as molecular potential energy.

Liquid water at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ holds $333\ \text{kJ}$ more [[2.4.1 Internal Energy|internal energy]] per kilogram than ice at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The two have the same temperature and nearly the same average kinetic energy per molecule. The difference is in the arrangement. The heating curve makes the point visually. Temperature stops rising on each plateau, but the horizontal axis, which is the energy added, keeps growing.

#### *(Evaporation Cools)* Molecules that leave a liquid below its boiling point take more than their share of energy with them.

Evaporation happens at every temperature, not only at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The molecules that escape from the surface are the fastest ones, from the tail of the [[2.3.3 Maxwell Speed Distribution|speed distribution]]. Each escaping molecule also has to pay the energy that bound it to the liquid. Both effects remove more than the average energy per molecule, so the liquid left behind cools. The latent heat for evaporation at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is about $2450\ \text{kJ/kg}$, a little above $L_V$ at the boiling point. Sweating cools the body this way. Evaporating one gram of sweat removes about $2.4\ \text{kJ}$, the heat that would otherwise warm the whole body by roughly $0.01\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

#### *(Freezing Releases Heat)* The latent heat comes back out when the transition runs in reverse.

Water that freezes gives up $333\ \text{kJ}$ per kilogram to its surroundings. Fruit growers spray orchards with water before a frost for this reason. As the water freezes on the fruit it releases heat and holds the fruit near $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ instead of letting it fall further. Steam that condenses on skin releases $2260\ \text{kJ/kg}$, which is why a steam burn is far worse than a burn from boiling water at the same temperature.

#### *(Pressure Shifts the Points)* Melting and boiling temperatures, and the latent heats, depend on pressure.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not discuss how pressure shifts melting and boiling points. This property is standard textbook material.

The values in the table hold at one atmosphere. At lower pressure water boils below $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$, so food cooks slowly at high altitude, and a pressure cooker raises the boiling point to speed cooking. The latent heat of vaporization falls slowly as the boiling point rises. The dependence of transition temperature on pressure is the subject of phase diagrams, which the course does not cover.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Ice to Steam
>A $0.500\ \text{kg}$ block of ice at $-10\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is turned into steam at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Use $c_{\text{ice}} = 2100$ and $c_{\text{water}} = 4186\ \text{J}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{K})$.
>
>>[!example]- How much heat is needed in each stage, and in total?
>>The process has four stages,
>>$$Q_1 = (0.500)(2100)(10) = 10.5\ \text{kJ}, \qquad Q_2 = (0.500)(333) = 166.5\ \text{kJ},$$
>>$$Q_3 = (0.500)(4186)(100) = 209.3\ \text{kJ}, \qquad Q_4 = (0.500)(2260) = 1130\ \text{kJ}.$$
>>The total is $1516\ \text{kJ}$. Boiling alone takes three quarters of it. Warming the ice from $-10\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is under one percent.

>[!example] (Medium) Ice in a Drink
>Two ice cubes with a total mass of $0.050\ \text{kg}$, at $-10\ ^\circ\text{C}$, are dropped into $0.300\ \text{kg}$ of water at $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Ignore the glass and the surroundings.
>
>>[!example]- Does all the ice melt, and what is the final temperature?
>>Bringing the ice to $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and melting it takes $(0.050)(2100)(10) + (0.050)(333 \times 10^{3}) = 1.05 + 16.65 = 17.7\ \text{kJ}$. Cooling the water to $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ would release $(0.300)(4186)(25) = 31.4\ \text{kJ}$, which is more than enough, so all the ice melts and $T_f > 0$. The [[2.4.5 Calorimetry|heat balance]] then has four terms: the ice warming, the ice melting, the melted ice warming from $0$ to $T_f$, and the water cooling,
>>$$1050 + 16650 + (0.050)(4186)\,T_f + (0.300)(4186)(T_f - 25) = 0.$$
>>Collecting terms gives $1465\, T_f = 13695$, so $T_f = 9.3\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Fifty grams of ice cooled six times its mass of water by $16\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Most of that came from the latent heat, not from the ice being cold.

>[!example] (Hard) Steam Burn
>Compare $10\ \text{g}$ of steam at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ with $10\ \text{g}$ of liquid water at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$, each landing on skin at $37\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and cooling to skin temperature.
>
>>[!example]- How much heat does each deliver to the skin?
>>The liquid water only cools,
>>$$Q_{\text{water}} = (0.010)(4186)(63) = 2.6\ \text{kJ}.$$
>>The steam first condenses and then cools as liquid,
>>$$Q_{\text{steam}} = (0.010)(2260 \times 10^{3}) + (0.010)(4186)(63) = 22.6 + 2.6 = 25.2\ \text{kJ}.$$
>>The steam delivers almost ten times as much heat, and nearly all of it comes from condensation. Both start at the same temperature, so the difference is entirely the latent heat. This is why steam burns are more severe than scalds from boiling water.
