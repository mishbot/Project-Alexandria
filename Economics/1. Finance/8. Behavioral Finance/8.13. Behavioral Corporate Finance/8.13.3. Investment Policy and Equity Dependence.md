>[!note] Investment Policy and Equity Dependence
>**Equity dependence** is the firm-level characteristic that determines whether investor sentiment distorts *real* corporate investment, not just financing decisions. The starting question, due to Stein ($1996$), is whether a rational manager facing a mispriced stock should change *real investment* in response to the mispricing — or whether the mispricing should affect only the firm's *financing* mix while real investment is set by the standard NPV rule.
>
>**Stein ($1996$) baseline.** A manager who maximizes long-run firm value should *not* let stock-price mispricing distort real investment. When the stock is overpriced, she should issue equity (capturing the overvaluation premium) but should *not* over-invest; the proceeds should be hoarded or returned. When the stock is underpriced, she should repurchase shares but should *not* under-invest; the financing for marginal real projects should come from cash or debt rather than from issuing under-priced equity.
>
>**Four channels by which sentiment can in fact affect investment.** Stein's baseline holds only under restrictive assumptions; in a more realistic environment, sentiment can leak into real investment through any of four channels:
>1. **Equity-dependence channel.** A firm that *depends on equity* to finance its marginal investment must cut investment when its stock becomes underpriced (issuing under-priced equity is too expensive) and must over-invest when its stock becomes overpriced (issuing overpriced equity is too cheap). Firms with strong internal cash flows escape this channel; firms with weak cash flows are exposed.
>2. **Investor-pressure channel.** Vocal investors may pressure the manager to undertake the investments that *they* find attractive, regardless of NPV.
>3. **Agency channel.** A manager seeking to maximize empire-building or perquisites may use investor exuberance as cover for over-investment that the rational benchmark does not justify.
>4. **Managerial-wavering channel.** A manager who attempts to maintain her own valuation discipline may eventually waver in the face of persistent investor exuberance, mistaking the market's enthusiasm for confirmation that her conservative estimates were too low.
>
>**The Baker-Stein-Wurgler ($2003$) test.** Identification: hold the stock-price fluctuation fixed and compare investment sensitivity across firms that differ in equity dependence. The authors construct a *KZ index* (following Kaplan-Zingales) that measures financing constraints, plus simpler proxies (low cash balances, low cash flow, high leverage) for equity dependence. They find that investment of equity-dependent firms is significantly more sensitive to stock-price fluctuations than that of unconstrained firms — exactly as the equity-dependence channel predicts.
>
>**The cross-sectional implication.** A given investor-sentiment shock affects firms heterogeneously. Equity-dependent firms ride the sentiment cycle in real investment; unconstrained firms time their financing but hold real investment steady. The aggregate implication is that sentiment-driven investment cycles will be concentrated in the small-firm, growth-firm, low-cash-flow segment of the economy — exactly the segment that drove the late-$1990$s investment boom and the post-$2000$ contraction.

# Intuition

The corporate-finance puzzle is whether the *real* economy responds to investor sentiment or whether sentiment affects only paper claims. The textbook NPV rule says only fundamentals should matter for real investment; investor sentiment should be neutralized by the manager's optimization. Stein ($1996$) shows that this neutrality holds in a stylized environment where the manager has full discretion over financing and faces no constraints on substituting between equity, debt, and internal cash. The neutrality breaks down when any of those features is relaxed.

The cleanest break is the equity-dependence channel. If a firm has no internal cash flow and limited debt capacity, it must finance its marginal investment by issuing new equity. When that equity is underpriced — the firm's stock is depressed by sentiment — issuing it is expensive: the firm gives up too large a fractional claim per dollar raised. The rational manager facing this expense should cut investment back, even when the underlying projects have positive NPV. Symmetrically, when the equity is overpriced, financing is cheap, and the firm has an incentive to push investment beyond the NPV-justified level.

The Baker-Stein-Wurgler test exploits exactly this prediction. Two firms exposed to the same stock-price shock should respond differently if one is equity-dependent and one is not. The cash-rich firm holds investment steady regardless of stock-price changes; the equity-dependent firm scales its investment with the stock price. The empirical finding — substantially higher investment-sentiment sensitivity for equity-dependent firms — confirms the channel and rules out fully-rational alternatives that would predict equal sensitivity across firms.

# Properties

#### *(Stein 1996 Baseline)* Rational manager separates financing from investment.

Stein ($1996$) develops the framework's baseline result: in a frictionless setting, a rational long-run-value-maximizing manager who detects equity mispricing should change her *financing* decisions (issue when overvalued, repurchase when undervalued) without changing her *real-investment* decisions. The investment level is set by the NPV rule using true (not market) discount rates; the issuance/repurchase decision is a side bet on the mispricing. The two decisions are separable in the baseline model, which makes the empirical question of whether they are separable in practice a clean test.

#### *(Equity-Dependence Channel)* Investment scales with stock price for cash-poor firms.

The equity-dependence channel breaks the Stein separability. A firm with no internal cash and limited debt capacity must issue equity to fund the marginal project. When equity is underpriced, the firm must give up a too-large stake per dollar raised — issuance is expensive — and the manager rationally cuts marginal investment back. When equity is overpriced, issuance is cheap and the manager can profitably push investment up. The channel predicts that equity-dependent firms have positive investment-stock-price elasticities; unconstrained firms do not.

#### *(Investor-Pressure Channel)* Vocal investors push the manager toward their favored projects.

A manager facing pressure from major investors — activists, long-tenured shareholders, board members — may undertake projects that the investor group finds attractive even if the NPV is negative. The investor pressure converts the investor's behavioral biases into corporate-investment distortions. The channel is harder to identify empirically because direct measures of investor pressure are scarce, but case studies of activist campaigns and proxy contests provide qualitative support.

#### *(Agency Channel)* Sentiment provides cover for empire-building.

A manager whose private utility scales with firm size — empire-building, perquisites, prestige — may use periods of investor exuberance as opportunities to over-invest. When investors are enthusiastic about the firm's prospects, the board is less likely to scrutinize new capital expenditures, and the manager can pursue projects that would not pass muster in a sober environment. The channel converts exogenous sentiment shocks into agency-driven over-investment.

#### *(Managerial-Wavering Channel)* Persistent exuberance erodes managerial discipline.

A manager initially committed to NPV discipline may eventually waver in the face of sustained investor enthusiasm. After several quarters of being told her firm is undervalued (by analysts, business press, board members), she may begin to update her own estimate of firm value upward — interpreting the market's enthusiasm as evidence that her own conservative estimates were wrong. The channel converts external sentiment into the manager's internal valuation through a learning-with-noise mechanism.

#### *(Baker-Stein-Wurgler 2003 Empirical Test)* Equity-dependent firms have higher investment-sentiment sensitivity.

Baker, Stein, and Wurgler ($2003$) classify firms into equity-dependent and non-equity-dependent groups using a Kaplan-Zingales-style index plus simpler proxies (low cash, low cash flow, high leverage). They regress investment on stock-price fluctuations and a stock-price-times-equity-dependence interaction. The interaction coefficient is large and significant: equity-dependent firms have investment that responds about three times as much to stock-price shocks as non-equity-dependent firms. The result confirms the equity-dependence channel and rules out alternatives that would predict equal sensitivity.

#### *(Aggregate Implication)* Sentiment-driven cycles concentrate in the cash-poor, high-growth segment.

Because the equity-dependence channel binds most tightly for cash-poor, high-growth, small-cap firms, sentiment-driven investment cycles concentrate in that segment of the economy. The late-$1990$s tech-investment boom was driven primarily by exactly this category — startup and growth-stage firms that depended on equity finance and faced equity prices inflated by sentiment. The post-$2000$ contraction was symmetric, with the same firms cutting investment sharply as their equity prices collapsed and external finance dried up.

# Examples

>[!example] A cash-poor tech firm in the late 1990s
>A growth-stage internet firm in $1999$ has minimal internal cash flow, no debt capacity, and a stock price that has tripled in the past year. Marginal investment requires issuing new equity. By the equity-dependence channel, the firm should *over-invest* — equity is cheap, every project that can be funded should be funded, even ones that look marginal at fundamental valuations. By $2002$, the same firm faces a stock price that has fallen by $80\%$. The equity-dependence channel now predicts sharp investment cuts even for projects that retain positive NPV at fundamental discount rates. The empirical pattern across late-$1990$s tech firms — extreme investment booms followed by extreme contractions — fits this channel exactly.

>[!example]- A cash-rich incumbent over the same period
>A mature manufacturing firm in $1999$ has $\$5$ billion in internal cash flow per year and a stable dividend. Its stock price moves with the market but the firm finances all marginal investment internally. The equity-dependence channel does not bind: investment is set by the NPV rule using fundamental discount rates regardless of stock-price fluctuations. Through the $2000$–$2002$ decline, this firm's investment continues at roughly the same pace; through the $1999$ peak, the firm does not over-invest despite the stock-price run-up. Baker-Stein-Wurgler's empirical work confirms that this cross-sectional difference between cash-rich and cash-poor firms is large in the data.

>[!example]- The four channels in one venture
>Consider an emerging-market commodity-resource firm whose stock price is depressed relative to its book value. The equity-dependence channel pulls investment down (issuing under-priced equity is expensive). The investor-pressure channel could pull investment up if vocal investors believe the project is undervalued. The agency channel could pull investment up if the manager wants to expand regardless of value. The managerial-wavering channel pulls toward the manager's currently-formed beliefs. The four channels can pull in opposite directions; the net effect on investment depends on which channel binds tightest in any given firm-period. Disentangling the channels empirically is the frontier of the literature.
