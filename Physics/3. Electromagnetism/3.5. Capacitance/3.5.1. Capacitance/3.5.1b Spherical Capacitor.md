>[!note] Spherical Capacitor
>A **spherical capacitor** is a conducting sphere of radius $a$ inside a concentric conducting shell of inner radius $b$. A wire reaches the inner sphere through a hole in the shell, small enough to leave the field unchanged. With charges $\pm Q$ on the two conductors, the capacitance is
>$$C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\,\frac{ab}{b-a}.$$
>Between the conductors, at distance $r$ from the center with $a < r < b$, the field is radial with size $E = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$. It points from the positive conductor to the negative one. The positive conductor is always at the higher potential:
>- With $+Q$ on the shell, the field points inward, and the shell is at the higher potential.
>- With $+Q$ on the sphere, the field points outward, and the sphere is at the higher potential.
>
>In both cases the size of the potential difference is
>$$V = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{b}\right) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{b-a}{ab}.$$
>Two limits follow from the formula:
>- A **thin gap**, $b - a \ll a$, gives $C \approx \varepsilon_0(4\pi a^2)/(b-a)$. This is the [[3.5.1a Parallel-Plate Capacitor|parallel-plate]] result with area $4\pi a^2$ and gap $b - a$.
>- A **distant shell**, $b \to \infty$, leaves an **isolated sphere** with $C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 a$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture derived $C$ but took neither limit. Both follow from the formula, and Giancoli section 24.2 covers the spherical capacitor.

# Intuition

A spherical capacitor has the same structure as a parallel-plate capacitor, with curved plates. The gap $b - a$ corresponds to $d$. The surfaces correspond to $A$, but their areas differ, $4\pi a^2$ inside and $4\pi b^2$ outside. The same charge covers less area on the inner sphere, so the field is stronger there. The exact formula accounts for that change in field across the gap.

When the gap is thin, the two areas are almost equal. Each surface is then nearly flat over distances comparable to the gap, so the parallel-plate result applies. As the shell moves far away, the capacitance approaches a finite value, $4\pi\varepsilon_0 a$. Almost all of the potential difference then comes from the region near the sphere, where the field is strong.

A Gaussian sphere grows from the inner sphere to the shell, and the graph traces the size of $E$. The green area under the curve is the potential difference. Then the Hard example changes the inner radius at fixed $V$ and $b$.

# Derivation

Follow the recipe of [[3.5.1. Capacitance]]. Put $-Q$ on the inner sphere and $+Q$ on the outer shell. The shell's charge sits on its inner surface, facing the sphere, as in [[3.3.3a Conducting Shells]].

Take a Gaussian sphere of radius $r$, with $a < r < b$. It encloses only the inner sphere, so $Q_{\text{enc}} = -Q$. By [[3.3.2a Spherical Symmetry|spherical symmetry]], the field is radial, $\vec E = E_r\,\hat r$, with $E_r$ the same at every point of the Gaussian sphere. Gauss's law gives
$$E_r\,4\pi r^2 = \frac{-Q}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad E_r = -\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}.$$
The minus sign means the field points inward, from the positive shell to the negative sphere.

Now integrate along a radial line from the sphere at $r = a$ to the shell at $r = b$. The step is $d\vec s = \hat r\,dr$, and the final point $b$ is the top limit:
$$V_b - V_a = -\int_a^b E_r\,dr = -\int_a^b \left(-\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\right) dr = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_a^b \frac{dr}{r^2}.$$
The integral of $1/r^2$ is $-1/r$, so
$$V_b - V_a = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[-\frac{1}{r}\right]_a^b = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{b}\right).$$
Since $b > a$, the bracket is positive. The shell is at the higher potential, which matches its positive charge. Over a common denominator the bracket is $(b - a)/(ab)$. Dividing the charge by the potential difference gives
$$C = \frac{Q}{V_b - V_a} = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\,\frac{ab}{b-a}.$$

For the thin gap, write $d = b - a$. When $d \ll a$, $b \approx a$, so $ab \approx a^2$ and $C \approx \varepsilon_0(4\pi a^2)/d$. For the distant shell, divide the top and bottom by $b$:
$$C = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,a}{1 - a/b}.$$
As $b \to \infty$, the ratio $a/b$ goes to zero, and $C \to 4\pi\varepsilon_0 a$.

# Properties

#### *(Check the Sign)* The potential difference from the negative conductor to the positive one must come out positive, which catches a reversed integral.

The potential falls along the direction of the field. The field runs from the positive conductor to the negative one. In the derivation, two minus signs cancel. One comes from the definition of $V$, and one comes from the inward field. Reversing the limits adds a third minus sign. The result is then a negative capacitance, which is impossible. The top limit of the integral is always the final point.

#### *(Exact Area Is $4\pi ab$)* The exact result is the parallel-plate formula with area $4\pi ab$ and gap $b - a$.

Rewrite $C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\,ab/(b-a)$ as $\varepsilon_0(4\pi ab)/(b-a)$. The area $4\pi ab$ lies between the sphere's area $4\pi a^2$ and the shell's area $4\pi b^2$. It is their geometric mean, $\sqrt{(4\pi a^2)(4\pi b^2)}$. Using the sphere's area alone makes $C$ too small by the fraction $(b-a)/b$.

#### *(Units Check)* The factor $ab/(b-a)$ is a length, so $C$ comes out as $\varepsilon_0$ times meters, which is farads.

The product $ab$ is in square meters, and $b - a$ is in meters. Their ratio is in meters. Since $\varepsilon_0$ is in $\text{F/m}$, the capacitance is in farads, as it must be. The formula does not look like $\varepsilon_0 A/d$, but its units agree with it. A dropped factor shows up at once. For example, $a/(b-a)$ has no units and cannot be part of a capacitance.

#### *(Shielded From Outside)* With equal and opposite charges, nothing outside the shell can change the capacitance.

[[3.3.3a Conducting Shells]] shows that a shell around equal and opposite charge has no field outside it. Its outer surface carries no charge. Charges and conductors placed outside therefore cannot change the field between the conductors. The capacitance stays the same, and the capacitor does not affect anything outside.

#### *(Earth as a Capacitor)* An isolated conducting sphere the size of the Earth has a capacitance of only about $700\ \mu\text{F}$.

With $a = 6.37 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}$, the isolated-sphere limit gives
$$C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 a = (1.112 \times 10^{-10})(6.37 \times 10^{6}) = 7.08 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{F}.$$
A conductor the size of a planet holds less than a millifarad. This is another sign of how large a farad is. Small circuit capacitors reach microfarads only because their gaps are tiny compared with their plate areas.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) A Small Spherical Capacitor
>A metal ball of radius $5.0\ \text{cm}$ sits at the center of a metal shell of inner radius $5.5\ \text{cm}$. The potential difference between them is $500\ \text{V}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the capacitance and the charge.
>>The capacitance is
>>$$C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\,\frac{ab}{b-a} = (1.112 \times 10^{-10})\,\frac{(0.050)(0.055)}{0.005} = 6.12 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{F} = 61.2\ \text{pF}.$$
>>The charge is $Q = CV = (6.12 \times 10^{-11})(500) = 3.06 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{C}$, or $30.6\ \text{nC}$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not compare the spherical capacitor with the parallel-plate formula. The next example uses the thin-gap limit above.

>[!example] (Medium) A Thin Spherical Gap
>A sphere of radius $10.0\ \text{cm}$ sits inside a shell of inner radius $10.1\ \text{cm}$.
>
>>[!example]- Compare the exact capacitance with the parallel-plate approximation that uses the sphere's area.
>>The exact value is
>>$$C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\,\frac{ab}{b-a} = (1.112 \times 10^{-10})\,\frac{(0.100)(0.101)}{0.001} = 1.123 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{F}.$$
>>The parallel-plate formula with $A = 4\pi a^2$ and $d = b - a$ gives
>>$$C \approx \frac{\varepsilon_0(4\pi a^2)}{b - a} = (1.112 \times 10^{-10})\,\frac{(0.100)^2}{0.001} = 1.112 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{F}.$$
>>The approximation is $1\%$ low. This matches the fraction $(b-a)/b = 0.0099$.

>[!example] (Hard) Choosing the Inner Radius
>An outer shell has inner radius $b = 10.0\ \text{cm}$. The potential difference is fixed at $10.0\ \text{kV}$, and the inner radius $a$ can be chosen.
>
>>[!example]- Find the field at the surface of the inner sphere for $a = 2.0$, $5.0$, and $8.0\ \text{cm}$. Which $a$ gives the smallest field there?
>>The charge is $Q = CV = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\,abV/(b-a)$. At the surface of the inner sphere, the field is that of a point charge at the center:
>>$$E(a) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^2} = \frac{bV}{a(b-a)}.$$
>>For $a = 2.0\ \text{cm}$,
>>$$E = \frac{(0.100)(1.00 \times 10^{4})}{(0.020)(0.080)} = 6.25 \times 10^{5}\ \text{V/m}.$$
>>For $a = 5.0\ \text{cm}$, $E = 4.0 \times 10^{5}\ \text{V/m}$. For $a = 8.0\ \text{cm}$, $E = 6.25 \times 10^{5}\ \text{V/m}$ again. A small sphere puts its charge on a small area, so the field is strong. A large sphere leaves a narrow gap, which needs a large charge for the same $V$.
>>
>>The field is smallest where the product $a(b-a)$ is largest. Its derivative is $b - 2a$, which is zero at $a = b/2$. There the field is $E = 4V/b = 4.0 \times 10^{5}\ \text{V/m}$. [[3.5.4a Dielectric Breakdown]] gives the field at which air sparks.
