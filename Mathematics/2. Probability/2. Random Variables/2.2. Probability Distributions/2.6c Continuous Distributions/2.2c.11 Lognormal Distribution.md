>[!note] Lognormal Distribution
>A positive random variable $Y$ has the **lognormal distribution** with parameters $(\mu, \sigma)$, where $\sigma > 0$, when its natural logarithm is normal:
>$$\ln Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2).$$
>Here $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the mean and the standard deviation of $\ln Y$, not of $Y$. Equivalently, $Y = e^X$ for a [[2.2c.3 Gaussian Distribution|normal]] random variable $X$ with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. The density of $Y$ is
>$$g(x; \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{\sigma x\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-(\ln x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}, \qquad x > 0,$$
>and $g = 0$ for $x \le 0$. The distribution function is $\mathbb{P}[Y \le x] = N\big((\ln x - \mu)/\sigma\big)$ for $x > 0$. Here $N$ is the standard normal CDF, the function written $\Phi$ in [[2.2c.3 Gaussian Distribution]]. The median, the mean, and the variance are
>$$\operatorname{median}(Y) = e^{\mu}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[Y] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \big(e^{\sigma^2} - 1\big)\,e^{2\mu + \sigma^2}.$$
>The density is skewed to the right, so the mean lies above the median. A product of independent lognormal variables is lognormal, but a sum is not. Under [[7.6 Geometric Brownian Motion|geometric Brownian motion]] the lognormal is the distribution of a future stock price, which makes it the basis of the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula]].

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules give the density, and the mean and variance only for stock prices. The general moment formulas, the median, and the product rule are standard results beyond the modules.

# Intuition

Many positive quantities change by percentages. A stock price or an investment account grows each period by a factor such as $1.05$ or $0.97$. After $n$ periods it equals its starting value times a product of $n$ positive factors. The logarithm turns this product into a sum. A sum of many small independent terms is close to normal by the [[3.3 Central Limit Theorem|central limit theorem]]. So the logarithm of the quantity is close to normal, and the quantity itself is close to lognormal. A lognormal variable is always positive. A normal model for a price cannot guarantee that.

# Properties

#### *(Moments)* Every moment of $Y$ follows from the moment generating function of a normal variable.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules state the mean and variance only for stock prices, and leave the variance as an exercise. The general formula for $\mathbb{E}[Y^k]$ is not stated.

Write $Y^k = e^{kX}$ with $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. For any real number $k$,
$$\mathbb{E}[Y^k] = \mathbb{E}[e^{kX}] = e^{k\mu + k^2\sigma^2/2}.$$
This is the moment generating function of $X$ evaluated at $k$. Setting $k = 1$ gives the mean. Setting $k = 2$ gives $\mathbb{E}[Y^2] = e^{2\mu + 2\sigma^2}$. Subtracting $(\mathbb{E}[Y])^2 = e^{2\mu + \sigma^2}$ gives the variance in the callout. The ratio of the standard deviation to the mean is $\sqrt{e^{\sigma^2} - 1}$. It depends on $\sigma$ alone, and it is close to $\sigma$ when $\sigma$ is small.

>[!example]- Proof of $\mathbb{E}[e^{kX}] = e^{k\mu + k^2\sigma^2/2}$
>Write the expectation as an integral against the normal density of $X$. Completing the square in the exponent gives
>$$kx - \frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} = -\frac{\big(x - (\mu + k\sigma^2)\big)^2}{2\sigma^2} + k\mu + \frac{k^2\sigma^2}{2}.$$
>The first term on the right is the exponent of a normal density with mean $\mu + k\sigma^2$ and variance $\sigma^2$. That density integrates to $1$. What remains is the constant factor $e^{k\mu + k^2\sigma^2/2}$.

#### *(Right Skew)* The mode, the median, and the mean always come in the order $e^{\mu - \sigma^2} < e^{\mu} < e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules show the skewed shape in a plot. The mode formula and the probability of exceeding the mean are not stated.

The peak of the density is at $e^{\mu - \sigma^2}$. Setting the derivative of $\ln g$ to zero gives $\ln x - \mu = -\sigma^2$. The median is $e^{\mu}$, because $\mathbb{P}[Y \le e^{\mu}] = \mathbb{P}[X \le \mu] = 1/2$. The three values come together only as $\sigma \to 0$. Large outcomes raise the mean, and no outcome can fall below $0$. As a result, the probability of exceeding the mean is $\mathbb{P}[Y > \mathbb{E}[Y]] = 1 - N(\sigma/2)$, which is below $1/2$. A lognormal stock price ends below its expected value more than half of the time.

#### *(Reading the Parameters)* Quantiles of $Y$ are the exponentials of the quantiles of $\ln Y$.

The function $e^x$ is increasing, so it keeps outcomes in the same order. An interval that holds $\ln Y$ with some probability maps to an interval that holds $Y$ with the same probability. About $68\%$ of the mass of $\ln Y$ lies in $[\mu - \sigma, \mu + \sigma]$. So about $68\%$ of the mass of $Y$ lies between $e^{\mu}e^{-\sigma}$ and $e^{\mu}e^{\sigma}$. With $\sigma = 0.2$ this interval runs from $0.82$ to $1.22$ times the median. Its ends are the median divided by $e^{\sigma}$ and the median times $e^{\sigma}$. In dollars, the upper end is further from the median than the lower end. The same mapping gives the CDF in the callout, $\mathbb{P}[Y \le x] = \mathbb{P}[\ln Y \le \ln x]$.

#### *(Products and Powers)* Multiplying independent lognormal variables adds their parameters.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules do not state rules for products or powers of lognormal variables.

Let $Y_1$ and $Y_2$ be independent and lognormal with parameters $(\mu_1, \sigma_1)$ and $(\mu_2, \sigma_2)$. Then $\ln(Y_1Y_2) = \ln Y_1 + \ln Y_2$ is a sum of independent normal variables. So $Y_1Y_2$ is lognormal with parameters $\big(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}\big)$. In the same way, $cY$ for a constant $c > 0$ has parameters $(\mu + \ln c, \sigma)$. A power $Y^a$ with $a \neq 0$ has parameters $(a\mu, |a|\sigma)$, so $1/Y$ has parameters $(-\mu, \sigma)$. A sum $Y_1 + Y_2$ is not lognormal, and its distribution has no simple formula. A single stock under geometric Brownian motion has an exact lognormal price. A portfolio of several such stocks does not.

#### *(Stock Prices)* Under geometric Brownian motion, the future stock price is lognormal with parameters set by today's price, the drift, and the volatility.

If the stock price follows $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW_t$, then [[7.6 Geometric Brownian Motion]] shows that
$$\ln S_t \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\ln S_0 + \left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t,\ \sigma^2 t\right).$$
So $S_t$ is lognormal with log-mean $\ln S_0 + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)t$ and log-standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{t}$. Putting these into the mean formula gives $\mathbb{E}[S_t] = S_0e^{\mu t}$. So the drift $\mu$ is the expected growth rate of the price. The [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula]] uses the same density with $\mu$ replaced by the risk-free rate $r$.

>[!warning] Two Meanings of $\mu$ and $\sigma$
>In the definition, $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the mean and standard deviation of $\ln Y$. In the stock model, $\mu$ is the drift and $\sigma$ is the volatility per year. The lognormal parameters of $S_t$ are $\ln S_0 + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)t$ and $\sigma\sqrt{t}$, which are different numbers.

# Derivation

We want the density of $Y = e^X$, where $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. For $y > 0$, the event $\{Y \le y\}$ is the event $\{X \le \ln y\}$, because $\ln$ is increasing. So
$$\mathbb{P}[Y \le y] = \int_{-\infty}^{\ln y} \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-(u - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}\,du.$$
This is the normal CDF of $X$ evaluated at $\ln y$. Substitute $u = \ln x$, so that $du = dx/x$. The limits $u = -\infty$ and $u = \ln y$ become $x = 0$ and $x = y$:
$$\mathbb{P}[Y \le y] = \int_0^{y} \frac{1}{\sigma x\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-(\ln x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}\,dx.$$
The integrand is the density $g(x; \mu, \sigma)$ in the callout. The factor $1/x$ comes from $du = dx/x$. For $y \le 0$ the event $\{Y \le y\}$ is empty, so $g = 0$ there.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Chance of a Price Drop
>An analyst models the price of a stock one year from now as a lognormal random variable $Y$ with parameters $\mu = 3.75$ and $\sigma = 0.25$. The stock trades at $\$40$ today.
>
>>[!example]- Find the median price, the probability that the price ends below $\$35$, and the probability that it ends above $\$50$.
>>The median is $e^{3.75} = \$42.52$. For the lower tail, standardize the log price:
>>$$\mathbb{P}[Y < 35] = N\left(\frac{\ln 35 - 3.75}{0.25}\right) = N\left(\frac{3.5553 - 3.75}{0.25}\right) = N(-0.7786) = 0.2181.$$
>>For the upper tail, $(\ln 50 - 3.75)/0.25 = (3.9120 - 3.75)/0.25 = 0.6481$. So $\mathbb{P}[Y > 50] = 1 - N(0.6481) = 0.2585$. The level $\$35$ is $\$7.52$ below the median, and $\$50$ is $\$7.48$ above it. The upper tail still has more probability, because the density is skewed to the right.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The next two examples use the general moment formulas and the product rule from the Properties section.

>[!example] (Medium) Revenue Growth of a Start-Up
>A start-up has revenue of $\$2$ million this year. The ratio $Y$ of next year's revenue to this year's is lognormal with parameters $\mu = 0.10$ and $\sigma = 0.50$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the median, the mean, and the standard deviation of next year's revenue, and the probability that revenue beats its expected value.
>>Next year's revenue is $R = 2Y$ in millions of dollars. The median of $Y$ is $e^{0.10} = 1.1052$, and its mean is $e^{0.10 + 0.125} = e^{0.225} = 1.2523$. The variance of $Y$ is
>>$$\big(e^{0.25} - 1\big)\,e^{0.20 + 0.25} = 0.2840 \times 1.5683 = 0.4454,$$
>>so its standard deviation is $0.6674$. Scaling by $2$ gives a median revenue of $\$2.21$ million, a mean of $\$2.50$ million, and a standard deviation of $\$1.33$ million. Revenue beats its mean when $\ln Y > 0.225$. That has probability
>>$$1 - N\left(\frac{0.225 - 0.10}{0.50}\right) = 1 - N(0.25) = 0.4013.$$

>[!example] (Hard) Two Years of Returns
>An investor puts $\$10{,}000$ into a fund for two years. The gross return in year one, $Y_1$, is lognormal with parameters $(0.08, 0.20)$. The gross return in year two, $Y_2$, is lognormal with parameters $(0.02, 0.30)$. The two years are independent. The account value after two years is $V = 10{,}000\,Y_1Y_2$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the distribution of $V$, the probability that the account doubles, the probability that it loses money, and its expected value. Check the expected value in a second way.
>>The product rule gives $Y_1Y_2$ lognormal with parameters $\mu = 0.08 + 0.02 = 0.10$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{0.04 + 0.09} = \sqrt{0.13} = 0.3606$. The account doubles when $\ln(Y_1Y_2) > \ln 2 = 0.6931$:
>>$$\mathbb{P}[V > 20{,}000] = 1 - N\left(\frac{0.6931 - 0.10}{0.3606}\right) = 1 - N(1.6451) = 0.0500.$$
>>The account loses money when $\ln(Y_1Y_2) < 0$, which has probability $N(-0.10/0.3606) = N(-0.2774) = 0.3908$.
>>
>>The mean of the product is $e^{0.10 + 0.13/2} = e^{0.165} = 1.1794$, so $\mathbb{E}[V] = \$11{,}793.93$. For the check, independence gives $\mathbb{E}[Y_1Y_2] = \mathbb{E}[Y_1]\,\mathbb{E}[Y_2]$. The two means are $e^{0.08 + 0.02} = 1.1052$ and $e^{0.02 + 0.045} = 1.0672$. Their product is $1.1794$, which matches. The median account value is $10{,}000\,e^{0.10} = \$11{,}051.71$, below the mean.
