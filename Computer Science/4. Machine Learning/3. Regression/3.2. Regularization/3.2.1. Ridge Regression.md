>[!note] Ridge Regression
>**Ridge regression** fits a linear model by least squares with a penalty on the squared length of the coefficient vector. With standardized predictors $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ and a centered response $\mathbf{y}$, it solves
>$$\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_\lambda = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\; \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2 + \lambda\|\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2,$$
>where $\lambda \ge 0$ sets the strength of the penalty. The intercept is not penalized and equals $\bar y$. Setting the gradient to zero gives two equal closed forms:
>$$\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_\lambda = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I}_p)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^T + \lambda\mathbf{I}_n)^{-1}\mathbf{y}.$$
>The first form inverts a $p \times p$ matrix. The second, the **kernel form**, inverts an $n \times n$ matrix and is cheaper when $p > n$. Both inverses exist for every $\lambda > 0$, even when $p > n$ or columns are collinear. With the SVD $\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^T$ of rank $r$,
>$$\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_\lambda = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_i^2 + \lambda}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{u}_i^T\mathbf{y}}{\sigma_i}\,\mathbf{v}_i, \qquad \mathrm{df}(\lambda) = \sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_i^2 + \lambda}.$$
>Here $\sigma_i$ are the singular values and $\mathbf{u}_i$, $\mathbf{v}_i$ the singular vectors. The number $(\mathbf{u}_i^T\mathbf{y})/\sigma_i$ is the least-squares coefficient along $\mathbf{v}_i$, and ridge keeps the fraction $\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2 + \lambda)$ of it. The **effective degrees of freedom** $\mathrm{df}(\lambda)$ falls from $r$ at $\lambda = 0$ toward $0$ as $\lambda \to \infty$. As $\lambda \to 0$, ridge tends to OLS when $\mathbf{X}$ has full column rank and to the minimum-norm solution $\mathbf{X}^+\mathbf{y}$ otherwise.
>
>Five descriptions give the same estimator:
>
>- **Penalized least squares**: the objective above.
>- **Shifted spectrum**: add $\lambda$ to every eigenvalue $\sigma_i^2$ of $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ before inverting.
>- **MAP estimate**: with $\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol\beta \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta, \sigma^2\mathbf{I})$ and prior $\boldsymbol\beta \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \tau^2\mathbf{I})$, the posterior mode is ridge with $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$.
>- **Fake data**: OLS on $\mathbf{X}$ stacked over $\sqrt{\lambda}\,\mathbf{I}_p$, with $p$ zero responses appended to $\mathbf{y}$.
>- **Fake features**: the minimum-norm solution of $[\mathbf{X}\;\; \sqrt{\lambda}\,\mathbf{I}_n]\,\boldsymbol\eta = \mathbf{y}$, whose first $p$ entries are ridge.
>
>A **generalized ridge** (Tikhonov) penalty $\|\boldsymbol\Gamma\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2$ replaces $\lambda\mathbf{I}$ by $\boldsymbol\Gamma^T\boldsymbol\Gamma$ in the first closed form. Gradient descent on the ridge objective is **weight decay**:
>$$\boldsymbol\beta_{t+1} = (1 - 2\eta\lambda)\,\boldsymbol\beta_t + 2\eta\,\mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta_t).$$
>Here $\eta$ is the learning rate. Each step multiplies the coefficients by $1 - 2\eta\lambda$ before the data gradient acts. The factor $2$ comes from the penalty's gradient $2\lambda\boldsymbol\beta$. [[2.3.2. Cross-Validation|Cross-validation]] over a logarithmic grid chooses $\lambda$, with time-ordered folds for time series. Ridge is the usual choice when many correlated predictors each carry some signal. [[3.2.2. Lasso|The lasso]] replaces it when the model must select a few predictors. [[3.2.3. Elastic Net|Elastic net]] replaces it when correlated groups must be selected together.

>[!warning] Notation in CS 182
>CS 182 writes the weights as $\mathbf{w}$ and the number of features as $d$, so its closed form reads $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I}_d)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$. This note uses the regression notation $\boldsymbol\beta$ and $p$. Both use the unnormalized sum of squares. If the loss is averaged over the $n$ samples, the same estimator has penalty $\lambda/n$. Here $\sigma_i$ always denotes a singular value, and $\sigma^2$ without a subscript is the noise variance.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture did not define $\mathrm{df}(\lambda)$. It follows ESL §3.4.1, where it replaces the parameter count $p$ in model-selection criteria such as AIC.

# Intuition

OLS divides each data coordinate $\mathbf{u}_i^T\mathbf{y}$ by $\sigma_i$. A small singular value marks a direction along which the predictors barely vary, as with two nearly equal columns. Noise along that direction gets multiplied by the large factor $1/\sigma_i$, so the coefficients vary widely from sample to sample. Ridge replaces $1/\sigma_i$ by $\sigma_i/(\sigma_i^2 + \lambda)$. Directions with $\sigma_i^2 \gg \lambda$ keep almost all of their coefficient, and directions with $\sigma_i^2 \ll \lambda$ shrink toward zero. The fit gains some bias and loses much more variance ([[2.3.1. Bias-Variance Decomposition]]).

Hoerl and Kennard (1970) introduced the method and its name. A common reading of the name is the constant $\lambda$ added along the diagonal of $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$. The MAP view describes the same method in terms of a prior. Ridge assumes that each coefficient is small unless the data show otherwise. A larger $\lambda$ means a tighter prior around zero.

The left panel tracks five ridge coefficients as $\lambda$ grows. The right panel shows each shrink factor $\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2 + \lambda)$. Small singular directions shrink first, and no coefficient reaches exactly zero.

# Properties

#### *(Bias and Variance per Direction)* Along each singular direction, ridge adds a bias that grows with $\lambda$ and removes variance that OLS cannot avoid.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>These formulas follow Hoerl and Kennard (1970) and ESL §3.4.1. Lecture stated the trade-off without them.

Assume full column rank and write $\theta_i = \mathbf{v}_i^T\boldsymbol\beta$ for the true coefficient along $\mathbf{v}_i$. Ridge estimates $\theta_i$ with bias $-\lambda\theta_i/(\sigma_i^2 + \lambda)$ and variance $\sigma^2\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2 + \lambda)^2$. Its mean squared error along this direction is
$$\mathrm{MSE}_i(\lambda) = \frac{\sigma^2\sigma_i^2 + \lambda^2\theta_i^2}{(\sigma_i^2 + \lambda)^2},$$
which equals the OLS value $\sigma^2/\sigma_i^2$ at $\lambda = 0$. Its derivative has the sign of $\lambda\theta_i^2 - \sigma^2$. Each direction therefore improves on OLS for every $\lambda$ up to $\sigma^2/\theta_i^2$, where its error is smallest. Any $\lambda$ between $0$ and $\sigma^2/\max_i\theta_i^2$ lowers the total error below OLS. The best value for one direction matches the MAP value $\sigma^2/\tau^2$ when $\theta_i^2 = \tau^2$.

#### *(Bounded Noise Gain)* Ridge never multiplies the data along any direction by more than $1/(2\sqrt{\lambda})$.

OLS multiplies $\mathbf{u}_i^T\mathbf{y}$ by $1/\sigma_i$, which has no upper bound as $\sigma_i \to 0$. Ridge uses $\sigma_i/(\sigma_i^2 + \lambda)$. Since $\sigma_i^2 + \lambda \ge 2\sigma_i\sqrt{\lambda}$, this gain is at most $1/(2\sqrt{\lambda})$, reached at $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda}$. A near-duplicate predictor therefore cannot make the coefficients grow without bound. The condition number of the matrix to invert also drops, from $\sigma_1^2/\sigma_p^2$ to $(\sigma_1^2 + \lambda)/(\sigma_p^2 + \lambda)$. A smaller condition number makes the solve less sensitive to rounding and makes gradient descent converge faster. Truncated SVD, or principal components regression, keeps directions above a cutoff whole and drops the rest. Ridge shrinks every direction by a smooth fraction.

#### *(Early Stopping Acts Like Ridge)* Plain gradient descent from zero, stopped after $t$ steps, filters the singular directions much as ridge with $\lambda \approx 1/(2\eta t)$ does.

Gradient descent with step $\eta$ on $\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2$, started at $\boldsymbol\beta_0 = \mathbf{0}$, keeps the fraction
$$q_t(\sigma_i) = 1 - (1 - 2\eta\sigma_i^2)^t$$
of the least-squares coefficient along $\mathbf{v}_i$. Directions with $\sigma_i^2 \gg 1/(2\eta t)$ are nearly fit, and directions with $\sigma_i^2 \ll 1/(2\eta t)$ have barely moved. Ridge with $\lambda = 1/(2\eta t)$ makes the same split, so stopping earlier acts like a larger $\lambda$. The two filters have different shapes. For $0 < 2\eta\sigma_i^2 < 1$, the bounds $1 - e^{-2\eta t\sigma_i^2} \le q_t \le 2\eta t\sigma_i^2$ hold. Gradient descent therefore approaches $1$ exponentially fast on strong directions, while ridge approaches $1$ only like $1 - \lambda/\sigma_i^2$. Gradient descent can also overshoot. If $2\eta\sigma_i^2 > 1$, then $q_t > 1$ at every odd $t$, while the ridge fraction always stays below $1$. In practice, early stopping saves checkpoints and keeps the one with the best validation error.

#### *(Weight Decay With Adam)* The penalty and the shrinkage step agree for plain gradient descent and disagree for adaptive optimizers.

[[6.4.3. Adam|Adam]] divides each gradient coordinate by a running scale of its past gradients. A penalty gradient $2\lambda\boldsymbol\beta$ added to the loss gets divided too, so coordinates with large gradients decay less. AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) applies the shrinkage $\boldsymbol\beta \leftarrow (1 - \eta\lambda_{\text{wd}})\,\boldsymbol\beta$ as a separate step, outside the moment estimates. Its rate $\lambda_{\text{wd}}$ sits in the factor without a $2$, so $\lambda_{\text{wd}} = 2\lambda$ matches the penalty here under plain gradient descent. PyTorch's `weight_decay` argument for SGD follows the same convention. Neural-network losses are usually averaged over the samples, which divides the matching $\lambda$ by $n$.

#### *(Fast Cross-Validation)* Ridge has an exact formula for leave-one-out cross-validation, so one SVD gives the whole error curve.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>This shortcut is from Golub, Heath and Wahba (1979) and ESL §7.10. Lecture chose $\lambda$ with held-out data.

Let $\mathbf{H}_\lambda = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T$ be the ridge hat matrix, so $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{H}_\lambda\mathbf{y}$ and $\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{H}_\lambda) = \mathrm{df}(\lambda)$. The leave-one-out error needs no refitting:
$$\mathrm{CV}_{(n)}(\lambda) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{y_i - \hat y_i}{1 - [\mathbf{H}_\lambda]_{ii}}\right)^2.$$
Generalized cross-validation (GCV) replaces each $[\mathbf{H}_\lambda]_{ii}$ by the average $\mathrm{df}(\lambda)/n$. One SVD of $\mathbf{X}$ gives $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ and the diagonal of $\mathbf{H}_\lambda$ for every $\lambda$ on the grid. The identity holds for a fixed design, including one with an unpenalized intercept column. It assumes exchangeable rows, so a time series still needs [[2.4.5 Time-Series Cross-Validation|time-ordered folds]].

# Derivation

Expanding the objective gives $f(\boldsymbol\beta) = \mathbf{y}^T\mathbf{y} - 2\boldsymbol\beta^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} + \boldsymbol\beta^T(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})\boldsymbol\beta$. Its gradient is $2(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})\boldsymbol\beta - 2\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$. Its Hessian $2(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})$ is positive definite for $\lambda > 0$, so the unique minimizer solves
$$(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I}_p)\,\boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}.$$
For the kernel form, start from the identity $\mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^T + \lambda\mathbf{I}_n) = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I}_p)\mathbf{X}^T$. Multiply on the left by $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I}_p)^{-1}$ and on the right by $(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^T + \lambda\mathbf{I}_n)^{-1}$:
$$(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I}_p)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T = \mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^T + \lambda\mathbf{I}_n)^{-1}.$$
For the SVD form, substitute $\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^T$ with square orthogonal $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{V}$. Then $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I} = \mathbf{V}(\boldsymbol\Sigma^T\boldsymbol\Sigma + \lambda\mathbf{I})\mathbf{V}^T$, and
$$\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_\lambda = \mathbf{V}(\boldsymbol\Sigma^T\boldsymbol\Sigma + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\boldsymbol\Sigma^T\mathbf{U}^T\mathbf{y}.$$
The middle matrix $(\boldsymbol\Sigma^T\boldsymbol\Sigma + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\boldsymbol\Sigma^T$ has entries $\sigma_i/(\sigma_i^2 + \lambda)$ on its diagonal, which gives the sum in the callout. For the MAP view, the negative log posterior is, up to constants,
$$-\log p(\boldsymbol\beta \mid \mathbf{y}) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2 + \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\|\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2.$$
Multiplying by $2\sigma^2$ gives the ridge objective with $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$. For the fake-data view, the stacked residual has squared length $\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{0} - \sqrt{\lambda}\,\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2$, which is the ridge objective.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Two Uncorrelated Predictors
>A bakery models daily croissant sales over $n = 50$ days. Its two standardized predictors, temperature and a weekend indicator, happen to be uncorrelated, so $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} = 50\,\mathbf{I}_2$. OLS gives $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (-3, 9)$ croissants per standard deviation.
>
>>[!example]- What does ridge return with $\lambda = 10$ and with $\lambda = 50$, and how many effective degrees of freedom does each fit use?
>>Both squared singular values equal $50$, so ridge multiplies both OLS coefficients by $50/(50 + \lambda)$. With $\lambda = 10$ the fraction is $5/6$, which gives $(-2.5, 7.5)$ and $\mathrm{df} = 2 \cdot 5/6 \approx 1.67$. With $\lambda = 50$ the fraction is $1/2$, which gives $(-1.5, 4.5)$ and $\mathrm{df} = 1$. The ratio of the two coefficients stays $-1/3$. With uncorrelated predictors of equal scale, ridge shrinks all coefficients by one factor and keeps their ranking.

>[!example] (Medium) Today's and Yesterday's Temperature
>A utility predicts daily electricity demand from today's and yesterday's standardized temperature over $n = 100$ days. The two predictors have correlation $0.9$, so
>$$\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 100 & 90 \\ 90 & 100 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 60 \\ 50 \end{bmatrix}.$$
>
>>[!example]- Compare OLS and ridge with $\lambda = 10$ along the two singular directions.
>>The eigenvectors of $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ are $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 1)/\sqrt{2}$ with $\sigma_1^2 = 190$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = (1, -1)/\sqrt{2}$ with $\sigma_2^2 = 10$. The first direction tracks the two-day average temperature, and the second tracks the change from yesterday. OLS gives $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (0.789, -0.211)$. Ridge keeps $190/200 = 0.95$ of the coefficient along $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $10/20 = 0.5$ along $\mathbf{v}_2$. The result is $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{10} = (0.525, 0.025)$, so the negative coefficient on yesterday nearly vanishes. Along $\mathbf{v}_2$, the OLS coefficient has variance $\sigma^2/10$ and the ridge coefficient has variance $10\sigma^2/20^2 = \sigma^2/40$. The condition number of the matrix to invert drops from $19$ to $10$.

>[!example] (Medium) More Biomarkers Than Patients
>A pilot study has $n = 2$ patients and $p = 3$ biomarkers. Take the columns as given and $\lambda = 1$:
>$$\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}.$$
>
>>[!example]- Compute ridge with the kernel form, check it against the first form, and say how a new patient is predicted.
>>OLS has no unique answer, since $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is a singular $3 \times 3$ matrix. The kernel form needs $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^T + \mathbf{I} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, whose inverse is $\frac{1}{8}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. So $\mathbf{a} = (\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^T + \mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y} = (0.125, 0.625)$, and $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_1 = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{a} = (0.125, 0.625, 0.75)$. Multiplying this vector by $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{I}$ returns $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y} = (1, 2, 3)$, so the first form agrees. A new patient $\mathbf{x}$ gets $\hat y = \mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{a}$, which uses only inner products with the training rows. Replacing those inner products by a kernel gives kernel ridge regression ([[3.10.1 Kernels]]). With $20{,}000$ genes and $100$ patients, the kernel form inverts a $100 \times 100$ matrix in place of a $20{,}000 \times 20{,}000$ one.

>[!example] (Hard) Stopping Early Versus Adding a Penalty
>An engineer fits a linear model by gradient descent on $\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2$, from $\boldsymbol\beta_0 = \mathbf{0}$ with step $\eta = 0.001$. The squared singular values of $\mathbf{X}$ are $400$, $25$ and $1$. She stops after $t = 100$ steps.
>
>>[!example]- How much of each least-squares coefficient does she keep, which ridge penalty gives a similar fit, and what would weight decay with that penalty do?
>>Every direction has $2\eta\sigma_i^2 \le 0.8 < 1$, so each fraction rises steadily toward $1$. The fractions $q_{100} = 1 - (1 - 0.002\,\sigma_i^2)^{100}$ are $1.000$, $0.994$ and $0.181$. The matching penalty is $\lambda = 1/(2\eta t) = 5$, and ridge keeps $400/405 = 0.988$, $25/30 = 0.833$ and $1/6 = 0.167$. Both methods keep the strong direction and suppress the weak one. Gradient descent keeps more of the middle direction because its fraction approaches $1$ exponentially fast. Gradient descent on the ridge objective with $\lambda = 5$ multiplies the coefficients by $1 - 2\eta\lambda = 0.99$ at every step. Those iterates converge to the ridge solution however long she trains. A step above $1/(2 \cdot 400) = 0.00125$ would make the strong direction oscillate, and a step above $1/400 = 0.0025$ would diverge.
