>[!note] Jointly Gaussian Random Variables
>**Joint Gaussians** are random variables whose joint behavior is completely characterized by a mean vector and covariance matrix. Let $X = \begin{bmatrix}X_{1} & \ldots & X_{n}\end{bmatrix}^T$ be a random vector, and let $Z \in\mathbb{R}^\ell$ be the standard normal vector (i.e., $Z_{i} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ for $i=1,\ldots,\ell$ i.i.d.). Then, $X_{1}, \ldots, X_{n}$ are **jointly Gaussian** if and only if:
>1. There exist some mean $\mu=\mathbb{E}[X] \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and real matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times \ell}$, s. t. $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, AA^T)$, and
>$$X=AZ+\mu.$$
>2. All elements of the affine hull of $X$ (i.e., all affine combinations $u \in \operatorname{aff}(X_1, \ldots, X_{n})$) follow a normal distribution, and therefore $\operatorname{aff}(X_1, \ldots, X_{n})$ is a **Gaussian subspace**.
>
>The PDF of joint Gaussians is defined by a positive definite covariance matrix $\Sigma = AA^T \in \mathbb{S}_{++}^n$ with entries $\Sigma_{i,j}=\operatorname{Cov}(X_{i}, X_{j})$, such that
>$$f_{X}(x_{1},\ldots, x_{n})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{ (2\pi)^n \det(\Sigma) }}\exp\left( -\frac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)  \right).$$
# Properties
#### (*Independence*) Joint Gaussians $X_{1},\ldots, X_{n}$ are indep. iff $\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2_{1}, \ldots, \sigma^2_{n})$.
Without loss of generality, we will consider the case of two jointly Gaussian random variables. Let $X_{1},X_{2}$ be uncorrelated joint Gaussians. Therefore,
$$\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{1}^2 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_{2}^2\end{bmatrix}.$$
Substituting $\Sigma^{-1}$ into the joint PDF, we have
$$\begin{align}
f_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{ (2\pi)^2 }\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}\exp\left( -\frac{1}{2}\left[ \frac{(x_{1}-\mu_{1})^2}{\sigma_{1}^2}+\frac{(x_{2}-\mu_{2})^2}{\sigma_{2}^2} \right] \right) \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{ 2\pi }\sigma_{1}}\exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \frac{(x_{1}-\mu_{1})^2}{\sigma_{1}^2} \right) \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{ 2\pi }\sigma_{2}}\exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \frac{(x_{2}-\mu_{2})^2}{\sigma_{2}^2} \right) \\
&= f_{X_{1}}(x_{1}) \cdot f_{X_{2}}(x_{2}).
\end{align}$$

#### (*Linear Combinations*) Any $U$ s.t. $U_{i} \in \operatorname{aff}(X_{1}, \ldots, X_{n})$ is jointly Gaussian.
Let $U$ be a random vector, such that each $U_{i}=\gamma_{i}^TX+\beta_{i}$ is a linear combination of $X=[X_{1}, \ldots, X_{n}]^T$ for some vector of coefficients $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}^n$. We also know that 
$$X_{i}=a_{i}^TZ+\mu_{i},$$
and therefore, 
$$\begin{align}
U_{i}&=\gamma_{1}(a_{1}^TZ+\mu_{1})+\gamma_{n}(a_{n}^TZ+\mu_{n})+\beta_{1} \\
&= \gamma^T(AZ+\mu)+\beta_{1}  \\
&= \gamma^TAZ+\gamma^T\mu+\beta_{1},
\end{align}$$
which is simply an affine combination of $\gamma_{i}X_{i}+\beta_{1}$. As such,
$$
U=\begin{bmatrix} U_{1} \\ \vdots \\ U_{n} \end{bmatrix} = \Gamma AZ+(\Gamma \mu+\beta)=BZ+\lambda,
$$
which satisfies definition 1 with coefficients $B=\Gamma A$ and mean $\lambda=\Gamma\mu+\beta$, and therefore $U \sim \mathcal{N}(\Gamma \mu+\beta, \Gamma AA^T\Gamma^T)$

#### (*Covariance*) For $X=AZ+\mu$, it follows that $\Sigma=AA^T$.
Let $\Sigma_{i,j}=\operatorname{Cov}(X_{i}, X_{j})$. This means that
$$\begin{align}
\Sigma_{i,j}&=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}X_{j}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]\mathbb{E}[X_{j}] \\
&= \mathbb{E}[(a_{i}^TZ+\mu_{i})(a_{j}^TZ+\mu_{j})]-(a_{i}^T\mathbb{E}[Z]+\mu_{i})(a_{j}^T\mathbb{E}[Z]+\mu_{j}) \\
&= \mathbb{E}[a_{i}^TZa^T_{j}Z+a_{j}^TZ\mu_{i}+a_{i}^TZ\mu_{j}+\mu_{i}\mu_{j}]-\mu_{i}\mu_{j} \\
&= a_{i}^T\mathbb{E}[ZZ^T]a_{j} \\
&= a_{i}^Ta_{j}
\end{align}$$
#### (*Covariance Partition*) For random vectors $X,Y$, $\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{X} & \Sigma_{XY} \\ \Sigma_{YX} & \Sigma_{Y}\end{bmatrix}$.
#### (*Covariance LLSE*) For any joint Gaussians, $\mathbb{L}(Y \mid X)=\mu_{Y}+\Sigma_{YX}\Sigma^{-1}_{X}(X-\mu_{X})$.
#### (LLSE/MMSE Equivalence) For any joint Gaussians, $\mathbb{L}(Y \mid X)=\mathbb{M}(Y \mid X)$.

# Example
>[!example] I. MMSE for Joint Gaussians
>Find $\mathbb{E}(X \mid Y,Z)$ for the jointly Gaussian random vector
>
>$$
\begin{bmatrix}
X\\
Y\\
Z
\end{bmatrix}
\sim
\mathcal{N}\left(
\mu = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0\end{bmatrix},
\Sigma=
\begin{bmatrix}
5 & 3 & 1\\
3 & 9 & 3\\
1 & 3 & 2
\end{bmatrix}
\right).
>$$
>
>>[!example]- Solution.
>>We proceed by acknowledging that $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=3$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Z)=1$, as well as that $\operatorname{Var}(X)=5$. Since LLSE is equal to the MMSE, we use the formula for general partition of the LLSE:
>>
>>$$\begin{align}
\mathbb{L}[X \mid Y,Z]&=\mathbb{L}[X \mid Y]+\mathbb{L}[X \mid Z-\mathbb{L}[Z \mid Y]] \\
&= \mathbb{L}[X \mid Y]+\mathbb{L}\left[ X \mid Z-\frac{1}{3}Y \right].
\end{align}$$
>>The variable $Z-\frac{1}{3}Y$ has a mean of 0 (since both $Y,Z$ are zero-mean), and a variance of $\operatorname{Var}(Z)-\frac{1}{9}\operatorname{Var}(Y)=2-1=1$. We also have by bilinearity that $\operatorname{Cov}\left( X,Z-\frac{1}{3}Y \right)=\operatorname{Cov}(X,Z)-\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=1-1=0$. Since the variables are jointly Gaussian, this implies that $X$ and $Z-\frac{1}{3}Y$ are independent, and therefore,
>>$$\begin{align}
\mathbb{L}\left[X \mid Z-\frac{1}{3}Y\right]=0.
\end{align}$$
>>Finally, we have that
>>$$\begin{align}
\mathbb{L}[X \mid Y,Z]&=\mathbb{L}[X,Y] \\
&= \boxed{\frac{1}{3}Y}.
\end{align}$$

>[!example] II. Joint Gaussians As Linear Transformations of IID Gaussians
>Let $X=\begin{bmatrix}X_{1} & X_{2}\end{bmatrix}^T$ be a jointly Gaussian random vector with mean $\mu=\begin{bmatrix}0 & 0\end{bmatrix}^T$ and covariance
>$$
\Sigma_X = 
\begin{bmatrix}
2 & 1\\
1 & 2
>\end{bmatrix}
=
>\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\sqrt{3}+1}{2} & \frac{\sqrt{3}-1}{2}\\[6pt]
\frac{\sqrt{3}-1}{2} & \frac{\sqrt{3}+1}{2}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\sqrt{3}+1}{2} & \frac{\sqrt{3}-1}{2}\\[6pt]
\frac{\sqrt{3}-1}{2} & \frac{\sqrt{3}+1}{2}
>\end{bmatrix}^T
= A A^T.$$
>
>**A)** Express $X_{1},X_{2}$ as linear combinations of i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables.
>>[!example]- A) Solution.
>>Let $Z$ be a random vector, such that $Z_{i} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Since $\mu=0$, we have
>>$$\begin{bmatrix}
X_{1} \\ X_2
>>\end{bmatrix}
=
>>A\begin{bmatrix}
Z_{1} \\ Z_{2}
>>\end{bmatrix}
=
>>\begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{\sqrt{ 3 }+1}{2}Z_{1}+\dfrac{\sqrt{ 3 }-1}{2}Z_{2} \\
\dfrac{\sqrt{ 3 }-1}{2}Z_{1}+\dfrac{\sqrt{ 3 }+1}{2}Z_{2}
>>\end{bmatrix}.$$
>
>**B)** Find a matrix $B$ such that the components of $BX$ are i.i.d. $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$.
>>[!example]- B) Solution.
>>We need to find some $B$, such that $BX=Z$. We observe that $X=AZ$. Since $A$ is invertible (i.e., it has a nonzero determinant), $A^{-1}X=Z$, and therefore $B=A^{-1}$. We proceed by finding $\det A=\sqrt{3}$, and therefore,
>>$$B=A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{ 3 }}\begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{\sqrt{ 3 }+1}{2} & \dfrac{1-\sqrt{ 3 }}{2} \\
\dfrac{1-\sqrt{ 3 }}{2} &\dfrac{\sqrt{ 3 }+1}{2}
\end{bmatrix}$$
