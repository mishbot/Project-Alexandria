>[!note] Heat Engine
>A **heat engine** is a device that runs in a **thermal cycle**, a closed path on the $P$-$V$ diagram, and turns heat into work. Around one cycle the working gas returns to its starting state, so $\Delta E_{\text{int}} = 0$ and the [[2.5.2 First Law of Thermodynamics|first law]] gives
>$$W_{\text{cycle}} = Q_{\text{cycle}},$$
>the net work done per cycle equals the net heat absorbed per cycle. On the diagram the net work is the area enclosed by the loop, positive for a clockwise cycle. Some legs of the cycle take heat in and others give heat out. Call the total heat taken in $Q_H$ and the total heat given out $Q_L$, both as **positive numbers**. Then $Q_{\text{cycle}} = Q_H - Q_L$ and
>$$W = Q_H - Q_L.$$
>The **efficiency** is what you get divided by what you pay for,
>$$e = \frac{W}{Q_H} = 1 - \frac{Q_L}{Q_H}.$$
>An engine with $e = 1$ would reject no heat. No such engine exists, and the [[2.7.2 Second Law of Thermodynamics|second law]] says why. The heat comes from a **high-temperature reservoir** at $T_H$, and the rejected heat goes to a **low-temperature reservoir** at $T_L$. A reservoir is a body so large that taking heat from it or dumping heat into it leaves its temperature unchanged. The highest efficiency any engine can reach between $T_H$ and $T_L$ is fixed by those two temperatures alone, and the [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot engine]] note derives it.
>
>To analyze a cycle, label its vertices and identify the process on each leg. Compute the heat $Q_i$ on each leg from the [[2.5.3 Thermodynamic Processes|process table]]. Add the positive heats to get $Q_H$ and the negative ones to get $Q_L$,
>$$Q_H = \sum_{Q_i > 0} Q_i, \qquad Q_L = \sum_{Q_i < 0} |Q_i|, \qquad Q_{\text{cycle}} = \sum_i Q_i = Q_H - Q_L.$$
>Then $W$ and $e$ follow. Compute the heats rather than the work: the heats also give the efficiency, and they are what the second law limits.

# Intuition

A steam engine on a bench boils water with an electric heater, lets the steam push a piston, and vents the cooled steam. It turns a wheel and lights a bulb. Three things happen every cycle: heat goes in, work comes out, and leftover heat goes out with the exhaust. The exhaust is not a defect that a better design could remove. The gas has to be brought back to its starting state to run again, and returning it means cooling it, and cooling it means dumping heat somewhere colder. The sketch shows where the three flows are.

Every engine, however it is built, fits the reservoir picture. A car engine's hot reservoir is the burning fuel and its cold reservoir is the air the exhaust and radiator dump into. A power plant's hot reservoir is the boiler and its cold reservoir is a river or a cooling tower. The details of the cycle fix $Q_H$, $Q_L$, and $W$, but the picture is always heat in at the top, work out the side, and heat out the bottom.

# Properties

#### *(Net Work Is Enclosed Area)* On a $P$-$V$ diagram the net work per cycle is the area inside the loop.

The work on each leg is the area under it, positive when the volume grows and negative when it shrinks. Going around a clockwise loop, the upper legs run left to right at high pressure and the lower legs run right to left at low pressure. The positive area exceeds the negative area by the area of the loop. A counterclockwise loop has negative net work and is a [[2.7.5a Refrigerator|refrigerator]]. Two cycles with the same enclosed area deliver the same work per cycle whatever their shapes.

#### *(Heats Are Positive, Signs Go in the Formulas)* $Q_H$ and $Q_L$ are magnitudes, and the minus sign lives in $W = Q_H - Q_L$.

The first law gives each leg a signed heat. Legs with $Q > 0$ contribute to $Q_H$. Legs with $Q < 0$ contribute to $Q_L$ after the sign is dropped. Keeping the two totals positive matches the reservoir diagram and every textbook formula. A common error is to carry the negative sign into $Q_L$ and get $e = 1 + Q_L/Q_H > 1$. When that happens, a sign was kept that should have been dropped.

#### *(Compare at Equal Temperatures)* Efficiencies of different cycles can only be compared when they run between the same $T_H$ and $T_L$.

There are infinitely many closed curves in the $P$-$V$ plane, so there are infinitely many cycles, and asking which is most efficient is meaningless without a constraint. Every cycle has a highest temperature and a lowest temperature. Since $T = PV/Nk$ at each point, they sit where the product $PV$ is largest and smallest,
$$T_{\max} = \frac{(PV)_{\max}}{Nk}, \qquad T_{\min} = \frac{(PV)_{\min}}{Nk}.$$
Fix those two and the comparison becomes fair. Common cycles use two adiabats and two isochores, as in the Otto cycle. Others use two isotherms and two isochores, or two adiabats and two isobars. Under that constraint one cycle is best, and the [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot engine]] note identifies it and derives its efficiency.

#### *(Reservoirs Do Not Change)* A reservoir supplies or absorbs heat without changing its own temperature.

A lake, the atmosphere, or a furnace that is continually refueled all qualify. Taking $1\ \text{kJ}$ from a lake changes its temperature by nothing measurable. The assumption lets $T_H$ and $T_L$ be treated as constants over a cycle. It also gives a reservoir the simplest possible bookkeeping when [[2.7.6 Entropy|entropy]] is introduced later in this section.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Efficiency from the Heats
>An engine takes in $8.0\ \text{kJ}$ of heat per cycle and exhausts $5.5\ \text{kJ}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the work per cycle and the efficiency.
>>The first law over a cycle gives $W = Q_H - Q_L = 8.0 - 5.5 = 2.5\ \text{kJ}$, and
>>$$e = \frac{W}{Q_H} = \frac{2.5}{8.0} = 0.31.$$
>>The engine turns $31$ percent of the heat into work and throws away the rest. Whether this is good depends on the temperatures it runs between, which the [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot engine]] note makes precise. For an engine running between $600\ \text{K}$ and $300\ \text{K}$, $31$ percent turns out to be respectable.

>[!example] (Medium) Which Legs Carry the Heat
>A monatomic gas runs the rectangular cycle in the diagram above: $A \to B$ at constant volume, $B \to C$ at constant pressure, $C \to D$ at constant volume, $D \to A$ at constant pressure.
>
>>[!example]- Identify the legs that supply $Q_H$ and the legs that carry $Q_L$, without numbers.
>>On $AB$ the pressure rises at fixed volume, so the temperature rises and $Q = \tfrac{3}{2} V \Delta P > 0$. On $BC$ the gas expands at fixed pressure, so the temperature rises and $Q = \tfrac{5}{2} P \Delta V > 0$. Both feed $Q_H$. On $CD$ the pressure falls at fixed volume, and on $DA$ the gas is compressed at fixed pressure; both cool the gas and both have $Q < 0$, so both feed $Q_L$. The hottest state is $C$, where $PV$ is largest, and the coldest is $A$. Reading the signs off the diagram before computing anything prevents most errors in cycle problems.

>[!example] (Hard) The Rectangular Cycle in Numbers
>The cycle above runs with $P_A = 1.00 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$, $P_B = 3.00 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$, $V_A = 1.00\ \text{L}$, and $V_C = 3.00\ \text{L}$, using a monatomic gas.
>
>>[!example]- Find $Q_H$, $Q_L$, the work, the efficiency, and the ratio of the extreme temperatures.
>>Use the [[2.5.3 Thermodynamic Processes|process table]] with $d = 3$ on each leg. On $AB$, $Q = \tfrac{3}{2} V_A \Delta P = \tfrac{3}{2}(1.00 \times 10^{-3})(2.00 \times 10^{5}) = 300\ \text{J}$. On $BC$, $Q = \tfrac{5}{2} P_B \Delta V = \tfrac{5}{2}(3.00 \times 10^{5})(2.00 \times 10^{-3}) = 1500\ \text{J}$. So $Q_H = 1800\ \text{J}$. On $CD$, $Q = -\tfrac{3}{2} V_C \Delta P = -900\ \text{J}$, and on $DA$, $Q = -\tfrac{5}{2} P_A \Delta V = -500\ \text{J}$, so $Q_L = 1400\ \text{J}$. Then
>>$$W = Q_H - Q_L = 400\ \text{J}, \qquad e = \frac{400}{1800} = 0.22.$$
>>The enclosed area is $(2.00 \times 10^{5})(2.00 \times 10^{-3}) = 400\ \text{J}$, which checks. The extreme temperatures are at $C$ and $A$, with $T_C / T_A = P_C V_C / P_A V_A = 9$, a wide temperature range for a $22$ percent efficiency. Most of its $Q_H$ enters on the isobaric leg at temperatures well below $T_C$, and most of its $Q_L$ leaves at temperatures well above $T_A$. The [[2.7.4b Carnot Engine|Carnot engine]] note returns to this cycle and shows how far it falls short of the best possible.
