>[!note] Contingent Claim
>A **contingent claim**, or **derivative**, is a financial contract whose value at its expiration date $T$ is determined exactly by the market price of an underlying asset at $T$. Let $S_T$ be the price of the underlying at $T$ and $K$ a price written into the contract. The four basic payoffs at $T$, per unit of the underlying, are
>$$\underbrace{S_T - K}_{\text{long forward}}, \qquad \underbrace{K - S_T}_{\text{short forward}}, \qquad \underbrace{\max(S_T - K, 0)}_{\text{call}}, \qquad \underbrace{\max(K - S_T, 0)}_{\text{put}}.$$
>A [[2.2.2a Forward Contract|forward]] is an obligation to buy (long) or sell (short) the underlying at the delivery price $K$ on date $T$. A [[2.2.3 Futures|futures contract]] is a forward traded on an exchange in standard sizes and dates. An [[2.3.1a Option Contract|option]] gives its holder the right, with no obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) at the strike $K$. Derivatives are built on primary assets such as equity, bonds, cash, and commodities. They are used to hedge risk, to make arbitrage profits, to speculate with leverage, and for tax, accounting, or regulatory reasons.
>
>A forward's payoff is linear in $S_T$, and an option's payoff is not. A linear payoff can be copied by a fixed portfolio of the underlying and cash, held to $T$ (**static replication**). An option payoff needs a portfolio that is rebalanced as the price moves (**dynamic replication**). [[4.2.2a Arbitrage]] turns either kind of replication into a price, since a derivative must cost what its replicating portfolio costs.

# Intuition

Derivatives exist because people want to trade a price risk without trading the asset today. An airline needs fuel next year and fears a price rise. An oil producer fears a price fall. A forward lets both fix the price now, with no cash paid today. Each side gives up the gain from a favorable move to remove the loss from an unfavorable one. An option removes the loss and keeps the gain. Its holder pays a premium today for this one-sided protection.

The three panels show the payoff at $T$ as a function of $S_T$. The forward is a straight line through $K$ with slope $1$, so it gains and loses dollar for dollar with the underlying. The call and the put bend at $K$. Each pays nothing on one side of $K$, so their payoffs are never negative. For this reason an option has a positive price today, while a new forward is set up to cost nothing.

# Properties

#### *(Linear and Non-Linear Payoffs)* A forward can be priced without knowing how volatile the underlying is, and an option cannot.

The forward pays $S_T - K$ over the whole range of $S_T$. Its value today depends only on the price today, the interest rate, the time to expiry, the delivery price, and the dividends ([[2.2.2b Forward Price]]). A call pays $S_T - K$ only in the states where $S_T > K$. Its price therefore depends on how likely the underlying is to end above $K$, and that depends on the volatility $\sigma$ (the typical size of price swings). An option price depends on six inputs: $S$, $K$, $T$, $r$, the dividends, and $\sigma$. The first five also set the forward price. Volatility is the new one.

#### *(Static and Dynamic Replication)* Forwards and swaps can be copied with a portfolio set up once, while options need a portfolio that changes as the price moves.

A static replication is bought at time $0$ and held unchanged to $T$. One share bought with a loan of $Ke^{-rT}$ is worth $S_T - K$ at $T$, which is the long forward payoff. Forwards, futures, vanilla interest rate swaps, currency swaps, and total return swaps all have static replications ([[2.2.5 Swaps]]). A fixed holding of $a$ shares and a bond that pays $b$ at $T$ has the payoff $aS_T + b$. That is a straight line in $S_T$, so no fixed holding produces the bend of an option at $K$. A dynamic replication changes the number of shares as $S$ moves. In a [[2.3.3c Binomial Tree|binomial tree]], the share count $\Delta$ is recomputed at every node. In continuous time this becomes [[2.3.3d Delta Hedging|delta hedging]]. The (Hard) example below shows both cases with numbers.

#### *(Uses)* The same contract can serve a hedger, an arbitrageur, or a speculator.

A hedger holds a derivative to cancel a risk it already has. An airline buys crude oil forwards to fix its fuel cost. An investor who owns a stock can keep its value at $T$ inside a range with a [[2.3.2b Collar|zero-cost collar]]: buy a put with a low strike and pay for it by selling a call with a high strike. An arbitrageur trades a derivative against its replicating portfolio when the two prices differ. A speculator uses a derivative for leverage, a large exposure for a small outlay. Take a stock at $\$40$ and a call with strike $\$40$ that costs $\$3$. If the stock ends at $\$46$, the stock gains $15\%$. The call pays $\$6$, twice its premium, a return of $100\%$. If the stock ends at $\$38$, the stock loses $5\%$ and the call loses the whole premium. Tax rules also matter. In the US, gains on qualified exchange-traded futures fall under the 60/40 rule: $60\%$ of the gain is taxed as long-term and $40\%$ as short-term, whatever the holding period.

#### *(Zero-Sum Payoffs)* The payoff to one side of a derivative is the loss of the other side.

The long forward pays $S_T - K$ and the short forward pays $K - S_T$, so the two sum to zero in every state. An option is the same once the premium is counted: the writer receives the premium today and pays the holder's payoff at $T$. A derivative therefore creates no wealth. It moves gains and losses between the two parties. The losing side must pay at $T$, so each side faces the risk that the other defaults. This is **counterparty risk**. A forward is a private contract, so each party carries this risk in full. A futures exchange settles gains and losses every day through margin accounts, so a losing party cannot build up a large unpaid debt ([[2.2.3 Futures]]).

#### *(Effect on the Cash Market)* Derivative prices carry information about the underlying, and derivative trades move its price.

Derivative prices show market sentiment. A high price for put options, for example, shows that traders will pay a lot for protection against a fall. Trades also pass from the derivative market to the cash market (the market for the underlying itself). Index arbitrageurs buy or sell the stocks in an index whenever the index futures price moves away from its no-arbitrage value. A large sale of index futures therefore leads to sales of the stocks. The 2010 Flash Crash in US stocks has been linked to unusual trading in S&P 500 futures.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Four Payoffs at Expiry
>A forward, a call, and a put on the same stock all have $K = \$50$ and expire on the same date. The stock may end at $\$35$, $\$45$, $\$50$, $\$55$, or $\$70$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the payoff of a long forward, a short forward, a long call, and a long put at each price, and say which payoffs can be negative.
>>Apply the four formulas from the callout with $K = 50$.
>>
>>| $S_T$ | $35$ | $45$ | $50$ | $55$ | $70$ |
>>|---|---|---|---|---|---|
>>| Long forward $S_T - 50$ | $-15$ | $-5$ | $0$ | $5$ | $20$ |
>>| Short forward $50 - S_T$ | $15$ | $5$ | $0$ | $-5$ | $-20$ |
>>| Long call $\max(S_T - 50, 0)$ | $0$ | $0$ | $0$ | $5$ | $20$ |
>>| Long put $\max(50 - S_T, 0)$ | $15$ | $5$ | $0$ | $0$ | $0$ |
>>
>>Only the forwards have negative payoffs. The long forward loses below $\$50$ and the short forward loses above it. The call and the put never pay less than zero, which is what their premiums buy. In every column the call payoff minus the put payoff equals the long forward payoff. [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity]] turns this fact into a pricing rule.

>[!example] (Medium) An Airline Fixes Its Fuel Cost
>An airline will need fuel equal to $500{,}000$ barrels of crude oil in six months. Assume its fuel bill moves one for one with the crude oil price. The six-month crude oil forward price is $\$78$ per barrel. The airline goes long a cash-settled forward on $500{,}000$ barrels with $K = \$78$, and buys its fuel in the spot market at $T$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the airline's net fuel cost if crude oil ends at $\$62$, $\$78$, or $\$95$ per barrel, and say what the hedge gives up.
>>The airline pays $500{,}000\,S_T$ for fuel and receives $500{,}000\,(S_T - 78)$ from the forward. The net cost is
>>$$500{,}000\,S_T - 500{,}000\,(S_T - 78) = 500{,}000 \times 78 = \$39{,}000{,}000.$$
>>The price $S_T$ cancels, so the cost is the same in every state.
>>
>>| $S_T$ | Fuel bought at spot | Forward payoff | Net cost |
>>|---|---|---|---|
>>| $\$62$ | $\$31.0$M | $-\$8.0$M | $\$39.0$M |
>>| $\$78$ | $\$39.0$M | $\$0$ | $\$39.0$M |
>>| $\$95$ | $\$47.5$M | $+\$8.5$M | $\$39.0$M |
>>
>>The hedge gives up the $\$8.0$ million saving when oil falls to $\$62$. A call option on oil would keep that saving and still cap the cost, but it costs a premium today. In practice jet fuel and crude oil do not move exactly one for one, so some risk remains. This leftover risk is called basis risk.

>[!example] (Hard) Why a Call Needs Dynamic Replication
>A stock trades at $\$100$ and pays no dividends. The risk-free rate is $5\%$ per year with continuous compounding, and $T = 1$ year. Consider a forward with delivery price $\$100$ and a call with strike $\$100$. A static portfolio holds $a$ shares and a zero-coupon bond that pays $b$ at $T$, where $b < 0$ means a loan. It pays $aS_T + b$ at $T$.
>
>>[!example]- Find a static portfolio that copies the forward and its cost. Then show that no static portfolio copies the call when $S_T$ can be $\$80$, $\$100$, or $\$120$, and find the one that works when only $\$80$ and $\$120$ are possible.
>>The forward pays $S_T - 100$. Take $a = 1$ and $b = -100$. The portfolio pays $S_T - 100$ for every value of $S_T$, so it copies the forward exactly. It costs one share minus a loan of $100e^{-0.05} = \$95.12$, so the forward is worth $100 - 95.12 = \$4.88$ today. The number is positive because $\$100$ is below the forward price $100e^{0.05} = \$105.13$.
>>
>>The call pays $0$, $0$, and $20$ at $S_T = 80$, $100$, and $120$. Matching the first two states requires $80a + b = 0$ and $100a + b = 0$. Subtracting gives $20a = 0$, so $a = 0$ and $b = 0$. That portfolio pays $0$ at $S_T = 120$, where the call pays $20$. Matching $S_T = 80$ and $S_T = 120$ instead gives $40a = 20$, so $a = 0.5$ and $b = -40$. That portfolio pays $0.5 \times 100 - 40 = 10$ at $S_T = 100$, where the call pays $0$. Three points that do not lie on one line cannot be matched by $aS_T + b$, so no static portfolio copies the call.
>>
>>With only two states, $a = 0.5$ and $b = -40$ work. The portfolio is half a share and a loan of $40e^{-0.05} = \$38.05$. It costs $50 - 38.05 = \$11.95$, which is the call price in that two-state market. This is the [[2.3.3a One-Step Binomial Model]]. With more states, the trader splits time into steps and picks a new $a$ after each price move. The call is then copied by a share count that changes over time, which is dynamic replication.
