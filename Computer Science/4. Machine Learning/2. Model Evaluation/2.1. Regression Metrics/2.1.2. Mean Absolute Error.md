>[!note] Mean Absolute Error
>The **mean absolute error** (MAE) averages the absolute residuals of $n$ predictions:
>$$\mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} |y_i - \hat y_i| = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} |e_i| .$$
>Here $y_i$ is the true value, $\hat y_i$ is the prediction and $e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$ is the residual. The pointwise loss is the **absolute error** $\ell(y, \hat y) = |y - \hat y|$, also called the L1 loss. MAE is in the units of $y$. Fitting a linear model by minimizing $\sum_i |y_i - \mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol\beta|$ is called **least absolute deviations** (LAD) regression. MAE has five headline facts:
>
>- **Minimizer**: the best constant prediction is any sample median. Over all functions of $\mathbf{x}$, the population risk $\mathbb{E}|y - f(\mathbf{x})|$ is minimized by the conditional median of $y$ given $\mathbf{x}$.
>- **Laplace likelihood**: suppose $y_i$ has the Laplace density $\frac{1}{2b}\exp(-|y_i - f(\mathbf{x}_i)|/b)$ with scale $b$. The negative log-likelihood is $n\log(2b) + \frac{1}{b}\sum_i |e_i|$. Minimizing MAE is then maximum likelihood, and the MLE of $b$ is the training MAE.
>- **Subgradient**: $\partial\ell/\partial\hat y = -\operatorname{sign}(y - \hat y)$ for $y \ne \hat y$. At $y = \hat y$ the loss has a corner. Every value in $[-1, 1]$ is then a subgradient. A subgradient is the slope of a line through the corner that stays below the loss.
>- **Robustness**: every point pulls on the fit with the same strength, whatever the size of its residual. The median has breakdown point $1/2$, so it stays bounded while fewer than half the values are corrupted.
>- **No closed form**: LAD regression is a linear program, solved by the simplex method, interior-point methods or iteratively reweighted least squares.
>
>MAE is the robust alternative to [[2.1.1. Mean Squared Error|MSE]] for data where outliers dominate or the median is wanted. [[2.1.3. Huber Loss|Huber loss]] keeps most of its robustness and smooths the corner at zero.

# Intuition

The name lists the steps. Take each error, drop its sign, and average. An error of $10$ costs ten times an error of $1$, and no more. A model trained on MAE therefore rates one error of $10$ and ten errors of $1$ as equally bad. MAE is also easy to read. An MAE of $3$ minutes means the predictions miss by $3$ minutes on average.

The median appears for a counting reason. Move a constant prediction $c$ up by a small amount. Every point above $c$ loses that amount of error, and every point below $c$ gains it. The total falls while more points lie above $c$ than below. It stops falling where the two counts balance, and that point is the median. The size of each residual never enters this count. A data-entry error of a million dollars moves the median no more than a one-dollar error on the same side.

The animation moves a constant guess $c$ across seven values. MSE is lowest at the mean, and MAE is lowest at the median. When the largest value moves farther out, only the mean follows it.

# Properties

#### *(Robust in $y$, Weak in $\mathbf{x}$)* LAD regression resists outliers in the response and fails against points with extreme inputs.

A huge $y_i$ at an ordinary $\mathbf{x}_i$ pulls the LAD fit with unit strength, like every other point. It therefore cannot move the line far. A point with an extreme input behaves differently. Its contribution to the subgradient with respect to $\boldsymbol\beta$ is $\pm\mathbf{x}_i$, so a large input carries a large weight. The fit then tilts toward that point until it passes close to it. LAD regression therefore has breakdown point $1/n$ for corrupted inputs, the same as least squares (Rousseeuw and Leroy, 1987). Methods with a high breakdown point, such as least median of squares or [[4.4.1. RANSAC]], handle bad inputs.

#### *(Constant Pull Slows Convergence)* Gradient descent on MAE does not slow down near the optimum.

Each point's subgradient with respect to its prediction has size $1$ whatever the residual. The full subgradient therefore stays large near the minimum. A fixed learning rate then overshoots and oscillates around the optimum instead of settling. Subgradient methods converge with a decaying learning rate, such as $\eta_t \propto 1/\sqrt{t}$. Deep learning frameworks set the gradient of $|e|$ at $e = 0$ to $0$, which picks one valid subgradient. These training issues are one reason [[2.1.3. Huber Loss|Huber loss]] often replaces MAE as a training loss.

#### *(Efficiency Cost)* Under Gaussian noise the median needs about $57\%$ more data than the mean for the same precision.

The sample median of $n$ Gaussian values with variance $\sigma^2$ has asymptotic variance $\pi\sigma^2/(2n)$, while the mean has variance $\sigma^2/n$. The ratio $2/\pi \approx 0.64$ is the **asymptotic efficiency** of the median relative to the mean. Under Laplace noise with scale $b$ the comparison flips. The median has variance $b^2/n$ and the mean has $2b^2/n$, so the median is twice as efficient. MAE therefore gains on heavy-tailed noise and loses on Gaussian noise. Huber loss recovers most of the lost efficiency and keeps most of the robustness.

#### *(Many Minimizers)* The MAE minimizer can be a whole interval, and an optimal LAD fit passes through data points.

With an even number of values, every point between the two middle values is a median. For the values $[2, 5, 9, 14]$, every $c \in [5, 9]$ gives the same total absolute error of $16$. LAD regression shows the same effect. Its objective is piecewise linear, so a minimum sits at a corner of the linear program. With $p$ predictors and an intercept, at least one optimal LAD fit passes exactly through $p + 1$ data points. Software returns one of the optimal fits, and a small change in the data can make it jump to another.

#### *(Quantile Loss)* Tilting the absolute error gives a loss whose minimizer is any chosen quantile.

The **pinball loss** at level $\tau \in (0, 1)$ is
$$\rho_\tau(e) = \max\big(\tau e,\ (\tau - 1)e\big).$$
It charges $\tau$ per unit of underprediction ($e > 0$) and $1 - \tau$ per unit of overprediction ($e < 0$). Its minimizer is the $\tau$-quantile of $y$ given $\mathbf{x}$. MAE is the case $\tau = 1/2$, since $\rho_{1/2}(e) = |e|/2$. Suppose a store loses $9$ dollars per unit of unmet demand and $1$ dollar per unsold unit. It should then stock the $0.9$ quantile of demand. Fitting a model with this loss is **quantile regression** (Koenker and Bassett, 1978). Prince (*Understanding Deep Learning*, ch. 5) lists it with the Laplace loss among regression losses.

# Derivation

We show that a median minimizes the total absolute error. Let $g(c) = \sum_{i=1}^n |y_i - c|$. Away from the data values, the term for $y_i$ has slope $-1$ if $y_i > c$ and $+1$ if $y_i < c$. The slope of $g$ is therefore
$$g'(c) = \#\{i : y_i < c\} - \#\{i : y_i > c\}.$$
The function $g$ is convex and piecewise linear. Its slope is negative while more than half of the values lie above $c$. It is positive while more than half lie below. The minimum lies where the slope changes sign, which is at a median. A point far above $c$ adds $-1$ to the slope, the same as a point barely above $c$.

For the Laplace link, suppose $y_i = f(\mathbf{x}_i) + \varepsilon_i$ with independent Laplace noise of scale $b$. The Laplace density is a two-sided [[2.2c.4 Exponential Distribution|exponential]] density. The log-likelihood is
$$\log L = \sum_{i=1}^n \log\left(\frac{1}{2b}\exp\left(-\frac{|e_i|}{b}\right)\right) = -n\log(2b) - \frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^n |e_i| .$$
For fixed $b$, maximizing $\log L$ minimizes the sum of absolute residuals. Setting $\partial\log L/\partial b = -n/b + \sum_i |e_i|/b^2 = 0$ gives $\hat b = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i |e_i|$, the training MAE. The Laplace tails decay like $\exp(-|e|/b)$, much more slowly than the Gaussian $\exp(-e^2/(2\sigma^2))$. A model that expects large errors more often penalizes each one less.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Late Deliveries
>A delivery app predicts five delivery times in minutes. The true times are $y = [32, 25, 41, 28, 36]$, and the predictions are $\hat y = [30, 27, 35, 29, 34]$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute MAE and RMSE. What share of each comes from the worst delivery?
>>The residuals are $e = [2, -2, 6, -1, 2]$. The absolute values sum to $13$, so $\mathrm{MAE} = 2.6$ minutes. The squares sum to $49$, so $\mathrm{MSE} = 9.8$ and $\mathrm{RMSE} = 3.13$ minutes. The worst delivery contributes $6/13 = 46\%$ of the total absolute error and $36/49 = 73\%$ of the total squared error. The ratio $\mathrm{RMSE}/\mathrm{MAE} = 1.20$ is close to the Gaussian value of $1.25$, so no single error dominates here.

>[!example] (Medium) A Mansion on the Street
>Five houses on one street sold for $[410, 450, 470, 520, 2900]$ thousand dollars. An appraiser must quote one typical price for the street.
>
>>[!example]- Find the best constant under absolute error and under squared error, and compare their MAEs.
>>The median is $470$ and the mean is $950$. At the median the absolute deviations are $[60, 20, 0, 50, 2430]$, so the MAE is $2560/5 = 512$. At the mean the MAE is $3900/5 = 780$. The mean lies above four of the five houses by at least $430$. Squared error ranks the two quotes the other way, with RMSE $975.6$ at the mean and $1087.3$ at the median. Each loss prefers its own minimizer. A buyer looking at the four ordinary houses is better served by the median.

>[!example] (Hard) Calibrating a Load Sensor
>A load sensor should output $\beta$ volts per kilogram, with no offset. A technician applies loads $x = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]$ kg and reads $y = [2.1, 3.9, 6.0, 8.2, 20.0]$ volts. The last reading is faulty.
>
>>[!example]- Fit $y = \beta x$ by least absolute deviations and by least squares. How far does the faulty reading move each estimate?
>>Since every $x_i > 0$, the LAD objective is $\sum_i |y_i - \beta x_i| = \sum_i x_i\,|y_i/x_i - \beta|$. Its minimizer is the median of the ratios $y_i/x_i$ with weights $x_i$. The ratios are $[2.1, 1.95, 2.0, 2.05, 4.0]$. Sorted with their weights they are $1.95\,(2)$, $2.0\,(3)$, $2.05\,(4)$, $2.1\,(1)$, $4.0\,(5)$. The cumulative weights are $2, 5, 9, 10, 15$, and half the total is $7.5$. The weighted median is therefore $\hat\beta_{\text{LAD}} = 2.05$. As a check, the slope of the objective is $-5$ on $(2.0, 2.05)$ and $+3$ on $(2.05, 2.1)$. Least squares through the origin gives $\hat\beta_{\text{OLS}} = \sum_i x_iy_i/\sum_i x_i^2 = 160.7/55 = 2.92$. With a correct last reading of $10.0$, the two estimates are $2.00$ and $2.01$. The fault moves LAD by $0.05$ and OLS by $0.91$. Any last reading of $10.25$ volts or more gives the LAD estimate $2.05$. The OLS estimate grows without bound as the reading grows.
