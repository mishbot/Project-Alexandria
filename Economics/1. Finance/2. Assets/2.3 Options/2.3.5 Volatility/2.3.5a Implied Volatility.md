>[!note] Implied Volatility
>The **implied volatility** $\sigma_I$ of an option is the volatility at which the Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) price equals the market price. The contract fixes the strike $K$ and the expiry $T$. The market quotes the stock price $S$, the risk-free rate $r$ and the dividend yield $q$. Volatility is the one input that nobody quotes. For a European call with market price $c_{\text{mkt}}$ and time to expiry $\tau = T - t$, the implied volatility solves
>$$Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1) - Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2) = c_{\text{mkt}}, \qquad d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r - q + \sigma_I^2/2)\tau}{\sigma_I\sqrt{\tau}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma_I\sqrt{\tau}.$$
>The left side is the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|BSM call price]] at $\sigma = \sigma_I$, and $N$ is the standard normal CDF. A put defines its implied volatility in the same way.
>
>Three different volatilities matter for an option. They differ in the period they describe and in how they are measured.
>- **Realized volatility** $\sigma_R$ is the volatility the stock delivers during the option's life $(t, T)$. The BSM equation needs this number, and nobody knows it at time $t$.
>- **Historical volatility** is estimated from past returns ([[3.1f Historical Volatility]]). It helps forecast $\sigma_R$, for example through GARCH models. GARCH (Generalized AutoRegressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity) models let the variance of the next return depend on recent returns.
>- **Implied volatility** $\sigma_I$ is read from option prices today. It is the option market's expectation of $\sigma_R$.
>
>The call price rises strictly with $\sigma$, since its vega $\mathcal{V} = Se^{-q\tau}\sqrt{\tau}\,n(d_1)$ is positive. Here $n(x) = e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ is the standard normal density. So a market price has exactly one implied volatility when it lies strictly inside the no-arbitrage bounds,
>$$\max\big(Se^{-q\tau} - Ke^{-r\tau},\, 0\big) < c_{\text{mkt}} < Se^{-q\tau}.$$
>The lower bound is the call price in the limit $\sigma \to 0$. The upper bound is its limit as $\sigma \to \infty$. The equation has no closed-form solution, so $\sigma_I$ is found by iteration.
>
>The extraction assumes a stable yield curve and an accurate dividend forecast. Both are reasonable for short-dated options. A European call and put with the same $K$ and $T$ share one implied volatility, since both prices satisfy put-call parity. In markets, $\sigma_I$ changes with $K$ and $T$, and the [[2.3.5b Volatility Surface]] records that pattern.

# Intuition

Every input to the BSM formula except $\sigma$ can be read from the contract or from a market quote. Volatility describes the future, so the formula needs a forecast of it. Option prices already contain one. Traders run the formula backward and solve for the $\sigma$ that the market price implies. The result is the price expressed in volatility units. A \$2 option on a \$40 stock and a \$30 option on a \$900 stock are hard to compare by price. Their implied volatilities compare directly, and so do implied volatilities across strikes and expiries.

# Properties

#### *(Numerical Solution)* Bisection and Newton's method find $\sigma_I$ by iteration, and both rely on the price rising with $\sigma$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules back out implied volatility from the BSM formula but give no numerical method. Bisection and Newton's method are standard choices.

The two methods trade safety against speed.
- **Bisection** starts from two volatilities $\sigma_{\text{lo}}$ and $\sigma_{\text{hi}}$ whose BSM prices lie below and above $c_{\text{mkt}}$. It prices the midpoint and keeps the half whose ends still lie on opposite sides of the market price. After $m$ steps the bracket has width $(\sigma_{\text{hi}} - \sigma_{\text{lo}})/2^m$. Bisection cannot fail once a bracket exists, but it gains only one binary digit per step.
- **Newton's method** replaces the price curve by its tangent line at the current guess. The slope of that line is vega.

Each Newton step moves the guess to the point where the tangent line meets the market price,
$$\sigma_{m+1} = \sigma_m - \frac{c_{\text{BSM}}(\sigma_m) - c_{\text{mkt}}}{\mathcal{V}(\sigma_m)}.$$
Here $c_{\text{BSM}}(\sigma_m)$ and $\mathcal{V}(\sigma_m)$ are the model price and vega at the guess $\sigma_m$. Near the money the method converges in two or three steps. Far from the money, vega can be close to zero, so a step can overshoot far outside any sensible range. A common safeguard switches to bisection whenever a Newton step leaves the current bracket.

#### *(Reading the Number)* $\sigma_I\sqrt{\tau}$ is the standard deviation of the log return until expiry that the option price implies.

Under the BSM model, $\ln(S_T/S_t)$ has standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{\tau}$. A two-month option with $\sigma_I = 32\%$ therefore implies a standard deviation of $32\% \times \sqrt{2/12} = 13.06\%$ until expiry. Divided by $\sqrt{250}$ instead, the same $32\%$ implies a typical daily move of $2.02\%$. Both conversions use the square-root-of-time rule from [[3.1f Historical Volatility]]. The number is the market's expectation of $\sigma_R$ plus any premium that option sellers charge. A premium for downside risk raises implied volatility at low strikes ([[2.3.5b Volatility Surface]]). Comparing $\sigma_I$ with the realized volatility that follows is the basis of [[2.3.5c Volatility Trading]].

#### *(Wrong Inputs)* An error in the dividend yield shifts the call and put implied volatilities in opposite directions.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules state the assumptions on the yield curve and dividends. The size of the shift below is an extension.

The implied volatility absorbs every error in the other inputs. Suppose the true yield is $q$ but the analyst uses $q + \delta q$. The model call price then falls by about $\tau Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1)\,\delta q$, and the model put price rises by about $\tau Se^{-q\tau}N(-d_1)\,\delta q$. To match the same market prices, the two implied volatilities shift by
$$\delta\sigma_I^{\text{call}} \approx \frac{\tau Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1)}{\mathcal{V}}\,\delta q, \qquad \delta\sigma_I^{\text{put}} \approx -\frac{\tau Se^{-q\tau}N(-d_1)}{\mathcal{V}}\,\delta q.$$
Each shift is the price error divided by vega, with opposite signs for the call and the put. So a wrong dividend forecast pulls the two implied volatilities apart. In the (Hard) example, ignoring a $1.6\%$ yield opens a gap of three points. The numerators grow like $\tau$ and vega grows like $\sqrt{\tau}$, so the shifts grow roughly like $\sqrt{\tau}$. This is why the extraction is most reliable for short-dated options. An error in $r$ works the same way through rho ([[2.3.4c Vega and Rho]]).

#### *(Calls and Puts Share One Volatility)* Put-call parity forces a European call and put with the same strike and expiry to have the same implied volatility.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules do not state this result. It is covered only in the homework.

The BSM prices satisfy [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity|put-call parity]] at every value of $\sigma$,
$$c_{\text{BSM}}(\sigma) - p_{\text{BSM}}(\sigma) = Se^{-q\tau} - Ke^{-r\tau}.$$
The right side does not depend on $\sigma$. Let $\sigma_c$ and $\sigma_p$ be the implied volatilities of the call and the put. By definition, each market price equals the BSM price at its own implied volatility:
$$c_{\text{mkt}} = c_{\text{BSM}}(\sigma_c), \qquad p_{\text{mkt}} = p_{\text{BSM}}(\sigma_p).$$
Suppose the market prices satisfy parity too. Then
$$p_{\text{mkt}} = c_{\text{mkt}} - Se^{-q\tau} + Ke^{-r\tau} = c_{\text{BSM}}(\sigma_c) - Se^{-q\tau} + Ke^{-r\tau} = p_{\text{BSM}}(\sigma_c).$$
The first step is market parity. The second uses the definition of $\sigma_c$, and the third is BSM parity at $\sigma_c$. Uniqueness then gives $\sigma_p = \sigma_c$.

If the quoted implied volatilities differ, parity fails and an arbitrage exists. Take $\sigma_p > \sigma_c$. The trade sells the put, buys the call, shorts the stock, and lends $Ke^{-r\tau}$. With a dividend yield, the short position starts at $e^{-q\tau}$ shares. The cash today is
$$p_{\text{mkt}} + Se^{-q\tau} - c_{\text{mkt}} - Ke^{-r\tau} = p_{\text{BSM}}(\sigma_p) - \big[c_{\text{BSM}}(\sigma_c) - Se^{-q\tau} + Ke^{-r\tau}\big] = p_{\text{BSM}}(\sigma_p) - p_{\text{BSM}}(\sigma_c).$$
The first step uses the two definitions, and the second is BSM parity at $\sigma_c$. The result is positive because the put price rises with $\sigma$. A call and a put with the same $K$ and $T$ have the same vega, so the gap is about $\mathcal{V}(\sigma_p - \sigma_c)$. When $\sigma_c > \sigma_p$, every leg of the trade is reversed. The argument uses BSM only to translate prices into volatilities. It holds even when the stock does not follow the BSM model.

# Derivation

We show that each call price strictly between the bounds has exactly one implied volatility. Write $c(\sigma)$ for the BSM call price as a function of $\sigma$, with the other inputs fixed and $\tau > 0$.

The price rises strictly with $\sigma$. Its derivative is vega, which [[2.3.4c Vega and Rho]] derives:
$$\frac{\partial c}{\partial \sigma} = \mathcal{V} = Se^{-q\tau}\sqrt{\tau}\,n(d_1) > 0.$$
Every factor on the right is positive.

Next, find the limit as $\sigma \to \infty$. Write $m = \ln(S/K) + (r - q)\tau$ and split each fraction in two:
$$d_1 = \frac{m}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} + \frac{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}{2}, \qquad d_2 = \frac{m}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}} - \frac{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}{2}.$$
These are the callout's $d_1$ and $d_2$ written at a general $\sigma$. As $\sigma \to \infty$, the second terms dominate, so $d_1 \to \infty$ and $d_2 \to -\infty$. Then $N(d_1) \to 1$ and $N(d_2) \to 0$, and $c \to Se^{-q\tau}$.

Then find the limit as $\sigma \to 0$. Now the first terms dominate, so the sign of $m$ decides. Note that $m > 0$ exactly when $Se^{-q\tau} > Ke^{-r\tau}$.
- If $m > 0$, both $d_1$ and $d_2$ tend to $\infty$, and $c \to Se^{-q\tau} - Ke^{-r\tau}$.
- If $m < 0$, both tend to $-\infty$, and $c \to 0$.
- If $m = 0$, both tend to $0$, and $c \to \frac{1}{2}\big(Se^{-q\tau} - Ke^{-r\tau}\big) = 0$.

In every case the limit is the lower no-arbitrage bound for a European call on a stock with yield $q$,
$$\lim_{\sigma \to 0} c(\sigma) = \max\big(Se^{-q\tau} - Ke^{-r\tau},\, 0\big).$$
The function $c(\sigma)$ is continuous, so by the intermediate value theorem it takes every value between its two limits. It is strictly increasing, so it takes each value only once. Every $c_{\text{mkt}}$ strictly inside the bounds therefore has exactly one $\sigma_I$. A price outside them breaks a bound of [[2.3.1b Option Price Bounds]] and has no implied volatility. For a put, the same steps give the range $\max(Ke^{-r\tau} - Se^{-q\tau}, 0) < p_{\text{mkt}} < Ke^{-r\tau}$.

>[!example] (Easy) Bisection by Hand
>A stock trades at \$40 and pays no dividends. A three-month European call with strike \$40 trades at \$2.75. The risk-free rate is $5\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Use four steps of bisection, starting from the bracket $[20\%, 60\%]$, to locate the call's implied volatility within $2.5$ percentage points.
>>First check the bracket. With $\tau = 0.25$, the BSM price is \$1.85 at $\sigma = 20\%$ and \$4.99 at $\sigma = 60\%$. The market price of \$2.75 lies between them, so $\sigma_I$ lies in $[20\%, 60\%]$.
>>
>>Each step prices the midpoint. At $\sigma = 40\%$, $d_1 = (0.05 + 0.08)(0.25)/0.20 = 0.1625$ and $d_2 = 0.1625 - 0.20 = -0.0375$. So $N(d_1) = 0.5645$ and $N(d_2) = 0.4850$, and
>>$$c = 40 \times 0.5645 - 39.50 \times 0.4850 = \$3.42.$$
>>Here $39.50 = 40e^{-0.0125}$ is the discounted strike. The other steps use the same formula.
>>
>>| Step | Bracket | Midpoint | Price at midpoint | New bracket |
>>|---|---|---|---|---|
>>| 1 | $[20\%, 60\%]$ | $40\%$ | \$3.42, above \$2.75 | $[20\%, 40\%]$ |
>>| 2 | $[20\%, 40\%]$ | $30\%$ | \$2.63, below \$2.75 | $[30\%, 40\%]$ |
>>| 3 | $[30\%, 40\%]$ | $35\%$ | \$3.03, above \$2.75 | $[30\%, 35\%]$ |
>>| 4 | $[30\%, 35\%]$ | $32.5\%$ | \$2.83, above \$2.75 | $[30\%, 32.5\%]$ |
>>
>>After four steps $\sigma_I$ lies between $30\%$ and $32.5\%$. Further steps give $\sigma_I = 31.48\%$.

>[!example] (Medium) Mismatched Call and Put Volatilities
>A stock trades at \$72 and pays no dividends. Six-month European options with strike \$75 are quoted at an implied volatility of $26\%$ for the call and $31\%$ for the put. The risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Show that the quotes allow an arbitrage, build the trade, and explain whether the trade depends on the BSM model being right.
>>Convert each quote into a price with the BSM formula and $\tau = 0.5$. The call costs \$4.61 at $26\%$, and the put costs \$7.14 at $31\%$. Parity requires
>>$$c - p = S - Ke^{-r\tau} = 72 - 75e^{-0.02} = 72 - 73.51 = -\$1.51.$$
>>The quotes give $c - p = 4.61 - 7.14 = -\$2.53$. The put is expensive relative to the call, which matches its higher implied volatility.
>>
>>Sell the expensive put and buy the cheap call. Also sell one share short and lend \$73.51 for six months. The cash flow today is
>>$$-4.61 + 7.14 + 72 - 73.51 = +\$1.02.$$
>>In symbols, the call price is $c_{\text{BSM}}(26\%)$ and the put price is $p_{\text{BSM}}(31\%)$. BSM parity at $26\%$ turns $c_{\text{mkt}} - S + Ke^{-r\tau}$ into $p_{\text{BSM}}(26\%) = \$6.12$. So the cash is
>>$$p_{\text{mkt}} + S - c_{\text{mkt}} - Ke^{-r\tau} = p_{\text{BSM}}(31\%) - p_{\text{BSM}}(26\%) = 7.14 - 6.12 = \$1.02.$$
>>At expiry, the long call and the short put together pay $S_T - 75$ in every state. Buying back the share costs $S_T$, and the loan returns \$75. The total is zero. The trade keeps \$1.02 today and owes nothing later.
>>
>>The profit is close to vega times the gap in volatility. Vega is $20.31$ per unit of $\sigma$ at either volatility, and $20.31 \times 0.05 = \$1.02$. The trade uses BSM only to turn the quotes into prices. Its payoff rests on put-call parity, which holds in any model without arbitrage.

>[!example] (Hard) Index Options With an Unknown Dividend Yield
>A stock index stands at $4{,}800$. Six-month European options with strike $4{,}700$ trade at \$333.21 for the call and \$183.00 for the put. The risk-free rate is $3.8\%$ per year with continuous compounding. An analyst does not know the index's dividend yield.
>
>>[!example]- Find the dividend yield implied by the two prices, find the implied volatility with Newton's method from a guess of $25\%$, check it with the put, and find the two implied volatilities the analyst would get by ignoring dividends.
>>Parity with a dividend yield is $c - p = Se^{-q\tau} - Ke^{-r\tau}$. The discounted strike is $4{,}700e^{-0.019} = 4{,}611.54$. Solving parity for the stock term gives
>>$$Se^{-q\tau} = 333.21 - 183.00 + 4{,}611.54 = 4{,}761.75.$$
>>This is the index value net of the dividends paid before expiry. Then $e^{-0.5q} = 4{,}761.75/4{,}800 = 0.99203$, so $q = -2\ln(0.99203) = 1.60\%$.
>>
>>Newton's method starts at $\sigma_0 = 25\%$. There $d_1 = 0.2697$, the call price is \$410.59, and vega is $1{,}295.29$. The first step is
>>$$\sigma_1 = 0.25 - \frac{410.59 - 333.21}{1{,}295.29} = 0.1903.$$
>>At $\sigma_1$ the price is \$333.54 and vega is $1{,}282.01$. The next step gives $\sigma_2 = 0.1903 - 0.33/1{,}282.01 = 0.1900$. The price at $\sigma_2$ matches \$333.21 to the cent, so $\sigma_I = 19.00\%$. Solving the put price with $q = 1.60\%$ also gives $19.00\%$, as parity requires.
>>
>>Now suppose the analyst sets $q = 0$. The call price then implies $17.08\%$, and the put price implies $20.11\%$. The gap of three points says nothing about the market's view of volatility. It comes from the missing yield, which raises the model call and lowers the model put at every $\sigma$. When a call and a put disagree in implied volatility, the dividend and rate inputs are the first things to check.
