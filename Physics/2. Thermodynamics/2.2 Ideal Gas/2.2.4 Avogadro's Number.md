>[!note] Avogadro's Number
>**Avogadro's number** $N_A$ is the number of molecules in one mole of a substance,
>$$N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}.$$
>The mole is defined so that one mole of carbon-12 atoms has a mass of $12\ \text{g}$. Since one carbon-12 atom has a mass of exactly $12\ \text{u}$, the number $N_A$ equals the number of [[2.1.1 Atomic Theory of Matter|atomic mass units]] in one gram. A substance whose molecules have mass $M$ in atomic mass units therefore has a molar mass of $M$ grams per mole. A sample of mass $m$ contains
>$$N = n N_A = \frac{m}{M}\, N_A$$
>molecules, where $n$ is the number of moles. Since 2019 the SI fixes the value $N_A = 6.02214076 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ exactly, and the mole is defined as that many entities.
>
>**Avogadro's hypothesis** states that equal volumes of any two gases at the same pressure and temperature contain the same number of molecules. It is the fact that lets one constant serve every gas in the [[2.2.3 Ideal Gas Law|ideal gas law]]. One mole of any ideal gas at STP occupies $22.4\ \text{L}$, and one cubic meter of any ideal gas at STP holds $2.69 \times 10^{25}$ molecules.
>
>**Boltzmann's constant** is the gas constant per molecule,
>$$k = \frac{R}{N_A} = \frac{8.314\ \text{J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K})}{6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}} = 1.381 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}.$$
>With it the ideal gas law reads $PV = NkT$. The product $kT$ is the natural unit of thermal energy per molecule. At room temperature ($293\ \text{K}$), $kT = 4.0 \times 10^{-21}\ \text{J}$, about $\tfrac{1}{40}$ of an electron-volt.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture defined the mole only as a count of $N_A$ particles and gave $k = R/N_A$. The carbon-12 definition, the 2019 SI value, Avogadro's hypothesis, and the STP figures were not covered. They follow Giancoli sections 17.1 and 17.9.

# Intuition

A mole is a counting unit, like a dozen. It stands for $N_A$ objects, and a count $N$ of molecules is a pure number with no units. Chemistry counts in moles because every everyday sample holds around $10^{23}$ molecules, and writing that power each time is tedious. These notes count $N$ directly and use $k$ instead of $R$. The number is enormous because atoms are tiny. A mole of atoms packed into a cube has about $8 \times 10^{7}$ atoms along each edge, the cube root of $6 \times 10^{23}$. The sketch shows that cube, with each dot standing for many atoms.

Nobody can count molecules one at a time, but anyone can weigh a sample. $N_A$ converts a mass in grams, which a balance reads, into a number of molecules. Its value is not a law of nature. It follows from two human choices, the gram as the unit of mass and carbon-12 as the reference. The hypothesis that carries Avogadro's name came first, in 1811. Chemists had found that gases combine in simple volume ratios. Two liters of hydrogen react with one liter of oxygen to give two liters of steam. Avogadro proposed that equal volumes hold equal numbers of molecules. That turns the volume ratios into molecule ratios and explains the pattern. The ideal gas law contains the hypothesis directly. At fixed $P$ and $T$, the volume $V = NkT/P$ depends on $N$ and on nothing else about the gas. The diagram shows the box that holds one mole of any ideal gas at STP.

# Properties

#### *(Counting by Weighing)* Dividing a mass by the molar mass and multiplying by $N_A$ counts molecules.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture counted the molecules in a bottle this way but did not define molar mass or show the numbers. The details follow Giancoli section 17.1.

The molar mass in grams per mole equals the molecular mass in atomic mass units because $N_A \times 1\ \text{u} = 1\ \text{g}$ to nine significant figures. Water has a molecular mass of $18.0\ \text{u}$, so a mole of water is $18.0\ \text{g}$, about one tablespoon. Molar masses of gases come from their molecular formulas. Nitrogen gas is $\text{N}_2$ with $M = 28.0\ \text{g/mol}$, oxygen gas is $\text{O}_2$ with $M = 32.0\ \text{g/mol}$, and the mixture in air averages $29.0\ \text{g/mol}$. The molecular formula matters. Treating nitrogen as single atoms would undercount its molar mass by half. One molecule has mass $m = M/N_A$. For a gas of known density $\rho$, a volume $V$ holds $N = \rho V/m$ molecules.

#### *(Per Mole and Per Molecule)* $R$ and $k$ carry the same physics at two scales.

Every quantity that appears per mole with $R$ appears per molecule with $k$, because $R = N_A k$. The choice between $PV = nRT$ and $PV = NkT$ depends on what is known. A chemistry-style problem that starts from grams and molar masses uses $n$ and $R$. A problem that counts molecules, or that asks about the energy of one molecule, uses $N$ and $k$. The [[2.3.1 Molecular Interpretation of Temperature|kinetic theory]] shows that the average translational kinetic energy of a molecule is $\tfrac{3}{2} kT$. This is why $kT$ sets the energy scale of thermal motion.

#### *(How It Was Measured)* Early values of $N_A$ came from Brownian motion.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture showed Brownian motion in a classroom demonstration and credited Einstein's explanation. It did not connect Brownian motion to $N_A$. Perrin's measurements are standard textbook history.

Jean Perrin measured $N_A$ around 1908 by watching small particles suspended in water. Einstein's theory of [[4.6 Brownian Motion|Brownian motion]] relates the average distance a particle wanders in a given time to Boltzmann's constant, and therefore to $N_A = R/k$. Perrin also measured how the concentration of suspended particles falls off with height, which gives $N_A$ a second way. His values agreed with each other and with estimates from other methods. The agreement convinced the remaining doubters that atoms are real. Modern values come from X-ray measurements of the spacing of atoms in a silicon crystal of known mass.

#### *(Size of the Number)* Macroscopic samples hold so many molecules that fluctuations average out.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture noted only that such large numbers make averages reliable. The $\sqrt{N}$ estimate of fluctuations is standard textbook material.

A person counting ten molecules per second would need about $2 \times 10^{15}$ years to count one mole. This is why the pressure and temperature of a gas are steady even though the molecules move at random. The random variation in a count of $N$ items is about $\sqrt{N}$. For $N = 10^{23}$ the relative variation $1/\sqrt{N}$ is about $3 \times 10^{-12}$, far below what any gauge can detect. Only very small systems, such as the pollen grains in Brownian motion, show the fluctuations directly.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Molecules in One Breath
>A normal breath draws in about $0.50\ \text{L}$ of air at $1.013 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$ and $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- How many molecules are in one breath?
>>The ideal gas law in molecular form gives
>>$$N = \frac{PV}{kT} = \frac{(1.013 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa})(0.50 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3)}{(1.381 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K})(293.15\ \text{K})} = 1.3 \times 10^{22}.$$
>>One breath holds about a fiftieth of a mole of molecules.

>[!example] (Easy) Molecules in a Bottle
>An empty $502\ \text{mL}$ bottle holds air at STP, with density $1.29\ \text{kg/m}^3$. Treat the air as nitrogen, $\text{N}_2$, with $M = 28.0\ \text{g/mol}$.
>
>>[!example]- Count the molecules in the bottle by weighing, and check the count with the ideal gas law.
>>The mass of the air is
>>$$m_{\text{air}} = \rho V = (1.29\ \text{kg/m}^3)(0.502 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3) = 6.48 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{kg}.$$
>>One nitrogen molecule has mass
>>$$m = \frac{M}{N_A} = \frac{28.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{kg/mol}}{6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}} = 4.65 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg}.$$
>>Dividing gives $N = m_{\text{air}}/m = 1.4 \times 10^{22}$ molecules. The ideal gas law at STP gives
>>$$N = \frac{PV}{kT} = \frac{(1.013 \times 10^{5})(0.502 \times 10^{-3})}{(1.381 \times 10^{-23})(273.15)} = 1.35 \times 10^{22}.$$
>>The two counts differ by about 4 percent. Air is not pure nitrogen, and its average molar mass is $29.0\ \text{g/mol}$. With that value the two counts agree.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Lecture used molar mass only for the bottle count above. This example follows Giancoli section 17.9.

>[!example] (Medium) A Glass of Water
>A glass holds $250\ \text{g}$ of water. The molecular mass of water is $18.0\ \text{u}$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the mass of one water molecule and the number of molecules in the glass.
>>The molar mass of water is $18.0\ \text{g/mol}$, so one molecule has a mass of
>>$$m_{\text{molecule}} = \frac{18.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{kg/mol}}{6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}} = 2.99 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg}.$$
>>The glass holds $n = 250 / 18.0 = 13.9\ \text{mol}$, which is
>>$$N = n N_A = (13.9\ \text{mol})(6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}) = 8.4 \times 10^{24}$$
>>molecules. The same answer follows from dividing $0.250\ \text{kg}$ by the mass of one molecule.

>>[!example]- How many molecules remain in each cubic centimeter?
>>The number per unit volume follows from $PV = NkT$,

>>which is $2.4 \times 10^{6}$ molecules per cubic centimeter. The pressure is thirteen orders of magnitude below atmospheric, and each cubic centimeter still holds millions of molecules.
