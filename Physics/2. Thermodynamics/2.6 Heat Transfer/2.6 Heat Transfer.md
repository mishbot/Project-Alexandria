>[!note] Heat Transfer
>The earlier sections treat heat as a quantity that enters or leaves a system. This section asks how it travels. There are three routes. [[2.6.1 Thermal Conduction|Conduction]] passes energy from molecule to molecule through a material at rest, at a rate $kA\,\Delta T / L$ set by the thermal conductivity $k$ of the material. [[2.6.2 Convection|Convection]] carries energy along with a moving fluid, and it has no simple formula. [[2.6.3 Thermal Radiation|Radiation]] carries energy as electromagnetic waves at a rate $\varepsilon \sigma A T^4$, and it works across a vacuum.
>
>Conduction and radiation each come with one law and one material constant, and they carry the problems in this section. Convection is described in words only.

- [[2.6.1 Thermal Conduction]]: Fourier's law, the table of conductivities, the steady-state line, and slabs in series. **Not taught:** surface versus air temperatures and the survey of conductors and insulators.
- [[2.6.2 Convection]]: natural and forced convection, why there is no formula, and why trapped air insulates. **Not taught:** the whole note.
- [[2.6.3 Thermal Radiation]]: the Stefan-Boltzmann law, emissivity as absorptivity, absorbed sunlight, blackbodies, net exchange with the surroundings, and heating over time. **Not taught:** the tilt factor, the rate equation $mc\,dT/dt = P_{\text{in}} - P_{\text{out}}$, and the steady temperature of a sunlit plate.

# Recommended Path

Read [[2.6.1 Thermal Conduction]] first and work the composite-wall example, which is the standard problem type. Then read [[2.6.3 Thermal Radiation]], paying attention to the argument that emissivity and absorptivity are the same number. Read [[2.6.2 Convection]] last, or skip it if time is short.
