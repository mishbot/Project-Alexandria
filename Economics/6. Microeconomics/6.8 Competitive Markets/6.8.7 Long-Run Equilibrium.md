>[!note] Long-Run Equilibrium
>In the **short run** the number of firms $J$ is fixed, and firms can earn positive profits ([[6.8.2 Short-Run Market Equilibrium]]). In the **long run** there is **free entry**: new firms with the same cost function $c(y)$ enter as long as profits are positive.
>- Entry of one firm from period $t - 1$ to period $t$ adds its supply function to industry supply:
>$$Y_t^S(p, w, r) = Y_{t-1}^S(p, w, r) + y(p, w, r).$$
>- For $p$ above min AC, $y(p, w, r) > 0$, so industry supply shifts right and flattens. For $p$ below min AC, the entrant produces nothing and industry supply is unchanged.
>- Entry continues until $\pi = 0$, that is, until $p = AC$. Each firm also maximizes profit, so $p = c'_y(y)$. Together these give $c'_y(y) = c(y)/y$, which holds where AC is at its minimum:
>$$\frac{d}{dy}\left(\frac{c(y)}{y}\right) = \frac{c'_y(y) - c(y)/y}{y} = 0.$$
>- **Long-run equilibrium.** The price is $p^* = \min_y AC(y)$. Each firm produces the output $\hat y$ that minimizes AC, called the **efficient scale**. The number of firms is $J^* = X^D(p^*, M)/\hat y$.
>- In a **constant-cost industry**, each firm's cost function does not depend on the number of firms. The long-run supply curve is then horizontal at $p = \min AC$.
>- After a rise in demand, the price first rises along the short-run supply curve and incumbents earn profits. Entry then shifts supply right until the price is back at min AC. In the long run a demand shift changes the number of firms only. The price and the size of each firm stay the same.
>- Profit, and so producer surplus, is zero in long-run equilibrium. In a constant-cost industry, consumers receive all the surplus and bear a per-unit tax in full, the flexible-supply case of [[6.8.4 Tax Incidence]].
>- The result needs an AC curve with its minimum at a positive output, such as a setup cost combined with rising MC ([[6.7.4 Cost Curves]]).

# Intuition

When the firms in a market earn profits, outsiders with the same technology want a share. A cook who sees food trucks clearing $\$125$ a day buys a truck too. Each new truck adds its supply curve to the market, so more food is offered at every price. The price falls, and every truck's profit shrinks. Entry stops only when profit reaches zero. A firm that maximizes profit produces where $p = MC$. A firm that earns zero profit has $p = AC$. Both hold at once only at the bottom of the AC curve, where MC crosses AC. So in the long run the price equals min AC, and each firm produces at its efficient scale. The figure uses the trucks of the Easy example, with $c(y) = 100 + y^2$ for $y > 0$ and demand $X^D = 200 - 5p$.

# Properties

#### *(Why Minimum AC)* Zero profit requires $p = AC$, profit maximization requires $p = MC$, and the two curves meet only at min AC.

Where $MC < AC$, an extra unit costs less than the average, so AC falls. Where $MC > AC$, AC rises. So $MC = AC$ exactly where AC is flat, at its minimum. Profit maximization also needs MC to be rising at the chosen output, and at the bottom of a U-shaped AC it is. If the price were above min AC, every firm would earn $y(p - AC) > 0$ and more firms would enter. A price below min AC cannot last either. Every firm would make a loss, so firms would exit until the price rose back to min AC.

>[!warning] Lecture Wording: Two Shortcuts on the Slides
>The slides write the cost function as $C(y)$ and typeset the minimum condition as "if $C'(y) = C(y)/y$, then $\frac{\partial}{\partial}\frac{C(y)}{y} = 0$", with the $y$ of $\partial y$ merged into the fraction. The derivative that is zero is the derivative of average cost, $\frac{d}{dy}\big(C(y)/y\big)$, not of $C(y)$. The slides also say supply rises "for $p$ above AC" and stays the same "for $p$ below AC". Both mean min AC, the price at which a firm starts producing. The slides discuss only entry. Exit when profits are negative, used above, follows the same logic.

#### *(Entry Flattens Supply)* Each entrant adds both quantity and slope to industry supply, so the price falls step by step toward min AC.

Differentiating $Y_t^S = Y_{t-1}^S + y$ gives $\partial Y_t^S/\partial p = \partial Y_{t-1}^S/\partial p + \partial y/\partial p$. Above min AC, each new firm adds $y > 0$ units and a slope $\partial y/\partial p > 0$, so the industry supply curve moves right and becomes flatter. The animation uses the Easy example. With $J$ trucks, supply is $Jp/2$ and the price is $400/(J + 10)$. It falls from $28.6$ with $4$ trucks to $25$, $22.2$, and finally $20$ with $10$ trucks, where profit is zero. Profit per truck falls from $104$ to $56$, $23$, and $0$.

#### *(Constant-Cost Industry)* When entry does not change any firm's costs, demand shifts move quantity and the number of firms but not the long-run price.

Every entrant faces the same $c(y)$, so it produces $\hat y$ at $p = \min AC$, whatever the number of firms. The industry can supply any quantity at that price by adding firms, so long-run supply is flat. A demand increase moves the market from $A$ to $B$ along the short-run supply curve, and incumbents earn profits. Entry then moves supply right until the market reaches $C$, back at min AC. Each firm ends at the same size, and only $J$ grows. The figure shows the Medium example.

#### *(Meaning of Zero Profit)* Zero long-run profit means the owners earn exactly what their inputs would earn elsewhere.

The cost function charges every input at its market price, including the rental rate $r$ on capital and a wage for the owner's own time. Zero profit therefore leaves owners with their [[6.5.1 Opportunity Cost|opportunity cost]], and no extra. With profit at zero, producer surplus in the sense of [[6.8.5 Consumer and Producer Surplus]] is zero too, and the flat long-run supply curve leaves no area above it. Any tax per unit is passed fully to consumers in the long run. Its deadweight loss is the triangle between demand and the flat supply line, as in the elastic-supply case of [[6.8.6 Welfare Applications]].

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The cases below, where the long-run result changes, were not covered.

#### *(When Free Entry Fails)* The result $p = \min AC$ needs a U-shaped AC, many firms, and input prices that do not rise with entry.

Under constant returns to scale, AC is flat, so the price equals AC but firm size is not pinned down. Under decreasing returns with no setup cost, AC is lowest as $y \to 0$, so free entry leads to many tiny firms. Under increasing returns, AC falls forever, and one firm can serve the market at the lowest cost, which leads to [[6.9.1 Monopoly]]. If $X^D(\min AC)/\hat y$ is not a whole number, the last entrant would push the price below min AC, so incumbents keep a small profit. If entry raises an input price, such as the rent of good locations, min AC rises with $J$ and long-run supply slopes up (an **increasing-cost industry**).

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Food Trucks
>Each food truck has cost $c(y) = 100 + y^2$ for $y > 0$ and $c(0) = 0$, where $100$ is a daily setup cost (generator, fuel, and parking) paid only on days the truck operates. Daily demand for meals is $X^D = 200 - 5p$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the long-run price, the output of each truck, and the number of trucks. Check that profit is zero.
>>$AC = 100/y + y$ and $MC = 2y$. Setting $MC = AC$ gives $y = 100/y$, so $\hat y = 10$ and $\min AC = 20$. The long-run price is $p^* = 20$. Demand at that price is $200 - 100 = 100$, so $J^* = 100/10 = 10$ trucks. Each truck earns $20 \cdot 10 - (100 + 100) = 0$. With fewer trucks, say $J = 8$, supply $8 \cdot p/2 = 4p$ meets demand at $p = 22.2$, and each truck earns $22.2^2/4 - 100 = 23.5$, which draws in more trucks.

>[!example] (Medium) A Festival Raises Demand
>A festival moves to town, and demand for meals rises to $X^D = 300 - 5p$. Trucks are as in the Easy example, and $10$ trucks are already operating.
>
>>[!example]- Find the short-run equilibrium before any truck enters, and the new long-run equilibrium. How does consumer surplus change?
>>In the short run supply is $10 \cdot p/2 = 5p$, and $5p = 300 - 5p$ gives $p = 30$. Each truck sells $15$ meals at $AC = 100/15 + 15 = 21.67$ and earns $15 \cdot (30 - 21.67) = 125$, the same as in [[6.7.5 Profit Maximization and Supply]]. These profits draw in entrants. In the long run the price returns to $20$, each truck sells $10$, and demand is $300 - 100 = 200$, so $J^* = 20$. Ten trucks enter.
>>
>>The choke price of the new demand is $60$. Consumer surplus is $\frac12 \cdot 150 \cdot (60 - 30) = 2{,}250$ in the short run and $\frac12 \cdot 200 \cdot (60 - 20) = 4{,}000$ in the long run. As trucks enter, the incumbents' short-run profits go to consumers as a lower price.

>[!example] (Hard) A Meal Tax Versus a License Fee
>Demand is $X^D = 300 - 5p$ and the long run has $20$ trucks, as in the Medium example. The city considers two policies. (a) A tax of $\$4$ per meal. (b) A new license fee of $\$44$ per truck for each day it operates.
>
>>[!example]- For each, find the long-run consumer price, output per truck, number of trucks, and city revenue. Which policy raises more money?
>>(a) The tax does not change $c(y)$, so trucks still need a producer price of $20$ and produce $10$ meals each. Consumers pay $24$, demand is $300 - 120 = 180$, and $J = 18$. Revenue is $4 \cdot 180 = 720$. Consumers bear the whole tax. Their surplus falls from $4{,}000$ to $\frac12 \cdot 180 \cdot 36 = 3{,}240$, a loss of $760$, which is the revenue plus a DWL of $\frac12 \cdot 4 \cdot 20 = 40$.
>>
>>(b) The fee raises the fixed cost to $144$, so $AC = 144/y + y$, minimized at $\hat y = 12$ with $\min AC = 24$. Consumers again pay $24$ and buy $180$ meals, but from $180/12 = 15$ larger trucks. Revenue is $15 \cdot 44 = 660$. Consumers face the same price in both cases, and the tax raises $60$ more. The fee pushes trucks to $12$ meals. There the real cost per meal, setup plus variable cost without the fee, is $(100 + 12^2)/12 = 20.33$ instead of $20$. On $180$ meals that wastes $60$. A per-unit tax leaves the efficient scale unchanged. A higher fixed cost changes it.
