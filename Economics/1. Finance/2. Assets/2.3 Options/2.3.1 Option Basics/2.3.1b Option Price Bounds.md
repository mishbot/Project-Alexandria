>[!note] Option Price Bounds
>**Option price bounds** are limits on option prices that follow from no-arbitrage alone, with no model of how the stock moves. Let $c$ and $p$ be the prices of a European call and put, and $C$ and $P$ the prices of the American call and put, all with strike $K$ and expiry $T$. Let $S$ be the stock price today and $r > 0$ the continuously compounded risk-free rate. For a stock that pays no dividends before $T$, the bounds are
>$$\max(S - Ke^{-rT}, 0) \le c \le C \le S, \qquad \max(Ke^{-rT} - S, 0) \le p \le P \le K.$$
>The first chain is for calls and the second for puts. Each lower bound is the [[2.3.1a Option Contract|intrinsic value]]. A call is never worth more than the share it can buy. A put is never worth more than the strike it can pay, and the European put has the tighter bound $p \le Ke^{-rT}$. American options can be exercised at once, so they also satisfy
>$$C \ge \max(S - K, 0), \qquad P \ge \max(K - S, 0).$$
>For the call this bound is weaker than $C \ge c \ge S - Ke^{-rT}$. For the put it is stronger than the European bound. An American option is worth at least the matching European one, and its value rises with $T$. Calls lose value as $K$ rises, and puts gain. All prices are at least zero. The bounds assume a frictionless market: no transaction costs, free short selling, and borrowing and lending at the same rate $r$. [[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity]] sharpens them for a call and a put together. A pricing model such as the [[2.3.3c Binomial Tree|binomial tree]] picks one price inside them.

# Intuition

A forward has a single no-arbitrage price, because a share and a bond copy its straight-line payoff. An option payoff bends at $K$, and no fixed mix of share and bond copies it. Its price depends on how likely the stock is to end in the money. That needs a view of volatility, which arbitrage alone does not supply. Arbitrage still rules out some prices. A call can never cost more than the share, since the share pays at least as much in every state. A call can never cost less than a share bought partly with a loan that repays $K$ at $T$. The call pays the same when the share ends above $K$, and its holder can decline to buy when the share ends below.

# Properties

#### *(Six Factors)* Six quantities set an option price, and volatility is the one a forward price does not need.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules list the six factors and give the effect of $K$, and of $T$ on American options. The rows for $\sigma$, $r$, and dividends, and the European $T$ entries, are standard results beyond the modules.

The six factors are the stock price $S$, the strike $K$, the time to expiry $T$, the rate $r$, the dividends, and the volatility $\sigma$ of the stock's return. The first five already set the value of a [[2.2.2a Forward Contract|forward contract]]. Volatility matters only for the option, because the option payoff is not linear in $S_T$ ([[2.2.1 Contingent Claim]]). The table gives the effect of raising one factor while the others stay fixed.

| Raise | $c$ | $p$ | $C$ | $P$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $S$ | up | down | up | down |
| $K$ | down | up | down | up |
| $T$ | mixed | mixed | up | up |
| $\sigma$ | up | up | up | up |
| $r$ | up | down | up | down |
| dividends | down | up | down | up |

On a stock with no dividends, $c$ rises with $T$ because $c = C$ ([[2.3.1c Early Exercise]]). A European put can lose value as $T$ grows. A deep in-the-money put pays close to $K$, and a later expiry delays that payment.

#### *(Optionality)* An American option carries every right of the matching European option, so it can never be worth less.

The holder of an American option can always wait until $T$ and act as a European holder. So the American price is at least the European price, $C \ge c$ and $P \ge p$. If not, buy the American option, write the European one, and hold both to expiry. An American option with a later expiry also carries every right of one with an earlier expiry. So its value rises with $T$. The argument needs the right to exercise early, so it does not carry over to European options.

#### *(Strike Order)* A call with a lower strike is worth at least as much as a call with a higher strike, and the reverse holds for puts.

Take strikes $K_1 < K_2$ with the same expiry. In every state, $\max(S_T - K_1, 0) \ge \max(S_T - K_2, 0)$. If the $K_1$ call costs less than the $K_2$ call, buy the $K_1$ call and write the $K_2$ call. The trade collects cash today, and the long call always pays at least what the short call owes. The trade also works with American calls. If the short call is exercised early, the trader exercises the long call at once. The trader pays $K_1$ for the share, delivers it, and receives $K_2 > K_1$. Puts reverse the order, since $\max(K_2 - S_T, 0) \ge \max(K_1 - S_T, 0)$.

#### *(American Bounds)* The immediate-exercise bound is the tighter one for a put, while for a call exercise pays less than the call is worth.

An American option that traded below $\max(S - K, 0)$ could be bought and exercised at once for a sure profit. For the put, $K - S$ exceeds the European bound $Ke^{-rT} - S$ by $K(1 - e^{-rT})$, the interest on the strike. Its upper bound is $K$ instead of $Ke^{-rT}$, because the holder can receive $K$ today. For the call, the chain $C \ge c \ge S - Ke^{-rT}$ gives more than $S - K$ when $r > 0$. A live American call is therefore worth more than the $S - K$ that exercise would pay. This is the reason an American call on a stock with no dividends is never exercised early, as [[2.3.1c Early Exercise]] shows.

#### *(Known Dividends)* Known cash dividends lower the call's lower bound and raise the put's, each by the present value of the dividends.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules derive the bounds for a stock with no dividends only. The dividend versions below follow from the same arguments.

Let $\text{PV}(D)$ be the present value of the cash dividends paid before $T$. The European bounds become
$$c \ge S - \text{PV}(D) - Ke^{-rT}, \qquad p \ge \text{PV}(D) + Ke^{-rT} - S.$$
The option holder does not receive the dividends, while the share in each arbitrage argument does. In the call argument, the short seller must pay the dividends to the share's lender. In the put argument, the long share collects them. Either way, $S - \text{PV}(D)$ replaces $S$. The immediate-exercise bounds $C \ge S - K$ and $P \ge K - S$ do not change. So on a dividend stock, $S - K$ can exceed the European call bound, and early exercise of a call can pay.

#### *(Strike Convexity)* For three equally spaced strikes, the middle call costs at most the average of the outer two.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>Strike convexity is not in the lecture notes. The homework covers it.

Take strikes $K_1 < K_2 < K_3$ with $K_3 - K_2 = K_2 - K_1$ and the same expiry. The call prices satisfy
$$c_2 \le \frac{c_1 + c_3}{2},$$
where $c_i$ is the price of the call with strike $K_i$. A long call at $K_1$, a long call at $K_3$, and two short calls at $K_2$ form a [[2.3.2e Butterfly Spread|butterfly spread]]. Its payoff is never negative, so its cost $c_1 + c_3 - 2c_2$ cannot be negative either. Plotted against $K$, the call price curve bends upward. The same argument with puts shows that put prices are convex in $K$ too. A quote that breaks convexity can pass every single-option bound, as the (Hard) example shows.

>[!example]- Proof of strike convexity
>Hold one call at $K_1$, one call at $K_3$, and write two calls at $K_2$. Equal spacing gives $2K_2 = K_1 + K_3$. The payoff at $T$ in each range of $S_T$ is
>
>| $S_T$ | Payoff |
>|---|---|
>| $S_T \le K_1$ | $0$ |
>| $K_1 < S_T \le K_2$ | $S_T - K_1 \ge 0$ |
>| $K_2 < S_T \le K_3$ | $(S_T - K_1) - 2(S_T - K_2) = K_3 - S_T \ge 0$ |
>| $S_T > K_3$ | $(S_T - K_1) + (S_T - K_3) - 2(S_T - K_2) = 0$ |
>
>The payoff is never negative. With no arbitrage, the portfolio cannot have a negative cost today. So $c_1 + c_3 - 2c_2 \ge 0$, which rearranges to $c_2 \le (c_1 + c_3)/2$.

# Derivation

We want to prove the two chains in the callout for a stock with no dividends.

An option never forces its holder to pay, so its payoff is never negative. A negative price would pay a buyer to take a free option, so $c, p, C, P \ge 0$.

For the upper bounds, suppose $c > S$ or $C > S$. Write the call and buy one share with the premium, keeping the difference. Whenever the call is exercised, deliver the share. If it is never exercised, keep the share. So $c \le C \le S$. Suppose $p > Ke^{-rT}$. Write the put and invest the premium at $r$. At $T$ it has grown to more than $K$, enough to pay for any exercise. So $p \le Ke^{-rT}$. For the American put, a premium above $K$ covers an exercise at any date, so $P \le K$.

For the call lower bound, suppose $c < S - Ke^{-rT}$. Sell one share short, buy the call, and deposit the rest, $A = S - c$, at rate $r$. The trade costs nothing today, and $A > Ke^{-rT}$, so the deposit grows to $Ae^{rT} > K$ by $T$. If $S_T > K$, exercise the call and use the share to close the short sale. If $S_T \le K$, let the call expire and buy back the share for $S_T$.

| Position | $S_T > K$ | $S_T \le K$ |
|---|---|---|
| Long call | $S_T - K$ | $0$ |
| Short share | $-S_T$ | $-S_T$ |
| Deposit | $Ae^{rT}$ | $Ae^{rT}$ |
| Total | $Ae^{rT} - K > 0$ | $Ae^{rT} - S_T > 0$ |

The total is positive in both states, which is an [[4.2.2a Arbitrage|arbitrage]]. With $c \ge 0$, this gives $c \ge \max(S - Ke^{-rT}, 0)$.

For the put lower bound, suppose $p < Ke^{-rT} - S$. Borrow $B = S + p$ at rate $r$, and buy one share and the put. Then $B < Ke^{-rT}$, so the debt at $T$ is $Be^{rT} < K$. If $S_T \le K$, exercise the put, deliver the share, and receive $K$. If $S_T > K$, let the put expire and sell the share for $S_T$.

| Position | $S_T > K$ | $S_T \le K$ |
|---|---|---|
| Long put | $0$ | $K - S_T$ |
| Long share | $S_T$ | $S_T$ |
| Debt | $-Be^{rT}$ | $-Be^{rT}$ |
| Total | $S_T - Be^{rT} > 0$ | $K - Be^{rT} > 0$ |

Again the total is positive in both states, so $p \ge \max(Ke^{-rT} - S, 0)$. The optionality argument gives $C \ge c$ and $P \ge p$, and the two chains in the callout follow.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Checking Four Quotes
>A stock trades at $\$60$ and pays no dividends. The risk-free rate is $5\%$ per year with continuous compounding. Four six-month options are quoted: (a) a European call with strike $\$50$ at $\$11.80$, (b) an American call with strike $\$55$ at $\$6.20$, (c) a European put with strike $\$70$ at $\$9.10$, and (d) an American put with strike $\$70$ at $\$9.60$.
>
>>[!example]- Check each quote against the bounds, and give the trade for any quote that fails.
>>The discount factor is $e^{-0.05 \times 0.5} = e^{-0.025}$. The discounted strikes are $50e^{-0.025} = \$48.77$, $55e^{-0.025} = \$53.64$, and $70e^{-0.025} = \$68.27$.
>>
>>(a) The bounds are $60 - 48.77 = 11.23 \le c \le 60$. The quote $\$11.80$ passes.
>>
>>(b) The immediate-exercise bound is $C \ge 60 - 55 = \$5$, which the quote passes. But $C \ge c \ge 60 - 53.64 = \$6.36$, and the quote $\$6.20$ fails. Short one share for $\$60$, buy the call for $\$6.20$, and deposit $\$53.80$. At expiry the deposit is $53.80e^{0.025} = \$55.16$. That covers the strike if the call is exercised, or the buyback if $S_T \le 55$. At least $\$0.16$ is left over.
>>
>>(c) The bounds are $68.27 - 60 = 8.27 \le p \le 68.27$. The quote $\$9.10$ passes, even though it is below $K - S = \$10$. A European put cannot be exercised today.
>>
>>(d) The American bound is $P \ge 70 - 60 = \$10$, and the quote $\$9.60$ fails. Buy the put and one share for $\$69.60$ in total, and exercise at once to receive $\$70$. The profit is $\$0.40$ per share with no risk.

>[!example] (Medium) A Put Below Its Lower Bound
>A stock trades at $\$36$ and pays no dividends. A three-month European put with strike $\$40$ is quoted at $\$3.10$. The risk-free rate is $5\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A trader can deal in 10 contracts of 100 shares each.
>
>>[!example]- Show that the put is too cheap, build the arbitrage, and find the cash left at expiry in each state.
>>The lower bound is $Ke^{-rT} - S = 40e^{-0.0125} - 36 = 39.50 - 36 = \$3.50$. The quote is $\$0.40$ below it.
>>
>>Per share, borrow $S + p = 36 + 3.10 = \$39.10$ for three months, and buy one share and one put. The debt at expiry is $39.10e^{0.0125} = \$39.59$.
>>
>>| $S_T$ | Put | Share | Debt | Net |
>>|---|---|---|---|---|
>>| $30$ | $10.00$ | $30.00$ | $-39.59$ | $0.41$ |
>>| $38$ | $2.00$ | $38.00$ | $-39.59$ | $0.41$ |
>>| $40$ | $0.00$ | $40.00$ | $-39.59$ | $0.41$ |
>>| $44$ | $0.00$ | $44.00$ | $-39.59$ | $4.41$ |
>>| $50$ | $0.00$ | $50.00$ | $-39.59$ | $10.41$ |
>>
>>At or below the strike, the put and the share together are worth exactly $\$40$, so the trade nets $40 - 39.59 = \$0.41$. Above the strike, the share alone is worth more than $\$40$, so the trade nets more. The sure part, $\$0.41$, is the price gap $\$0.40$ grown at $r$ for three months. With 1,000 shares, the trade locks in at least $1{,}000 \times 0.4082 = \$408.18$ at expiry.

>[!example] (Hard) A Butterfly From Three Calls
>A stock trades at $\$50$ and pays no dividends. The risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding. Six-month European calls are quoted at $\$7.05$ for strike $\$45$, $\$4.70$ for strike $\$50$, and $\$2.05$ for strike $\$55$.
>
>>[!example]- Show that each quote passes the single-option bounds and the strike order, find the arbitrage that remains, and give its cash flows for 10 butterflies of 100 shares each.
>>The discount factor is $e^{-0.02}$. The lower bounds are $50 - 45e^{-0.02} = \$5.89$, $50 - 50e^{-0.02} = \$0.99$, and $0$. Each quote lies above its bound and below $\$50$. The prices also fall as the strike rises. No single-option test fails.
>>
>>Convexity fails. The average of the outer calls is $(7.05 + 2.05)/2 = \$4.55$, below the $\$4.70$ quote for the middle call. Buy one $\$45$ call, buy one $\$55$ call, and write two $\$50$ calls. The cash today is
>>$$-7.05 - 2.05 + 2 \times 4.70 = +\$0.30$$
>>per butterfly. The payoff at expiry is never negative.
>>
>>| $S_T$ | $\le 45$ | $47$ | $50$ | $53$ | $\ge 55$ |
>>|---|---|---|---|---|---|
>>| Payoff per share | $0$ | $2$ | $5$ | $2$ | $0$ |
>>
>>Ten butterflies cover 1,000 shares. They bring in $1{,}000 \times 0.30 = \$300$ today. At expiry they pay between $\$0$ and $\$5{,}000$, the maximum at $S_T = \$50$. The trader keeps the $\$300$ in every state. The quotes stop allowing this trade once the middle call costs at most $\$4.55$.
