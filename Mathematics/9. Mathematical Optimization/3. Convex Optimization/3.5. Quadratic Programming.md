> [!note] Quadratic Program
> A **Quadratic Program (QP)** is a [[3.1. Convex Optimization|convex optimization problem]] with a **convex quadratic objective** and **affine constraints**. Its standard matrix form is
>$$\begin{aligned}
> \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^n} \quad & x^\top P_0 x + q_0^\top x + r_0 \\
> \text{s.t.} \quad & A x = b, \\
> & C x \le d,
> \end{aligned}$$
> where $P_0 \in \mathbb{S}^n$ is a symmetric matrix, $q_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$, and $r_0 \in \mathbb{R}$. Convexity of the problem requires $P_0 \succeq 0$ (i.e., $P_0$ is PSD), since the Hessian of the objective is $2P_0$. When $P_0 = 0$, the quadratic objective reduces to a linear one, and the problem becomes a [[3.2. Linear Programming|linear program]], showing that
>$$\text{LP} \subset \text{QP} \subset \text{Convex Optimization}$$
>
> Because all constraints in a QP are affine, the **feasible set is identical in structure to that of an LP**: it is the intersection of hyperplanes and half-spaces, forming a **polyhedron** (or a **polytope** if bounded). Thus, geometrically, QPs optimize over the same feasible regions as LPs, but with a curved objective function instead of a flat one.   
> 
> Unlike LPs, where optimal solutions always occur at vertices of the feasible set, a QP’s optimal solution may lie **strictly inside the feasible region**, since the quadratic objective can have a unique minimizer in the interior. In particular, if $P_0 \succ 0$, the objective is **strictly convex**, guaranteeing a **unique global minimizer**.
> 
> Although QPs remain convex and tractable, they are generally **harder to solve than LPs**, because evaluating and minimizing a quadratic objective is more computationally expensive than a linear one. 
> 
> Nevertheless, many important problems–such as least-squares regression, regularized optimization, and portfolio optimization–naturally take the form of QPs, highlighting their role as the most immediate and practically important extension of Linear Programming.

# Examples
>[!example] Epigraph Technique for QPs
>For each $f_{i}$, formulate the optimization problem in quadratic form. If such formulation is not possible, explain why.
>>[!example]- $f_{1}(x) = \|Ax-y\|_{2}^{2}+\|x\|_{1}$ (LASSO)
>>This is equivalent to an L1-regularized least squares with $\lambda=1$. We already know from previous notes that for the regularizer term we can introduce a slack variable $u \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$, such that
>>$$-u_{i} \le x_{i} \le u_{i} \qquad \text{for } i=1,\ldots,n.$$
>>Since $\|Ax-y\|_{2}^{2}$ is quadratic, we simply refactor the norm as $(Ax-y)^T(Ax-y)=x^TA^TAx-2y^TAx+y^Ty$. As such, our objective becomes
>>$$f_{1}(x)=x^TA^TAx-2y^TAx+y^Ty+1^Tu,$$
>>subject to the constraint above.
>
>>[!example]- $f_{2}(x) = \|Ax-y\|_{2}^{2}-\|x\|_{1}$
>>Since $\|x\|_{1}$ is convex, it follows that $-\|x\|_{1}$ is concave. The difference of two convex functions (or equivalently the addition of convex and concave) is generally non-convex. As such, the objective function is not convex, and the problem cannot be formulated as a QP.
>
>>[!example]- $f_{3}(x) = \|Ax-y\|_{2}^{2}+\|x\|_{1}^{2}$
>We already know that $\|Ax-y\|_{2}^{2}=x^TA^TAx - 2y^TAx + y^Ty$. Recall that L1 norm is the sum of absolute values. From earlier, we know that it can be expressed using $1^Tz$, where $z \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is a slack variable, such that
>$$-z_{i} \le x_{i} \le z_{i} \qquad \text{for } i = 1,\ldots,n.$$
>Therefore, L1 norm squared is simply $(1^Tz)^2$! Our final objective function becomes
>$$f_{3}(x)=x^TA^TAx - 2y^TAx + y^Ty+(1^Tz)^2.$$
>To go one step further, let $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, such that $Q=11^T$ (i.e., a matrix full of ones). Then, we can express the objective as
>$$f_{3}(x)=x^TA^TAx - 2y^TAx + y^Ty+z^TQz.$$
