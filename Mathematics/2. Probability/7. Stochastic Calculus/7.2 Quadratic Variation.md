>[!note] Quadratic Variation
>The **quadratic variation** of a path over a time interval is the total of its squared changes, measured over shorter and shorter steps. Split $[0, t]$ into $n$ steps of length $\Delta t = t/n$, at times $t_j = j\,\Delta t$. Let $\Delta W_j = W_{t_j} - W_{t_{j-1}}$ be the change of a [[7.1 Generalized Wiener Process|Wiener process]] over step $j$. The sum of squared changes, its mean, and its variance are
>$$Q_n = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (\Delta W_j)^2, \qquad \mathbb{E}[Q_n] = t, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Q_n) = \frac{2t^2}{n}.$$
>Each squared step has mean $\Delta t$ and variance $2(\Delta t)^2$. The variance of the sum goes to $0$, so $Q_n \to t$, and the quadratic variation of $W$ over $[0, t]$ is $t$. A smooth path, one with a continuous derivative, has quadratic variation $0$. A Wiener path has infinite total variation, meaning that the sum of absolute changes $\sum_j |\Delta W_j|$ grows without bound. It also has no derivative at any point. In the limit, products of small increments follow three multiplication rules:
>$$(dt)^2 = 0, \qquad dt\,dW_t = 0, \qquad (dW_t)^2 = dt.$$
>Only the square of $dW_t$ survives, and it is as large as $dt$. For an [[7.3 Ito Process|Itô process]] $dx = a\,dt + b\,dW_t$ the rules give $(dx)^2 = b^2\,dt$. So the quadratic variation of $x$ over $[0, t]$ is $\int_0^t b^2\,ds$, equal to $b^2t$ for constant $b$, and the drift $a$ has no effect. For this reason the sum of squared log returns of a stock over many short intervals, divided by the length of the period, estimates $\sigma^2$. This estimate is called realized variance. Quadratic variation produces the $-t/2$ in $\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s = \frac{1}{2}W_t^2 - \frac{1}{2}t$ ([[7.4 Ito Integral]]) and the extra term $\frac{1}{2}b^2\,\partial^2 G/\partial x^2$ in [[7.5 Ito's Lemma|Itô's lemma]].

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules show the mean $\Delta t$ and the variance $2(\Delta t)^2$ of one squared step. They also use the limit $\sum (\Delta W)^2 \to t$ inside the Itô integral example. They do not name quadratic variation. The variance of the sum, smooth paths, total variation, the multiplication rules, the quadratic variation of an Itô process, and realized variance go beyond the modules.

# Intuition

A trader wants to know how volatile a stock was over the past year. She takes the $250$ daily changes in its log price, squares each one, and adds them up. Squaring makes every move count, up or down, and gives large moves more weight. A natural worry is that the answer depends on the sampling. Hourly data have more terms, each of them smaller. The sum could grow, shrink, or settle as the sampling gets finer.

For a smooth path the sum shrinks to zero. Take the straight line $x(t) = t$ on $[0, 1]$. With $4$ steps each change is $0.25$, and the squares add to $4 \times 0.0625 = 0.25$. With $16$ steps they add to $16 \times (1/16)^2 = 0.0625$, and with $256$ steps to $0.0039$. Each change is about the slope times $\Delta t$, so its square is of order $(\Delta t)^2$. There are $1/\Delta t$ terms, so the sum is of order $\Delta t$ and goes to $0$.

A Wiener path behaves differently. Its change over a step has typical size $\sqrt{\Delta t}$, so its square is about $\Delta t$, and $n$ squares add to about $n\,\Delta t = t$. One simulated path on $[0, 1]$ gives sums of $1.436$, $1.032$, $1.012$, and $0.993$ on grids of $4$, $16$, $64$, and $256$ steps. The sums stay close to the elapsed time $t = 1$ as the grid gets finer.

The name says what is computed. "Quadratic" means that the changes are squared, and "variation" means that the movements of the path are added up. Each step above gives one piece of the result. Steps of size $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ make each square have mean $\Delta t$, so the sum has mean $t$. Adding many independent squares makes the variance $2t^2/n$ small, so the sum settles at $t$. The trader's sum settles in the same way, at $\sigma^2$ times the length of the period, and finer data make it more precise.

# Properties

#### *(One Squared Step)* A single squared step $(\Delta W)^2$ is random, with a standard deviation larger than its mean.

Write $\Delta W = \varepsilon\sqrt{\Delta t}$ with $\varepsilon$ standard normal. Then $(\Delta W)^2 = \varepsilon^2\Delta t$, with mean $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2]\,\Delta t = \Delta t$. A standard normal variable has fourth moment $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^4] = 3$, so the variance of one squared step is
$$\operatorname{Var}\big((\Delta W)^2\big) = \mathbb{E}\big[(\Delta W)^4\big] - \big(\mathbb{E}\big[(\Delta W)^2\big]\big)^2 = 3(\Delta t)^2 - (\Delta t)^2 = 2(\Delta t)^2.$$
Its standard deviation is $\sqrt{2}\,\Delta t$, about $1.4$ times the mean, on every grid. With daily steps, $\Delta t = 0.004$, a squared step has mean $0.004$ and standard deviation $0.0057$. So one squared step alone is far from $\Delta t$. The useful comparison is with the step itself. The step $\Delta W$ has standard deviation $\sqrt{\Delta t} = 0.0632$, more than ten times larger. Measured against $\Delta W$, the square $(\Delta W)^2$ is of order $\Delta t$ in both its mean and its spread. The heuristic derivation of [[7.5 Ito's Lemma|Itô's lemma]] uses this to replace $(\Delta W)^2$ by $\Delta t$.

#### *(How Fast the Sum Settles)* The error of $Q_n$ relative to $t$ is $\sqrt{2/n}$, whatever the length of the interval.

From the derivation below, $Q_n$ has mean $t$ and standard deviation $t\sqrt{2/n}$. So its error relative to $t$ depends only on the number of steps. With $250$ daily steps it is $8.9\%$, with $52$ weekly steps $19.6\%$, and with $12$ monthly steps $40.8\%$. Halving the error takes four times as many steps. The same percentages hold over a week or over a decade, because only $n$ enters. The convergence $Q_n \to t$ is a form of the [[3.2 Laws of Large Numbers|law of large numbers]], since $Q_n/t$ is the average of $n$ independent copies of $\varepsilon^2$.

#### *(Smooth Paths Versus Wiener Paths)* A path with a continuous derivative has quadratic variation $0$, so a Wiener path cannot have one.

Let $g$ have a continuous derivative $g'$ on $[0, t]$. Over a step, $\Delta g_j \approx g'(t_j)\,\Delta t$, so the sum of squared changes is
$$\sum_{j=1}^n (\Delta g_j)^2 \approx \Delta t\sum_{j=1}^n g'(t_j)^2\,\Delta t \approx \Delta t\int_0^t g'(s)^2\,ds.$$
The sum is $\Delta t$ times a fixed number, so it vanishes as the steps shrink. A Wiener path has quadratic variation $t - s > 0$ on every interval $[s, t]$, so it has no continuous derivative on any interval. Its slope over one step, $\Delta W/\Delta t$, has standard deviation $1/\sqrt{\Delta t}$. That is $15.8$ for daily steps, and it grows without bound as the steps shrink. The path also has infinite total variation. Since $\mathbb{E}|\varepsilon| = \sqrt{2/\pi}$, the sum of absolute changes has mean $n\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\sqrt{2/\pi} = \sqrt{2nt/\pi}$. Over one year this is $12.6$ with daily steps and $126.2$ with $100$ times as many steps. The distance a Wiener path travels is unbounded, while the sum of its squared steps settles at $t$.

#### *(Multiplication Rules)* In sums over many small steps, $(\Delta t)^2$ and $\Delta t\,\Delta W$ add up to zero, while $(\Delta W)^2$ adds up to the elapsed time.

Each rule records what a product of increments adds up to over $[0, t]$. The products $(\Delta t)^2$ add to $n(\Delta t)^2 = t\,\Delta t$, which goes to $0$. The products $\Delta t\,\Delta W_j$ add to exactly $\Delta t\,W_t$, which also goes to $0$. The squares $(\Delta W_j)^2$ add to $Q_n$, which goes to $t$. The table lists the three cases.

| Product | Sum over $[0, t]$ | Limit | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| $(\Delta t)^2$ | $t\,\Delta t$ | $0$ | $(dt)^2 = 0$ |
| $\Delta t\,\Delta W$ | $\Delta t\,W_t$ | $0$ | $dt\,dW_t = 0$ |
| $(\Delta W)^2$ | $Q_n$ | $t$ | $(dW_t)^2 = dt$ |

The rules matter when a function $G$ of an Itô process is expanded to second order. In the term $\frac{1}{2}\,(\partial^2 G/\partial x^2)\,(\Delta x)^2$ with $\Delta x = a\,\Delta t + b\,\Delta W$, only $b^2(\Delta W)^2$ survives, and it becomes $b^2\,dt$. This gives the extra drift $\frac{1}{2}b^2\,\partial^2 G/\partial x^2$ in [[7.5 Ito's Lemma|Itô's lemma]]. In ordinary calculus all second-order terms vanish. The same rule gives the $-t/2$ in $\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s$. The left-endpoint sum that defines that integral equals $\frac{1}{2}W_t^2 - \frac{1}{2}Q_n$ on every grid, and [[7.4 Ito Integral]] takes the limit.

#### *(Drift Drops Out)* The quadratic variation of $dx = a\,dt + b\,dW_t$ depends on $b$ alone, so short-horizon data estimate the volatility well and the drift badly.

Square one step: $(\Delta x)^2 = a^2(\Delta t)^2 + 2ab\,\Delta t\,\Delta W + b^2(\Delta W)^2$. By the multiplication rules only the last term survives in the sum. So the quadratic variation over $[0, T]$ is $\int_0^T b^2\,dt$, or $b^2T$ when $b$ is constant. Under [[7.6 Geometric Brownian Motion|geometric Brownian motion]] the log price of a stock has $b = \sigma$. So the sum of squared log returns over $n$ short intervals, divided by the length $T$ of the period, estimates $\sigma^2$:
$$\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=1}^n r_j^2.$$
Here $r_j$ is the log return over interval $j$. This annualized estimate is the **realized variance**. Its relative error is about $\sqrt{2/n}$, so more frequent sampling within the same period makes it more precise. The drift behaves the opposite way. The natural estimate of $a$ is $(x_T - x_0)/T$, whose standard deviation $b/\sqrt{T}$ shrinks only as the period gets longer. [[3.1f Historical Volatility]] uses the sample standard deviation of daily returns instead, which subtracts the mean return and divides by $n - 1$.

# Derivation

We want the mean and variance of $Q_n$ and its limit. Each step is $\Delta W_j = \varepsilon_j\sqrt{\Delta t}$, with independent standard normal draws $\varepsilon_j$ and $\Delta t = t/n$. So the sum of squares is
$$Q_n = \sum_{j=1}^n \varepsilon_j^2\,\Delta t = t\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n \varepsilon_j^2,$$
which is $t$ times an average of $n$ independent copies of $\varepsilon^2$. Since $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2] = 1$, the mean is $\mathbb{E}[Q_n] = t$. Since $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^4] = 3$, each copy has variance $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon^2) = 3 - 1 = 2$. The terms are independent, so their variances add, and
$$\operatorname{Var}(Q_n) = n \cdot 2(\Delta t)^2 = \frac{2t^2}{n}.$$
The variance falls in proportion to $1/n$. Chebyshev's inequality bounds the chance of an error of at least $c > 0$:
$$\mathbb{P}\big[\,|Q_n - t| \ge c\,\big] \le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(Q_n)}{c^2} = \frac{2t^2}{nc^2}.$$
The bound goes to $0$ as $n \to \infty$ for every fixed $c$. So $Q_n \to t$, and the quadratic variation of $W$ over $[0, t]$ is $t$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Squared Steps on Two Grids
>A Wiener path on $[0, 1]$ is sampled every quarter, at $t = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1$. Its four changes are $0.3$, $-0.7$, $0.4$, and $0.5$. So the sampled values are $W = 0, 0.3, -0.4, 0, 0.5$.
>
>>[!example]- Compute the sum of squared changes on the four-step grid and on the two-step grid $t = 0, 0.5, 1$. Do the same for the straight line from $0$ to $0.5$. Then compute the sum of absolute changes on both grids, for the path and for the line.
>>On four steps, $Q_4 = 0.09 + 0.49 + 0.16 + 0.25 = 0.99$. On two steps the changes are $-0.4$ and $0.9$, so $Q_2 = 0.16 + 0.81 = 0.97$. Both are close to $t = 1$. The straight line from $0$ to $0.5$ changes by $0.125$ per quarter. Its sum is $4 \times 0.015625 = 0.0625$ on four steps and $2 \times 0.0625 = 0.125$ on two steps. Each refinement halves it, and on $100$ steps it would be $0.0025$.
>>
>>The sum of absolute changes behaves the other way. For the path it is $0.3 + 0.7 + 0.4 + 0.5 = 1.9$ on four steps and $0.4 + 0.9 = 1.3$ on two steps. Halving the step raised it by almost half, while the sum of squares barely moved. For the line it is $0.5$ on both grids, the distance from $0$ to $0.5$. For a Wiener path the mean of this sum is $\sqrt{2nt/\pi}$, from the Smooth Paths Versus Wiener Paths property. That is $1.13$ on two steps and $1.60$ on four, and it grows without bound as $n$ grows.

>[!example] (Medium) Realized Variance From One Week
>The daily log returns of a stock over one week are $1.2\%$, $-0.8\%$, $1.5\%$, $-2.1\%$, and $0.4\%$. A year has $250$ trading days.
>
>>[!example]- Estimate the annual volatility from the realized variance. Compare it with the sample standard deviation used in [[3.1f Historical Volatility]]. Then find how many daily returns give a relative error of $10\%$ in the variance.
>>The squared returns are $0.000144$, $0.000064$, $0.000225$, $0.000441$, and $0.000016$. They add to $0.000890$. Five days are $T = 5/250 = 0.02$ years, so the realized variance and volatility are
>>$$\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{0.000890}{0.02} = 0.0445, \qquad \hat\sigma = 0.2110.$$
>>The estimated volatility is $21.10\%$ per year. The sample standard deviation subtracts the mean return, $0.04\%$, and divides by $n - 1 = 4$. The mean is tiny, so subtracting it lowers the sum of squares only to $0.000889$. Dividing by $4$ instead of $5$ gives a daily variance of $0.000222$, or $0.0556$ per year, a volatility of $23.57\%$. The gap between the two estimates comes almost entirely from the divisor. With five terms the relative error of the realized variance is $\sqrt{2/5} = 0.63$, so neither estimate is precise. A relative error of $10\%$ needs $\sqrt{2/n} = 0.1$, or $n = 200$ daily returns.

>[!example] (Hard) A Month of a Stock
>A stock trades at $\$100$ and follows geometric Brownian motion with $\mu = 0.12$ and $\sigma = 0.30$. By [[7.5 Ito's Lemma|Itô's lemma]], its log price follows $d(\ln S) = (\mu - \sigma^2/2)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t$. A risk manager watches the stock for one month of $20$ trading days, so $T = 20/250 = 0.08$ years and $\Delta t = 1/250$.
>
>>[!example]- Find the quadratic variation of $\ln S$ over the month. Find the standard deviation of the realized variance from the $20$ daily log returns, and a rough range for the estimated volatility. Compare with how well the same data estimate the drift. Then estimate the quadratic variation of the price itself.
>>The log price has diffusion coefficient $b = \sigma$, so its quadratic variation over the month is $\sigma^2T = 0.09 \times 0.08 = 0.0072$. The drift $\mu - \sigma^2/2 = 0.075$ does not enter. The standard deviation of the monthly log return is $\sqrt{0.0072} = 0.0849$.
>>
>>The sum of the $20$ squared daily returns has mean close to $0.0072$. The drift adds only $20 \times (0.075 \times 0.004)^2 = 0.0000018$ to it. Its standard deviation is $0.0072\sqrt{2/20} = 0.00228$. A two-standard-deviation range is $0.0072 \pm 0.00455$, or $0.00265$ to $0.01175$. Dividing by $T$ gives an annual variance between $0.0331$ and $0.1469$. So the estimated volatility lies roughly between $18.19\%$ and $38.33\%$, a wide range around the true $30\%$, though narrow enough to rule out $15\%$ or $45\%$.
>>
>>The same month says almost nothing about the drift. The estimate $\ln(S_T/S_0)/T$ has standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{T} = 0.30/\sqrt{0.08} = 1.0607$ per year. That is fourteen times the true drift of $0.075$.
>>
>>The price itself has diffusion coefficient $\sigma S$, so its quadratic variation is $\int_0^T \sigma^2S_t^2\,dt$, which depends on the path. If the price stays near $\$100$, it is about $\sigma^2 \times 100^2 \times T = 72$ squared dollars. That corresponds to a monthly dollar standard deviation of about $\$8.49$.
