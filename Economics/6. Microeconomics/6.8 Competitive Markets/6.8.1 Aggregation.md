>[!note] Aggregation
>**Aggregation** builds the market curves for one good $i$ from the choices of individual firms and consumers. All other prices $p_1, \dots, p_{i-1}, p_{i+1}, \dots, p_n$ and input prices $w, r$ stay fixed.
>- There are $J$ firms, $j \in \{1, \dots, J\}$. Firm $j$ has the supply function $y_i^{j*}(p_i, w, r)$ from [[6.7.5 Profit Maximization and Supply]]. **Industry supply** is the total output at each price:
>$$Y_i(p_i, w, r) = \sum_{j=1}^{J} y_i^{j*}(p_i, w, r).$$
>- There are $J$ consumers. The letter $J$ counts both firms and consumers here, but the two numbers need not be equal. Consumer $j$ has income $M^j$ and [[6.2.2 Utility Maximization|Marshallian demand]] $x_i^{j*}(p_1, \dots, p_n, M^j)$. **Market demand** is the total quantity bought at each price:
>$$X_i(p_1, \dots, p_n, M^1, \dots, M^J) = \sum_{j=1}^{J} x_i^{j*}(p_1, \dots, p_n, M^j).$$
>- Both sums are **horizontal**: at a given price, add the quantities. With $J$ identical agents, multiply the quantity by $J$. Five firms with $y = 5p$ give $Y = 25p$. Ten consumers with $x = 10 - 5p$ give $X = 100 - 50p$.
>- In inverse form, $J$ identical consumers with $p = a - bx$ give $p = a - (b/J)X$. The price intercept stays the same and the curve becomes $J$ times flatter.
>- The market curve has a kink at each price where a consumer starts buying or a firm starts producing. It jumps where a firm's supply jumps from $0$ to the output at min AC. Write it piecewise.
>- Market demand depends on the whole income distribution $(M^1, \dots, M^J)$. A transfer of $dM$ from consumer 1 to consumer 2 changes $X_i$ by $\big(\partial x_i^{2*}/\partial M - \partial x_i^{1*}/\partial M\big)\,dM$.
>- Slopes add: $\partial X_i/\partial p_i = \sum_j \partial x_i^{j*}/\partial p_i$. Each term is negative for a normal good and can have either sign for an inferior good. Industry supply slopes up because each firm's supply slopes up.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The slides state the two sums and that market demand depends on the income distribution, and leave the graphs to the board. The inverse-form rule, the piecewise formulas at kinks and jumps, and the transfer formula are extensions built from those definitions.

# Intuition

Every buyer and every seller in a market faces the same price. To find how much the market buys at a price $p$, ask each consumer how much she buys at $p$ and add the answers. Repeat for every $p$ and the result is the market demand curve. Industry supply works the same way with firms. These two curves are the inputs to [[6.8.2 Short-Run Market Equilibrium]].

In the diagram, 100 students each demand $8 - 2p$ and stop buying at $p = 4$. Fifty professionals each demand $12 - p$ and stop buying at $p = 12$. At $p = 2$ the students buy $400$ units and the professionals buy $500$, so the market buys $900$. Above $p = 4$ only professionals buy, so the market curve follows their curve. The market curve bends at $p = 4$, where the students enter.

# Properties

#### *(Why Sum Horizontally)* The market curve is a horizontal sum, because all agents share one price while their quantities differ.

Adding the curves vertically would add prices at a fixed quantity, which has no economic meaning. In formulas, the sum runs over the quantity functions $x_i^{j*}(p)$, never over the inverse functions $p^j(x)$. A common slip with $J$ identical consumers is to scale the intercept of $x = a - bp$ and forget the slope. Both terms scale, so $J(a - bp) = Ja - Jbp$. In the $(X, p)$ plane the market curve keeps each agent's price intercept and is $J$ times flatter. The same rule holds for firms. Each firm's supply slopes up because $\partial y^{j*}/\partial p = 1/c''_j > 0$, so the sum slopes up too.

#### *(Kinks and Jumps)* A single formula for the market curve is valid only between the prices where agents enter or leave.

A consumer with $x^j = a - bp$ buys $\max\{a - bp, 0\}$. Below her choke price $a/b$ she adds $-b$ to the market slope, and above it she adds nothing. Firms enter at the bottom of their supply curves. A firm with a setup cost has a U-shaped AC, and its supply jumps from $0$ to the output that minimizes AC. The industry supply curve then jumps too. In the figure, ten mills with $c(y) = y^2$ supply $5p$ at every price. Ten newer mills with $c(y) = 16 + y^2$ for $y > 0$ have min AC $= 8$ at $y = 4$. They supply $0$ below $p = 8$ and $p/2$ each above it. At $p = 8$ each new mill is indifferent between $0$ and $4$ units, so any total in $\{40, 44, \dots, 80\}$ can be supplied. The Hard example uses this.

#### *(Income Distribution Matters)* Moving income between consumers shifts market demand unless both spend the same part of an extra dollar on the good.

Total income can stay fixed while market demand changes. A transfer of $dM$ from consumer 1 to consumer 2 lowers her demand by $(\partial x_i^{1*}/\partial M)\,dM$ and raises his by $(\partial x_i^{2*}/\partial M)\,dM$. The net change is zero only when the two income derivatives are equal. With [[6.4a Cobb-Douglas]] demand $x^{j*} = \alpha_j M^j/p$, the derivative is $\alpha_j/p$. A transfer toward the consumer with the larger budget share $\alpha_j$ raises market demand at every price. A good can also be normal for one group and [[6.3.3 Income Changes|inferior]] for another. [[6.8.2 Short-Run Market Equilibrium]] works out the effect of such a transfer on the equilibrium price.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Five Firms and Ten Consumers
>Five identical firms each have the supply function $Y_j(p) = 5p$. Ten identical consumers each have the demand function $X_j(p) = 10 - 5p$. A classmate answers $AD = 100 - 5p$.
>
>>[!example]- Find aggregate supply $AY$ and aggregate demand $AD$. Find the mistake.
>>At any price, each firm supplies $5p$, so $AY = 5 \cdot 5p = 25p$. Each consumer buys $10 - 5p$, so $AD = 10\,(10 - 5p) = 100 - 50p$. The classmate scaled the intercept and kept the individual slope. At $p = 1$ each consumer buys $5$, so ten consumers buy $50$. The correct formula gives $100 - 50 = 50$, and the classmate's gives $95$. Both formulas hold only for $p \le 2$, since each consumer buys nothing at higher prices. In inverse form, each consumer has $p = 2 - x/5$ and the market has $p = 2 - X/50$, the same intercept with a slope ten times flatter.

>[!example] (Medium) A Kinked Market Demand
>A campus print shop sells to 100 students, each with demand $\max\{8 - 2p, 0\}$, and to 50 professionals, each with demand $\max\{12 - p, 0\}$.
>
>>[!example]- Write market demand. At what price does the market buy $300$ units, and at what price $1000$?
>>Students buy only when $p < 4$, and professionals only when $p < 12$. Market demand is
>>$$X(p) = \begin{cases} 1400 - 250p & \text{if } 0 \le p < 4, \\ 600 - 50p & \text{if } 4 \le p < 12, \\ 0 & \text{if } p \ge 12. \end{cases}$$
>>The two pieces agree at $p = 4$, where both give $400$. For $X = 300$, the low-price piece gives $p = 4.4$, which is not below $4$, so it is the wrong piece. The middle piece gives $600 - 50p = 300$, so $p = 6$, which lies in $[4, 12)$. For $X = 1000$, the low-price piece gives $1400 - 250p = 1000$, so $p = 1.6$. At that price the students buy $480$ and the professionals buy $520$. Using $1400 - 250p$ at every price would predict $-100$ units at $p = 6$.

>[!example] (Hard) Supply With Entering Mills
>The industry has the two groups of mills from the figure above. Ten old mills have $c(y) = y^2$. Ten new mills have $c(y) = 16 + y^2$ for $y > 0$ and $c(0) = 0$.
>
>>[!example]- Find industry supply at $p = 6$ and $p = 10$. Then find every way the industry can supply exactly $60$ units with each mill maximizing profit.
>>An old mill has $MC = 2y$ and $AC = y$, so it supplies $y = p/2$ at every $p > 0$. A new mill has $AC = 16/y + y$, which is smallest at $y = 4$ with $AC = 8$. It supplies $p/2$ for $p > 8$ and $0$ for $p < 8$. At $p = 6$ only old mills produce, $10 \times 3 = 30$ units. At $p = 10$ all mills produce $5$ each, $100$ units.
>>
>>For $60$ units, the branch $Y = 5p$ needs $p = 12$, but the formula holds only below $8$. The branch $Y = 10p$ needs $p = 6$, but it holds only above $8$. Neither branch works. At $p = 8$ the old mills supply $40$ and each new mill earns zero profit with either $0$ or $4$ units. Exactly $5$ new mills producing $4$ units each gives $40 + 20 = 60$. The price is $8$, and which $5$ mills produce does not matter. The old mills earn $8 \cdot 4 - 16 = 16$ each, while the producing new mills earn $0$. This zero-profit price is the logic of [[6.8.7 Long-Run Equilibrium]].
