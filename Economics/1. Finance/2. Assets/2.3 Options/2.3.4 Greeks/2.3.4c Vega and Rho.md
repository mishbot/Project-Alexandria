>[!note] Vega and Rho
>**Vega** and **rho** are two of the [[2.3.4 Greeks|Greeks]], the sensitivities of an option's value $f$ to its inputs. They cover the two market parameters of the [[2.3.3f Black-Scholes-Merton Formula|Black-Scholes-Merton formula]]. Vega is the derivative with respect to the volatility $\sigma$. Rho is the derivative with respect to the continuously compounded risk-free rate $r$. Both hold the stock price $S$, the time $t$, and the other inputs fixed:
>$$\mathcal{V} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \sigma}, \qquad \rho = \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}.$$
>The inputs $\sigma$ and $r$ enter the formula as decimals, so $\mathcal{V}$ is the change in value per $1.00$ change in $\sigma$. The change for a one-point move, say from $25\%$ to $26\%$, is $\mathcal{V}/100$. The same holds for $\rho$ and a one-point move in $r$.
>
>For European options on a stock with continuous dividend yield $q$, let $K$ be the strike and $\tau = T - t$ the time to expiry. Let $N$ be the standard normal CDF and $n(x) = e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ its density, with
>$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r - q + \sigma^2/2)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau}.$$
>A call and a put with the same strike and expiry have the same vega,
>$$\mathcal{V} = S\sqrt{\tau}\,e^{-q\tau}\,n(d_1) > 0.$$
>Rho has a different sign for the two options:
>$$\rho_{\text{call}} = K\tau e^{-r\tau}N(d_2) > 0, \qquad \rho_{\text{put}} = -K\tau e^{-r\tau}N(-d_2) < 0.$$
>The signs give the holder's gain or loss from each change.
>- A long call and a long put both gain when volatility rises. Buying an option is a purchase of volatility, and writing one is a sale.
>- A long call gains when the rate rises. The holder pays $K$ at $T$, and a higher rate lowers the present value of that payment.
>- A long put loses when the rate rises. The holder receives $K$ at $T$, and that receipt is worth less today.
>
>Vega is largest near the money and grows with $\tau$, so long-dated at-the-money options carry the most vega. Rho also grows with $\tau$, and a call's rho grows as the call moves into the money. The model treats $\sigma$ and $r$ as constants. Traders use vega and rho to measure what happens to the model price when the market changes those inputs. [[2.3.4b Gamma and Theta]] covers the sensitivities to $S$ and $t$.

>[!warning] Notation for Vega
>Vega is not a Greek letter and has no standard symbol. This note writes $\mathcal{V}$. The lecture writes the word "vega" in the formulas and $v$ in the profit-and-loss decomposition.

# Intuition

The Black-Scholes-Merton formula has two inputs that the market moves but the model holds fixed, $\sigma$ and $r$. A desk may hedge the delta and gamma of a written option. It still loses if the market's view of volatility rises, since the option it owes is then worth more. Vega measures that exposure. Volatility raises an option's value because the payoff is cut off at zero. More volatility makes large payoffs more likely, while the payoff can never fall below zero. Rho comes from the strike. The call holder pays $K$ at expiry and the put holder receives it, and the present value of $K$ depends on $r$.

# Properties

#### *(Size and Shape of Vega)* Near the money vega is about $0.4\,S\sqrt{\tau}\,e^{-q\tau}$, and it stays large over a band of width about $\sigma\sqrt{\tau}$ in log price.

At the money with a short time to expiry, $d_1$ is close to $0$ and $n(d_1) \approx 1/\sqrt{2\pi} \approx 0.4$. Vega is then about $0.4\,S\sqrt{\tau}\,e^{-q\tau}$, so quadrupling the maturity roughly doubles it. The approximation gets worse for long maturities, where $d_1$ moves away from $0$. As $S$ moves away from $K$, $n(d_1)$ falls. When $\ln(S/K)$ moves by $\sigma\sqrt{\tau}$, $d_1$ moves by $1$ and $n(d_1)$ drops to about $61\%$ of its peak. A short-dated option therefore has vega only in a narrow band of stock prices around $K$. A long-dated option has vega over a wide band. Raising $\sigma$ widens the band as well. At-the-money vega then barely changes, while vega away from the money rises. For a one-year at-the-money call with $r = 5.5\%$ and $q = 3\%$, vega stays between $35.9$ and $37.8$ as $\sigma$ runs from $10\%$ to $70\%$.

#### *(Vega and Gamma)* Vega and gamma share the factor $n(d_1)$, and they are linked by $\mathcal{V} = \sigma S^2\tau\,\Gamma$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules give the formulas for vega and gamma separately. The relation between them is an extension.

Dividing the vega formula by the gamma formula from [[2.3.4b Gamma and Theta]] gives $\mathcal{V}/\Gamma = S\sqrt{\tau} \cdot S\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = \sigma S^2\tau$. For the same gamma, a longer-dated option has more vega. For the same vega, a shorter-dated option has more gamma. Both Greeks measure the value of uncertainty about the stock. A long-gamma position earns money when the stock moves a lot from day to day. A long-vega position earns money when the market's estimate of future moves rises. So traders use short-dated options to trade the stock's daily moves and long-dated options to trade changes in volatility.

#### *(A Sensitivity Outside the Model)* The model assumes a constant $\sigma$, yet vega measures the effect of changing it.

In the derivation of the [[2.3.3e Black-Scholes-Merton Equation]], $\sigma$ never changes. Traders quote options through the implied volatility $\sigma_I$, the $\sigma$ that makes the formula match the market price ([[2.3.5a Implied Volatility]]). This number moves every day. A change $d\sigma_I$ moves the option price by about $\mathcal{V}\,d\sigma_I$, which [[2.3.5c Volatility Trading]] calls the vega P&L. Since $\mathcal{V} > 0$, the price rises strictly with $\sigma$. So each price between the no-arbitrage bounds matches exactly one volatility. Rho measures the same kind of exposure for $r$, which is also a constant in the model. A desk reports both exposures in dollars at the end of each day ([[2.3.4 Greeks]]).

#### *(Rho From the Bond Leg)* Rho is the time to expiry times the bond position in the replicating portfolio.

The call formula splits into a stock part $Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1)$ and a loan of $B = Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2)$. A change in $r$ moves $d_1$ and $d_2$, but those two effects cancel, as the derivation below shows. Only the discount factor responds, and $\partial e^{-r\tau}/\partial r = -\tau e^{-r\tau}$. A higher rate shrinks the present value of the loan the call holder owes, so $\rho_{\text{call}} = \tau B$ is positive. The put's replicating portfolio lends $Ke^{-r\tau}N(-d_2)$, which loses value when $r$ rises, so the put's rho is negative. Differentiating put-call parity in $r$ gives
$$\rho_{\text{call}} - \rho_{\text{put}} = K\tau e^{-r\tau}.$$
The right side is minus the rho of a zero-coupon bond that pays $K$ at $T$. A long call and a short put form a forward ([[2.3.1d Put-Call Parity]]), and the forward is short that bond.

With two years left, it ranges from $13.3$ at $S = 60$ to $149.8$ at $S = 140$. For a deep in-the-money call, $N(d_2) \to 1$ and rho approaches the bond's $K\tau e^{-r\tau}$, which is $181.0$ at two years. Raising $\sigma$ lowers $N(d_2)$ near the money, so an at-the-money call's rho falls. A far out-of-the-money call's rho rises with $\sigma$, because its risk-neutral probability of exercise, $N(d_2)$, rises.

#### *(Dividend Sensitivity)* A higher dividend yield lowers a call's value and raises a put's value, by $\tau$ times the stock part of each.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules define the dividend sensitivity $\partial f/\partial q$ but do not give its formulas.

The stock part of the call's replicating portfolio is $Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1)$. A change in $q$ moves $d_1$ and $d_2$, and those effects cancel as they do for rho. Only the factor $e^{-q\tau}$ responds. So the dividend sensitivities are
$$\frac{\partial c}{\partial q} = -\tau Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1), \qquad \frac{\partial p}{\partial q} = \tau Se^{-q\tau}N(-d_1).$$
A higher $q$ lowers the forward price $Se^{(r - q)\tau}$, which hurts the call and helps the put. The two formulas mirror rho, with the stock part in place of the bond part.

# Derivation

We derive vega and rho from the call formula $c = Se^{-q\tau}N(d_1) - Ke^{-r\tau}N(d_2)$. One identity removes most of the algebra. The two densities satisfy
$$Se^{-q\tau}\,n(d_1) = Ke^{-r\tau}\,n(d_2).$$
To check it, note that $n(d_2)/n(d_1) = e^{(d_1^2 - d_2^2)/2}$ and $(d_1^2 - d_2^2)/2 = \sigma\sqrt{\tau}\,d_1 - \sigma^2\tau/2 = \ln(S/K) + (r - q)\tau$.

For vega, differentiate $c$ in $\sigma$. Only $d_1$ and $d_2$ depend on $\sigma$, so
$$\frac{\partial c}{\partial \sigma} = Se^{-q\tau}n(d_1)\frac{\partial d_1}{\partial \sigma} - Ke^{-r\tau}n(d_2)\frac{\partial d_2}{\partial \sigma} = Se^{-q\tau}n(d_1)\,\frac{\partial (d_1 - d_2)}{\partial \sigma}.$$
The second step uses the identity. Since $d_1 - d_2 = \sigma\sqrt{\tau}$, the last derivative is $\sqrt{\tau}$, which gives $\mathcal{V} = S\sqrt{\tau}e^{-q\tau}n(d_1)$. Parity, $c - p = Se^{-q\tau} - Ke^{-r\tau}$, has no $\sigma$ on the right side. So the put has the same vega.

For rho, differentiate $c$ in $r$. Now $d_1$ and $d_2$ both depend on $r$, with the same derivative $\sqrt{\tau}/\sigma$, and the factor $e^{-r\tau}$ depends on $r$ too:
$$\frac{\partial c}{\partial r} = \big[Se^{-q\tau}n(d_1) - Ke^{-r\tau}n(d_2)\big]\frac{\sqrt{\tau}}{\sigma} + K\tau e^{-r\tau}N(d_2).$$
The bracket is zero by the identity, which leaves $\rho_{\text{call}} = K\tau e^{-r\tau}N(d_2)$. Differentiating parity in $r$ gives $\rho_{\text{call}} - \rho_{\text{put}} = K\tau e^{-r\tau}$. With $1 - N(d_2) = N(-d_2)$, this yields $\rho_{\text{put}} = -K\tau e^{-r\tau}N(-d_2)$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Repricing After a Volatility and Rate Move
>A stock trades at $\$72$ and pays a continuous dividend yield of $1\%$. A six-month European call has strike $\$70$. The volatility is $32\%$ per year, and the risk-free rate is $3.5\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Find the call's vega and rho. Then estimate the change in its price if $\sigma$ rises to $34\%$ and $r$ rises to $3.75\%$ at the same time.
>>Here $\tau = 0.5$. The pieces of $d_1$ are $\ln(72/70) = 0.02817$, $(r - q + \sigma^2/2)\tau = (0.035 - 0.01 + 0.0512)(0.5) = 0.03810$, and $\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = 0.32 \times 0.70711 = 0.22627$. So $d_1 = 0.06627/0.22627 = 0.2929$ and $d_2 = 0.0666$. The table values are $n(d_1) = 0.3822$ and $N(d_2) = 0.5266$. The discount factors are $e^{-q\tau} = 0.99501$ and $e^{-r\tau} = 0.98265$.
>>
>>Vega and rho are
>>$$\mathcal{V} = 72 \times 0.70711 \times 0.99501 \times 0.3822 = 19.36, \qquad \rho = 70 \times 0.5 \times 0.98265 \times 0.5266 = 18.11.$$
>>The changes are $d\sigma = 0.02$ and $dr = 0.0025$. The estimated price change is
>>$$19.36 \times 0.02 + 18.11 \times 0.0025 = 0.387 + 0.045 = \$0.43.$$
>>Exact repricing gives $\$0.432$. The volatility move accounts for nearly all of it. The put with the same strike has the same vega and a rho of $-16.28$, so its price rises by about $0.387 - 0.041 = \$0.35$.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The (Medium) example uses the relation $\mathcal{V} = \sigma S^2\tau\,\Gamma$, which is not in the modules.

>[!example] (Medium) Short or Long Maturity
>A stock trades at $\$90$ and pays no dividends. Its volatility is $24\%$ per year, and the risk-free rate is $4\%$ per year with continuous compounding. A trader compares two at-the-money European calls with strike $\$90$. One expires in one month, and the other in two years.
>
>>[!example]- Find the vega and gamma of each call. Then find how many one-month calls match the vega of one two-year call, and compare their total gamma with the two-year call's.
>>For the one-month call, $\tau = 1/12$, $\sqrt{\tau} = 0.28868$, and $\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = 0.06928$. So $d_1 = (0.04 + 0.0288)(1/12)/0.06928 = 0.0828$ and $n(d_1) = 0.3976$. Then
>>$$\mathcal{V} = 90 \times 0.28868 \times 0.3976 = 10.33, \qquad \Gamma = \frac{0.3976}{90 \times 0.06928} = 0.0638.$$
>>For the two-year call, $\sqrt{\tau} = 1.41421$ and $\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = 0.33941$. So $d_1 = 0.0688 \times 2/0.33941 = 0.4054$ and $n(d_1) = 0.3675$. Then $\mathcal{V} = 90 \times 1.41421 \times 0.3675 = 46.77$ and $\Gamma = 0.3675/(90 \times 0.33941) = 0.01203$.
>>
>>Matching vega takes $46.77/10.33 = 4.53$ one-month calls. Their gamma is $4.53 \times 0.0638 = 0.2887$, which is $24$ times the two-year call's gamma. The factor $24$ is the ratio of maturities, $2/(1/12)$, because $\mathcal{V} = \sigma S^2\tau\,\Gamma$ for both calls. The bundle also loses time value much faster, with a theta of $-75.56$ per year against $-4.56$.
>>
>>The rule $0.4\,S\sqrt{\tau}$ gives $10.39$ for the one-month call, close to $10.33$. For the two-year call it gives $50.91$ against $46.77$, because $d_1 = 0.41$ is not near $0$. A trader who wants exposure to changes in volatility buys the long-dated call. A trader who wants to profit from the stock's daily moves buys the short-dated calls.

>[!example] (Hard) A Synthetic Forward Has Rho but No Vega
>A desk is long $500$ European calls and short $500$ European puts, all with strike $\$105$ and $18$ months to expiry. The stock trades at $\$100$ and pays a continuous dividend yield of $2\%$. The volatility is $27\%$ per year, and the risk-free rate is $5\%$ per year with continuous compounding.
>
>>[!example]- Show that the position has zero vega. Find its rho, and show that the rho does not depend on $\sigma$. Then compare the rho estimate with exact repricing after $r$ rises by half a percentage point.
>>Each call and each put has the same vega, $46.86$, so the position's vega is $500 \times (46.86 - 46.86) = 0$. Its gamma cancels in the same way. By parity the position is worth $500\,(Se^{-q\tau} - Ke^{-r\tau})$, a long forward with delivery price $\$105$, and $\sigma$ does not appear in it.
>>
>>Here $\tau = 1.5$ and $\sigma\sqrt{\tau} = 0.33068$. The pieces of $d_1$ are $\ln(100/105) = -0.04879$ and $(0.05 - 0.02 + 0.03645)(1.5) = 0.09968$. So $d_1 = 0.1539$ and $d_2 = -0.1768$, with $N(d_2) = 0.4298$ and $N(-d_2) = 0.5702$. The bond factor is $K\tau e^{-r\tau} = 157.5 \times e^{-0.075} = 146.12$. So
>>$$\rho_{\text{call}} = 146.12 \times 0.4298 = 62.81, \qquad \rho_{\text{put}} = -146.12 \times 0.5702 = -83.31.$$
>>The position's rho is $500 \times (62.81 + 83.31) = 500 \times 146.12 = 73{,}060$ per unit of $r$. Since $N(d_2) + N(-d_2) = 1$, the term $N(d_2)$ drops out, and with it all dependence on $\sigma$.
>>
>>For $dr = 0.005$, the estimate is $73{,}060 \times 0.005 = \$365.30$. Exact repricing moves the position from $-\$184.26$ to $+\$179.68$, a change of $\$363.93$. The gap of $\$1.37$ is the second-order term $\frac{1}{2} \times 500 \times K\tau^2e^{-r\tau}(dr)^2$. Each leg alone has a rho that depends on $\sigma$. The calls gain $\$158.05$ against an estimate of $\$157.02$, and the puts lose $\$205.88$ against an estimate of $\$208.28$.
