>[!note] Cash-and-Carry Arbitrage
>**Cash-and-carry arbitrage** buys an asset with borrowed money and sells it today through a futures or forward contract. The trader holds the asset until the delivery date $T$ and then delivers it. The trade earns a riskless profit when the futures price $F$ exceeds the cost of buying and holding the asset. **Reverse cash and carry** runs the trade backwards when $F$ is below that cost. The formulas treat futures as forwards and ignore daily settlement. Let $S$ be the spot price. The **carry** $C$ is the net cost of holding the asset until $T$, as a fraction of $S$. No arbitrage then requires
>$$F = S(1 + C).$$
>The carry, also called the **cost of carry**, can be positive or negative. It has the following parts.
>- **Finance** adds the interest on the money tied up in the asset, $e^{rT} - 1$ at a continuously compounded rate $r$.
>- **Payouts** from the asset, such as dividends and coupons, lower the carry, since the holder receives them.
>- **Storage**, **insurance**, and **transportation** raise the carry, and they are negligible for financial assets.
>
>In each trade, the trader builds a forward from the asset and a loan, then trades it against the futures.
>- **Cash and carry** applies when $F > S(1 + C)$. The trader sells the futures, borrows to buy the asset, and delivers it at $T$, earning $F - S(1 + C)$.
>- **Reverse cash and carry** applies when $F < S(1 + C)$. The trader buys the futures, sells the asset short, and invests the proceeds, earning $S(1 + C) - F$ at $T$.
>
>The reverse trade needs an asset that can be borrowed. For most commodities, only an owner of the asset can run it. For an asset with no payouts, the market futures price $F_{\text{market}}$ implies a carry and a financing rate,
>$$C_{\text{implied}} = \frac{F_{\text{market}}}{S} - 1, \qquad r_{\text{implied repo}} = \frac{1}{T}\ln\left(1 + C_{\text{implied}}\right).$$
>The **implied repo rate** $r_{\text{implied repo}}$ is continuously compounded. Traders compare it with the repo rate, the rate on a loan secured by the asset. If the implied rate is above the repo rate, cash and carry pays. If it is below, the reverse pays.
>
>With trading costs, the single price becomes a band. Take a stock with no dividends and bid and ask prices $S_{\text{bid}}$ and $S_{\text{ask}}$. Each stock trade pays a commission $h_C$, a fraction of its value. Let $r_b$ be the borrowing rate, $r_l$ the lending rate, and $b$ the annual fee to borrow the stock. A forward at a single price $F$ allows no arbitrage when
>$$S_{\text{bid}}(1 - h_C)e^{(r_l - b)T} \le F \le S_{\text{ask}}(1 + h_C)e^{r_bT}.$$
>Call the two edges $F_{\text{low}}$ and $F_{\text{high}}$. Cash and carry pays only if the forward bid exceeds $F_{\text{high}}$. The reverse pays only if the forward ask is below $F_{\text{low}}$. [[2.2.2b Forward Price]] gives the frictionless price for each kind of payout. [[4.2.2a Arbitrage]] treats the band as one case of no-arbitrage bounds.

# Intuition

A buyer who needs an asset at $T$ has two routes. One route buys a futures contract and pays $F$ at $T$. The other buys the asset today and holds it until $T$. Holding costs interest on the purchase price, plus storage and insurance for a physical good. Holding also earns any dividends or coupons along the way. Both routes deliver the same asset on the same date, so they must cost the same. Arbitrageurs enforce this. When $F$ is too high, they take the second route and sell the futures. When $F$ is too low, they run the second route backwards with a short sale. In real markets each trade pays its own costs, so the two trades enforce two different edges.

# Properties

#### *(Physical Commodities)* A storage cost paid at delivery adds to the futures price one for one.

Let $U$ be the storage and insurance cost per unit, paid at $T$. The carry is then $C = (e^{rT} - 1) + U/S$, so $F = Se^{rT} + U$. Cash and carry works as for a stock, with $U$ as an extra payment at $T$. The reverse trade needs a short sale, and commodities are hard to borrow. A firm that already stores the commodity can still run it. The firm sells one unit today and invests the cash. It buys a futures contract to get the unit back at $T$. It earns interest and saves the storage it would have paid. At $T$ it holds the same unit as before, plus $Se^{rT} + U - F$ in cash. Such firms keep the futures price from staying far below $S(1 + C)$.

#### *(Reading the Implied Repo Rate)* The implied repo rate measures the financing rate only for an asset with no payouts.

>[!warning] Not Taught in Lecture
>The modules define the implied repo rate only for an asset with no payouts. The dividend-yield version at the end of this property is an extension.

A cash-and-carry trader often funds the purchase with a repo, a loan secured by the asset itself. The lender holds the asset as collateral, so a repo costs less than an unsecured loan. With a repo rate $r_{\text{repo}}$, continuously compounded over the life of the trade, the profit per unit at $T$ is
$$F_{\text{market}} - Se^{r_{\text{repo}}T} = S\left(e^{r_{\text{implied repo}}T} - e^{r_{\text{repo}}T}\right).$$
The profit has the sign of the gap between the two rates. A holder of the asset can use the same number in reverse. The holder can sell the asset, lend the cash, and buy the futures to get the asset back. This beats holding when the holder's lending rate exceeds $r_{\text{implied repo}}$. For an asset with a dividend yield $q$, the futures price is $Se^{(r - q)T}$, so $\ln(F/S)/T$ measures $r - q$. The implied financing rate is then $\ln(F/S)/T + q$. The payout-free formula applied to a stock index understates the financing rate by $q$.

#### *(Width of the Band)* The band's log-width is the sum of the stock spread, the round-trip commission, and the rate gaps over the trade.

Dividing the upper edge by the lower edge gives
$$\ln\frac{F_{\text{high}}}{F_{\text{low}}} = \ln\frac{S_{\text{ask}}}{S_{\text{bid}}} + \ln\frac{1 + h_C}{1 - h_C} + (r_b - r_l + b)T.$$
The first term is close to the relative bid-ask spread of the stock, and the second is close to $2h_C$. Only the third term grows with $T$, so it dominates for long maturities. The edges also move unevenly. A larger borrow fee $b$ lowers only $F_{\text{low}}$, so the frictionless price can sit near the top of the band. If the stock cannot be borrowed at all, the lower edge disappears and only cash and carry can act. The forward's spread adds more, since one trade sells at its bid and the other buys at its ask. Taxes widen the band further.

#### *(Stock Index Arbitrage)* In index arbitrage, a trader trades index futures against the index stocks, and most risks come from the stock basket.

A typical trade buys S&P 500 futures and sells the index stocks at the same time, or the reverse. The carry of an index includes the dividends of all its stocks. The reference price is therefore $Se^{(r - q)T}$, where $q$ is the index dividend yield. Traders send the stock orders by program trading, computer orders that buy or sell the whole basket at once. The trade has several practical risks. Dividends must be estimated, and the estimates get worse for longer contracts. The index composition may change during the trade. Short-sale orders may take longer to fill than purchases. The futures and the stock legs may not be set at the same moment, a risk called legging. Many traders hold a subset of the stocks that tracks the index. The gap between the two is tracking risk.

#### *(Treasury Basis Trade)* The Treasury cash-futures basis trade is cash and carry in government bonds, and most of its risks come from funding.

The trader holds the bonds on repo and sells Treasury futures against them. The trade earns the gap between the implied repo rate and the repo rate the trader pays. The trade is popular among hedge funds. It has three practical risks. Futures are marked to market daily, so gains and losses settle in cash each day. If bond prices rise, the short futures position pays cash each day. The gain on the bonds may not arrive as cash at the same time. The trader must fund this margin from its own cash. Repo rates also fluctuate, and a repo shorter than the futures must be renewed at unknown rates. Markets can also become illiquid, and then the trader cannot close the position at fair prices. The repo funds most of the purchase, so each loss is large compared with the trader's own capital.

# Derivation

We want the range of forward prices that rules out arbitrage for a stock with no dividends. Each edge comes from one trade, priced with the worst terms the trader faces. The trader buys at the ask and sells at the bid, for both the stock and the forward. The trader borrows at $r_b$ and lends at $r_l$.

For the upper edge, the trader runs cash and carry on one share.

| Position | $t = 0$ | $T$ |
|---|---|---|
| Borrow at $r_b$ | $+S_{\text{ask}}(1 + h_C)$ | $-S_{\text{ask}}(1 + h_C)e^{r_bT}$ |
| Buy one share at the ask, plus commission | $-S_{\text{ask}}(1 + h_C)$ | deliver the share |
| Sell the forward at $F_{\text{bid}}$ | $0$ | $+F_{\text{bid}}$ |
| Total | $0$ | $F_{\text{bid}} - S_{\text{ask}}(1 + h_C)e^{r_bT}$ |

The trade profits only if $F_{\text{bid}} > S_{\text{ask}}(1 + h_C)e^{r_bT}$, which gives the upper edge.

For the lower edge, the trader runs reverse cash and carry. The short sale raises $S_{\text{bid}}(1 - h_C)$ after commission. The stock lender charges the fee $b$, which comes out of the deposit, so the deposit grows at $r_l - b$.

| Position | $t = 0$ | $T$ |
|---|---|---|
| Short one share at the bid, less commission | $+S_{\text{bid}}(1 - h_C)$ | return the share |
| Deposit at $r_l$, less the fee $b$ | $-S_{\text{bid}}(1 - h_C)$ | $+S_{\text{bid}}(1 - h_C)e^{(r_l - b)T}$ |
| Buy the forward at $F_{\text{ask}}$ | $0$ | $-F_{\text{ask}}$, receive the share |
| Total | $0$ | $S_{\text{bid}}(1 - h_C)e^{(r_l - b)T} - F_{\text{ask}}$ |

The trade profits only if $F_{\text{ask}} < S_{\text{bid}}(1 - h_C)e^{(r_l - b)T}$, which gives the lower edge. For a forward with a single price $F = F_{\text{bid}} = F_{\text{ask}}$, neither trade pays inside the band in the callout. Without frictions, $S_{\text{bid}} = S_{\text{ask}} = S$, $h_C = b = 0$, and $r_b = r_l = r$. Both edges then equal $Se^{rT} = S(1 + C)$ with $C = e^{rT} - 1$, the [[2.2.2b Forward Price|forward price]] of a stock with no dividends. Other carry costs add rows to the first table. A storage cost $U$ paid at $T$ adds $-U$ at $T$ and raises the upper edge by $U$.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Reading the Implied Repo Rate
>A stock that pays no dividends trades at $\$84.00$. Its nine-month futures price is $\$86.10$. A trader can borrow on repo at $2.9\%$ per year with continuous compounding. Treat the futures as a forward.
>
>>[!example]- Find the implied carry and the implied repo rate, choose a trade, and find its profit per share.
>>The implied carry is $C_{\text{implied}} = 86.10/84.00 - 1 = 0.0250$. The implied repo rate is
>>$$r_{\text{implied repo}} = \frac{1}{0.75}\ln(1.025) = \frac{0.024693}{0.75} = 3.2923\%.$$
>>The futures price implies financing at $3.29\%$, and the trader can borrow at $2.9\%$. So the futures price is too high, and cash and carry pays. The trader borrows $\$84.00$ on repo, buys the share, and sells the futures at $\$86.10$. At $T$ the trader delivers the share, receives $\$86.10$, and repays
>>$$84.00\,e^{0.029 \times 0.75} = 84.00 \times 1.021988 = \$85.85.$$
>>The profit is $86.10 - 85.85 = \$0.25$ per share, with no money down.

>[!example] (Medium) Crude Oil in Storage
>Crude oil trades at $\$68.00$ per barrel. Storage and insurance cost $\$0.60$ per barrel for six months, paid at the end. The risk-free rate is $4.5\%$ per year with continuous compounding. Consider two cases for the six-month futures price, $\$71.05$ and $\$69.40$. A trading firm holds crude oil in its own tanks.
>
>>[!example]- Find the carry and the no-arbitrage futures price. For each futures price, name the trade, say who can run it, and find the gain per barrel.
>>The finance part of the carry is $e^{0.045 \times 0.5} - 1 = 0.022755$. The storage part is $0.60/68.00 = 0.008824$. So $C = 0.031579$, and the no-arbitrage price is
>>$$F = S(1 + C) = 68.00 \times 1.031579 = \$70.15.$$
>>This equals the loan balance $68.00\,e^{0.0225} = \$69.55$ plus the storage cost of $\$0.60$.
>>
>>A futures price of $\$71.05$ is too high. Anyone can run cash and carry. Borrow $\$68.00$, buy a barrel, store it, and sell the futures. At six months, receive $\$71.05$, repay $\$69.55$, and pay $\$0.60$ for storage. The gain is $71.05 - 70.15 = \$0.90$ per barrel.
>>
>>A futures price of $\$69.40$ is too low. The reverse trade needs a short sale, and crude oil is hard to borrow. The trading firm can run it from its tanks. It sells a barrel for $\$68.00$, invests the cash, and buys the futures at $\$69.40$. At six months the cash has grown to $\$69.55$, and the firm has saved $\$0.60$ of storage. It pays $\$69.40$ and gets its barrel back. Compared with holding the barrel, the firm is $69.55 + 0.60 - 69.40 = \$0.75$ better off per barrel.

>[!example] (Hard) Two Dealers and a Band
>A stock that pays no dividends trades at bid $\$64.95$ and ask $\$65.05$. Each stock trade pays a commission of $0.10\%$ of its value. A trader borrows at $5.5\%$ and lends at $4.2\%$ per year. Borrowing the stock costs a fee of $0.8\%$ per year. All rates use continuous compounding. Dealer A quotes a nine-month forward at bid $\$66.20$ and ask $\$66.40$. Dealer B quotes it at bid $\$67.70$ and ask $\$67.85$. An analyst uses the mid price $\$65.00$ and the average rate $4.85\%$, and claims that both quotes are mispriced.
>
>>[!example]- Find the band, decide which dealer offers a true arbitrage, and give the trade for $10{,}000$ shares.
>>With $T = 0.75$, the upper edge is
>>$$F_{\text{high}} = 65.05 \times 1.001 \times e^{0.055 \times 0.75} = 65.11505 \times 1.042113 = \$67.86.$$
>>The lower edge is
>>$$F_{\text{low}} = 64.95 \times 0.999 \times e^{(0.042 - 0.008) \times 0.75} = 64.88505 \times 1.025828 = \$66.56.$$
>>The analyst's frictionless price is $65.00\,e^{0.0485 \times 0.75} = \$67.41$, which lies inside the band.
>>
>>Dealer A's ask of $\$66.40$ is below $F_{\text{low}}$, so reverse cash and carry pays. The table shows the trade for $10{,}000$ shares.
>>
>>| Position | $t = 0$ | $T$ |
>>|---|---|---|
>>| Short $10{,}000$ shares at $\$64.95$, less commission | $+648{,}850.50$ | return the shares |
>>| Deposit at $4.2\%$, less the $0.8\%$ fee | $-648{,}850.50$ | $+665{,}608.95$ |
>>| Buy the forward at $\$66.40$ | $0$ | $-664{,}000.00$ |
>>| Total | $0$ | $+1{,}608.95$ |
>>
>>Dealer B's bid of $\$67.70$ is $\$0.29$ above the frictionless price. The analyst would sell the forward and buy the stock. That trade buys at the ask, pays commission, and borrows at $5.5\%$. Its cost at $T$ is $F_{\text{high}} = \$67.86$ per share, which exceeds the bid. The trade loses $\$0.16$ per share, and Dealer B's quote lies inside the band.
>>
>>The band is $\$1.30$ wide. Its log-width of $0.0193$ splits into $0.0015$ from the spread, $0.0020$ from commissions, and $0.0158$ from rates and fee. The rate gaps cause most of the width at this maturity.
