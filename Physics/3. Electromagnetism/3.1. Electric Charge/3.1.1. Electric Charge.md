>[!note] Electric Charge
>**Electric charge** is a property of matter that makes particles push or pull on each other. It plays the role that mass plays in gravity. Charge comes in two kinds, called positive and negative. Like charges repel each other, and opposite charges attract. The SI unit of charge is the **coulomb**, written $\text{C}$.
>
>Ordinary matter is built from three particles, and two of them carry charge:
>- The **proton** has charge $+e$ and mass $m_p = 1.673 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$. Protons sit in the atomic nucleus.
>- The **neutron** has charge $0$ and mass $m_n = 1.675 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$. Neutrons also sit in the nucleus.
>- The **electron** has charge $-e$ and mass $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$, about $1836$ times smaller than $m_p$. Electrons form a cloud around the nucleus.
>
>The size of the proton and electron charge is the **elementary charge**,
>$$e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}.$$
>Every charge in ordinary matter is a whole-number multiple of $e$. For this reason charge is called **quantized**. An object with $N_p$ protons and $N_e$ electrons carries the **net charge**
>$$Q = N_p e - N_e e = (N_p - N_e)\, e.$$
>A neutral object has $Q = 0$ because $N_p = N_e$. It still contains huge numbers of both kinds of charge.
>
>Objects become charged when electrons move from one object to another:
>- A **positive** object has fewer electrons than protons.
>- A **negative** object has more electrons than protons.
>
>Rubbing two different materials together moves electrons onto the material that holds them more tightly. That material turns negative, and the other turns positive.
>
>One coulomb is a very large charge, about $6.24 \times 10^{18}$ elementary charges. Problems use millicoulombs ($1\ \text{mC} = 10^{-3}\ \text{C}$), microcoulombs ($1\ \mu\text{C} = 10^{-6}\ \text{C}$), and nanocoulombs ($1\ \text{nC} = 10^{-9}\ \text{C}$). A plastic rod rubbed with fur carries tens of nanocoulombs. The step $e$ is so small that problems treat charge as a continuous quantity.

# Intuition

A classroom demonstration rubs two plastic rods with fur. One rod rests on a pivot. When the other rod comes near, the pivoted rod swings away. It started at rest, so by [[1.2.1. Newton's Laws|Newton's second law]] some force acted on it. Nothing touched it, and nothing visible passed between the rods. Rubbing put something on both rods, and that something pushes. Physicists call it charge and find the force from where the charges sit.

Charge is carried by particles that every object already contains. Each atom holds protons and neutrons in a tiny nucleus and electrons in a cloud around it. The protons are bound inside the nucleus. The outer electrons are light and loosely held, so rubbing can scrape some of them from one surface onto another. Charging an object never creates charge. It moves electrons that were already there.

The animation follows three electrons as rubbing moves them from the fur to the rod. The fur ends with $+3e$, the rod with $-3e$, and the total stays zero.

# Properties

#### *(Only Electrons Move)* In ordinary charging an object gains or loses electrons, while its protons stay where they are.

Adding protons would need a proton beam from a particle accelerator. Some hospitals have one, but no lab bench does. Removing protons would mean reaching into nuclei and turning one element into another. Electrons sit in the outer cloud, and rubbing removes them. A positive object therefore has an electron deficit, and a negative object has an electron excess. Electrons are also about $1836$ times lighter than protons. The same force gives an electron about $1836$ times the acceleration of a proton, by $a = F/m$. For both reasons, almost every problem in this course follows the electrons.

#### *(Neutral Is Not Empty)* Zero net charge means equal amounts of positive and negative charge, and the amounts are enormous.

Copper has $29$ electrons per atom and a molar mass of $63.55\ \text{g/mol}$. One gram of copper therefore holds about $2.75 \times 10^{23}$ electrons, with a total charge of about $-4.4 \times 10^{4}\ \text{C}$. Its protons carry exactly the opposite amount, so the net charge is zero. Outside the object, the effects of the two kinds cancel. A small shift in where the electrons sit can still produce large forces. The note [[3.1.6. Induced Charge]] follows that shift.

#### *(A Coulomb Is Huge)* One coulomb of positive charge on a mole of atoms means about one atom in $10^{5}$ has lost an electron.

One coulomb holds $N = Q/e = 6.24 \times 10^{18}$ elementary charges. Dividing by [[2.2.4 Avogadro's Number|Avogadro's number]], $N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}$, gives a fraction of $1.04 \times 10^{-5}$. That is about one atom in $96{,}000$. That tiny imbalance would make sparks jump from the object. Real charged objects have imbalances many orders of magnitude smaller still.

#### *(Quantized but Continuous)* Charge comes in steps of $e$, but the steps are far too small to notice on everyday objects.

A charge of $1\ \mu\text{C}$ contains about $6.24 \times 10^{12}$ elementary charges. Adding one more electron changes it by about one part in $6 \times 10^{12}$. No ordinary instrument can detect that. Problems therefore treat charge as a smooth quantity that can be split into small pieces $dq$. The note [[3.2.4. Electric Field of a Continuous Charge Distribution]] builds on this idea.

#### *(The Pair Decides the Sign)* After rubbing, the material that holds electrons more tightly ends up negative.

The result depends on both materials. A material can turn negative when rubbed with one partner and positive with another. Once electrons have moved, the two surfaces carry opposite charges and attract each other. Pulling them apart takes work, and the person doing the rubbing supplies it. The note [[3.1.3. Conductors and Insulators]] explains why rubbing works best on insulators.

# Examples

>[!example] (Easy) Is the Measured Charge Possible?
>A student measures the charge on two dust grains. The results are $4.81 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$ and $2.40 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- Which result is possible?
>>Divide each charge by $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$. The first gives
>>$$\frac{4.81 \times 10^{-19}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}} = 3.00,$$
>>so it is a grain with three missing electrons. The second gives $1.50$. No object can carry half an elementary charge, so the second measurement is wrong.

>[!example] (Medium) Charging a Balloon
>A rubber balloon rubbed on dry hair ends with a charge of $-0.30\ \mu\text{C}$.
>
>>[!example]- How many electrons moved, and how much mass did the balloon gain?
>>The balloon is negative, so it gained electrons. The number is
>>$$N = \frac{|Q|}{e} = \frac{0.30 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}} = 1.87 \times 10^{12}.$$
>>The added mass is
>>$$\Delta m = N m_e = (1.87 \times 10^{12})(9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}) = 1.7 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{kg}.$$
>>No balance can detect this mass. The hair lost the same electrons and carries $+0.30\ \mu\text{C}$.

>[!example] (Hard) How Many Atoms Are Ionized?
>A solid aluminum sphere of mass $10.0\ \text{g}$ carries a charge of $+2.0\ \text{nC}$. Aluminum has a molar mass of $26.98\ \text{g/mol}$.
>
>>[!example]- What fraction of the aluminum atoms has lost an electron?
>>The sphere contains
>>$$n_{\text{atoms}} = \frac{10.0\ \text{g}}{26.98\ \text{g/mol}} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1} = 2.23 \times 10^{23}$$
>>atoms. The positive charge comes from missing electrons, and their number is
>>$$N = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{C}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}} = 1.25 \times 10^{10}.$$
>>The fraction is $N / n_{\text{atoms}} = 5.6 \times 10^{-14}$. About one atom in $18$ trillion has lost an electron. Charging an object changes almost none of its atoms.
